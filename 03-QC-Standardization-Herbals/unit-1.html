1 Quality, QC & Standardization: the Big Picture

Why does a herb need so many tests when a paracetamol tablet needs only a few?

🎬 Story mode

The golden flower grew from a single drop of sunlight. Every healer in Corona wanted it, so fakes appeared: daisies dipped in yellow dye, flowers picked in the rain that went mouldy, petals from the roadside covered in soot. The queen's healers learnt to ask three questions of every basket: Is it really the sun-flower? (identity) Is it clean? (purity) Has it still got its glow? (strength). A synthetic tablet is one pure molecule made the same way every time; a herb is a living mixture of hundreds of molecules that change with soil, season, sunshine and storage. That is why herbs need a whole kingdom of tests.

📘 Exam definitions (write exactly this)

Crude drug: a natural product of plant, animal or mineral origin that has not been processed beyond collection, cleaning and drying, and is used as a medicine or as the raw material for one.

Herbal medicines (WHO): herbs, herbal materials, herbal preparations and finished herbal products that contain, as active ingredients, parts of plants, other plant materials or combinations of them.

Quality control (QC): the set of tests and specifications used to check that every batch of a drug meets its standards of identity, purity and strength (content of active principles).

Herbal drug standardization: the process of prescribing a set of standards (inherent characteristics, constant parameters and definite qualitative and quantitative values) for a herbal drug, and then confirming them, so that every batch carries an assurance of quality, safety, efficacy and reproducibility.

Evaluation of a crude drug: confirming its identity (authentication), determining its quality and purity, and detecting adulteration.

Why standardization is essential (the "need")

🌦️

Natural variation

Content of actives changes with species/chemotype, soil, climate, altitude, season and time of collection.
🧺

Post-harvest changes

Poor drying, storage and transport cause moisture gain, mould, enzymatic breakdown and loss of volatile oils.
🎭

Adulteration & substitution

Look-alike, exhausted or cheaper materials; misidentification by vernacular names.
☠️

Contamination

Pesticides, heavy metals, microbes, aflatoxins, radioactivity and residual solvents threaten safety.
🔁

Reproducibility

The same dose must give the same effect batch after batch; this is the basis of clinical trust.
🌍

Regulation & trade

Licensing, pharmacopoeial compliance and export registration all demand standard specifications.

Intrinsic (genetic) factors

  • Species, variety, chemotype
  • Plant part used
  • Age / stage of growth
VS

Extrinsic (environmental) factors

  • Soil, climate, light, water
  • Cultivation & collection methods
  • Harvest time, drying, processing, transport, storage

⚠️ Common mistake

Quality control ≠ standardization. QC is checking a batch against standards; standardization is setting those standards (and adjusting/confirming the product to meet them). Evaluation is the broader word for identifying the drug and judging its quality.

2 Basic Tests for Drugs (WHO): Substances, Plant Materials & Dosage Forms

Syllabus line 1. The "frying-pan-and-candle" tests that work even without a big laboratory.

🎬 Story mode

Rapunzel had no laboratory in her tower: just a lens, a candle, a frying pan and a chameleon. Yet she could still tell real flowers from painted ones by colour, smell, a drop of water and a careful look. WHO wrote its Basic Tests manuals for exactly such places: small district stores and inspectors in low-resource countries who need a quick "is this what the label says?" check.

📘 Definition

Basic tests are simple, rapid and inexpensive tests, using readily available reagents and simple equipment, that confirm the identity of a pharmaceutical substance, a medicinal plant material or a dosage form and give a first indication of gross degradation. They help detect mislabelled, substandard or counterfeit drugs where full pharmacopoeial analysis is not possible. They do not replace pharmacopoeial monographs; a failed basic test means "send the sample for full analysis".

The WHO "Basic tests" series

📗1986Basic tests for pharmaceutical substances
📘1991Basic tests for pharmaceutical dosage forms
📙1998Basic tests for drugs: pharmaceutical substances, medicinal plant materials and dosage forms (23 substances, 58 dosage forms, 4 plant materials added)

Features of basic tests

  • Simple, quick, cheap; no sophisticated instruments; can be done outside a fully equipped laboratory.
  • Can be performed by staff with basic training in chemistry/pharmacy.
  • Use common reagents (acids, alkalis, ferric chloride, iodine, silver nitrate…) and simple apparatus (test tubes, water-bath, hand lens, simple TLC kit).
  • Check identity (and sometimes gross degradation) only: they are screening tests, not quality assurance tests.
⚗️

A. Pharmaceutical substances

  • Description: colour, crystal form, odour
  • Solubility in water, ethanol, acids, alkalis
  • Melting point (simple apparatus)
  • Colour/precipitate reactions of ions or groups, e.g. chloride + silver nitrate → white curdy ppt (soluble in ammonia); sulfate + barium chloride → white ppt insoluble in HCl; salicylate + ferric chloride → violet
  • Simple TLC against a reference substance
🌿

B. Medicinal plant materials

  • Macroscopic: shape, size, colour, surface, fracture, odour, (taste)
  • Microscopic: trichomes, stomata, crystals, starch, fibres, vessels
  • Histochemical tests: phloroglucinol–HCl (lignin, pink), iodine (starch, blue), ferric chloride (tannin, blue-black), Sudan red (oils), KOH (anthraquinones, red)
  • Simple chemical tests for constituent groups (Section 3) and a TLC fingerprint
💊

C. Dosage forms

  • Check label, batch no., expiry, packaging integrity
  • Visual description: colour, shape, markings, coating; signs of deterioration (mottling, cracks, swelling, stickiness, cloudiness, particles)
  • Extract the active from tablet/capsule/injection, then apply the substance identity test
  • Simple TLC of the extract vs. reference; simple physical checks

A district drug store in a flood-hit area receives "senna leaf" from a new supplier. 3 Basic Chemical (Phytochemical) Tests for Plant Materials

Pascal changes colour to tell you what he feels. Plant extracts change colour to tell you what they contain.

🎬 Story mode

Pascal the chameleon turns pink when he's happy and green when he hides. Phytochemical tests are Pascal's party trick done in test tubes: add a reagent and the extract "blushes" orange (alkaloids with Dragendorff), turns blue-black (tannins with ferric chloride) or shows a red ring (steroids with Salkowski). Each colour = one class of constituent.

📘 Definition

Preliminary phytochemical screening is the set of simple qualitative chemical tests performed on plant extracts to detect the classes of constituents present (alkaloids, glycosides, steroids, flavonoids, tannins, saponins, carbohydrates, proteins, oils), based on characteristic colour changes or precipitates.

4.1 Powder fineness & sieve size

Fineness is described by the nominal aperture (µm) of the sieve the powder passes through; sieve numbers = aperture in µm (e.g. sieve 180 = 180 µm). WHO sieves conform to ISO 565.

4.2 Sampling (WHO general advice)

Plant material is not homogeneous, so the sample must represent the whole batch.

  • Number of containers: up to 5 units → sample every one; 6–50 → sample 5; over 50 → sample 10%, rounded up to the nearest multiple of ten (e.g. 51 units treated as 60 → 6 packages).
  • Inspect packaging first; sample damaged containers separately. On opening, note colour, texture, odour, presentation, foreign matter, mould, insects.
  • From each container take three original samples (top, middle, bottom; in sacks the first from not less than 10 cm deep). Seeds: grain probe.
  • Combine into a pooled sample, mix, and reduce by quartering: make a square heap, divide diagonally into four, keep two opposite quarters, repeat → average sample (about 100–200 g for flowers, up to 10 kg for some roots).
  • Quarter again into four final samples for: degree of fragmentation (sieve test); identity and impurities; moisture and ash; active ingredients. Keep a portion as reference/retest sample.
  • Retail packs: two consumer packs from each wholesale container; ten packs from small batches (1–5 boxes).

4.3 Determination of foreign matter (foreign organic matter)

📘 Definition (WHO)

Foreign matter is any material other than the drug as specified: (1) other parts of the same plant, or other plants, beyond the permitted limit; (2) any organism, part or product of an organism not named in the specification (insects, moulds, animal excreta); (3) mineral admixtures not adhering to the drug (soil, stones, sand, dust). Drugs must be free of visible mould, insects and animal contamination, abnormal odour, discoloration, slime or deterioration; poisonous or harmful foreign matter is never allowed.

4.4 Macroscopic & microscopic examination

👁️ Macroscopic (sensory) examination

  • Size: graduated ruler (mm); small seeds: line up 10 on calibrated paper and divide by 10.
  • Colour: untreated sample in diffuse daylight, compared with a reference sample.
  • Surface, texture, fracture: lens 6–10×; touch (soft/hard), bend and break: fibrous, smooth, rough, granular fracture.
  • Odour: strength (none, weak, distinct, strong) and sensation (aromatic, fruity, musty, mouldy, rancid…); compare, e.g. peppermint ≈ menthol, clove ≈ eugenol.
  • Taste: only when specifically required (safety!).

🔬 Microscopic examination (slide steps)

  1. Preliminary treatment: soften dried parts in a moist chamber or by soaking (hard barks in water–ethanol–glycerol).
  2. Preparation of specimens: powder mounts (water, glycerol–ethanol, chloral hydrate); cleared leaf surfaces; TS, RLS, TLS sections by razor/microtome (elder pith support).
  3. Clarification of particles: chloral hydrate (dissolves starch, aleurone, plastids, volatile oil); lactochloral; lactophenol; sodium hypochlorite (bleach); xylene (remove fats).
  4. Histochemical detection of cell walls & contents (table below).
  5. Disintegration of tissues: nitric acid + potassium chlorate (Schultze's), nitro-chromic acid, or caustic alkali (KOH/NaOH) maceration.
  6. Measurement: ocular micrometer calibrated with a stage micrometer.
  7. Leaf surface data: stomata types, stomatal index, vein-islet, palisade ratio (Section 8).

5 WHO QC · Ash, Extractive Values, Moisture & Volatile Oil

Burn it, soak it, dry it, distil it: four "numbers" that every monograph quotes.

🎬 Story mode

Flynn Rider tried to sell the palace a sack of "golden petals" that felt suspiciously heavy. Rapunzel burnt a pinch in a crucible: real petals leave a little white ash, but Flynn's sack left a pile of gritty sand (acid-insoluble ash). Then she soaked another pinch in alcohol: real petals give plenty of golden extract, while petals that had already been boiled for tea gave almost none (low extractive value = exhausted drug).

5.1 Ash values

📘 Definition

Ash value is the inorganic residue left after complete incineration of a drug, expressed in mg per g (or % w/w) of air-dried drug. It includes physiological ash (from the plant tissue itself, e.g. calcium oxalate, salts) and non-physiological ash (from adhering soil, sand, dust, or added inorganic adulterants).

5.2 Extractive values (determination of extractable matter)

📘 Definition

Extractive value is the amount of constituents extracted from a given amount of air-dried drug by a specified solvent under specified conditions, expressed as % w/w (or mg/g). WHO uses it for drugs for which no suitable chemical or biological assay exists yet.

🔥 Method 1 · Hot extraction (WHO, slide)

  1. 4.0 g coarse powder in a glass-stoppered conical flask.
  2. Add 100 ml water (or specified solvent); weigh flask + contents.

6.1 Bitterness value

📘 Definition

The bitterness value is the bitterness of a plant material compared with that of quinine hydrochloride, expressed in units per g, where one unit equals the bitterness of a solution containing 1 g quinine hydrochloride in 2000 ml.   • Used for "bitters" (gentian, chirata/Swertia, kalmegh/Andrographis, quassia, neem) which stimulate gastric secretion as appetizers.

6.2 Haemolytic activity

Saponin-rich plants (families Caryophyllaceae, Araliaceae, Sapindaceae, Primulaceae, Dioscoreaceae) damage erythrocyte membranes so haemoglobin leaks out (haemolysis). Activity is compared with reference saponin R = 1000 units/g (against ox blood).

  • Blood suspension: fresh ox blood in sodium citrate (36.5 g/l); 1 ml citrated blood diluted to 50 ml with phosphate buffer pH 7.4 → 2% suspension.
  • Preliminary test: 4 tubes (0.10, 0.20, 0.50, 1.00 ml extract + buffer to 1 ml + 1 ml blood); mix gently without foam; stand 6 h; total haemolysis = clear red solution with no deposit → decide dilution for main test.
  • Main test: 13-tube series (0.40–1.00 ml); read after 24 h. Run saponin R in parallel to cancel variation in the blood's resistance.
  • Your slide in short: mix RBC suspension with equal volumes of serial dilutions of extract, allow to stand, find the lowest concentration giving complete haemolysis.

6.3 Determination of tannins (hide-powder method)

Tannins are polyphenols that bind proteins and turn hide into leather; on living tissue this is the astringent action. They oxidise and polymerise easily in solution, losing activity.

T₁ · total water-extractable

Evaporate 50.0 ml of the extract to dryness, dry at 105 °C for 4 h, weigh.

T₂ · not bound by hide powder

80.0 ml extract + 6.0 g hide powder R, shake 60 min, filter; evaporate 50.0 ml of clear filtrate, dry at 105 °C, weigh.

T₀ · solubility of hide powder

6.0 g hide powder R + 80.0 ml water, shake 60 min, filter; evaporate 50.0 ml, dry at 105 °C, weigh (blank).

Extract preparation: specified quantity of powdered drug + 150 ml water on a boiling water-bath for 30 min; cool; transfer to a 250-ml volumetric flask and make up with water; let solids settle; filter through 12-cm paper, discarding the first 50 ml.

% tannins = [T₁ − (T₂ − T₀)] × 500 ÷ ww = weight of drug (g). 500 = 5 (50 ml taken from 250 ml) × 100 (for %). T₂ − T₀ removes the hide powder's own soluble matter.

"Astringency content" (RGUHS brief) = the tannin content estimated this way, i.e. the protein-precipitating (astringent) fraction.

7.5 Radioactive contamination

  • Some exposure to ionizing radiation is unavoidable: natural radionuclides in the ground and atmosphere.
  • Dangerous contamination follows nuclear accidents; released radionuclides (fission products, actinides, activation products) vary with the facility (reactor, reprocessing plant, isotope unit).
  • Risk is significant only with consumption of more than about 20 kg of plant material per year, so WHO proposes no limits for medicinal plants (processing may also reduce levels).

Flynn's wanted poster got his nose wrong. Judging by looks alone can fool you; that's why there are five ways to evaluate a drug.

🎬 Story mode

The palace guards hunted Flynn using a poster with a ridiculous nose, and kept arresting the wrong people. 📘 Definitions

Evaluation of a crude drug = confirmation of its identity, determination of its quality and purity, and detection of the nature of adulteration.

Adulteration = debasement of a drug by substitution, addition of inferior or spurious material, or removal of valuable constituents, making it differ from the official standard. Authentication = establishing that a plant material is the correct species and part, by comparison with an authentic (herbarium voucher) specimen using morphology, microscopy, chemical/chromatographic profiles and, today, DNA barcoding.

8.1 Why commercial crude drugs need evaluation

  • Natural variation in active content (source, season, collection, drying).
  • Deterioration during storage (moisture, moulds, insects, enzymes, oxidation).
  • Adulteration and substitution, deliberate or accidental (same vernacular names, look-alikes).
  • Drugs reach the market cut, broken or powdered, losing their identifying features.
  • Need for uniform potency for safe dosing, and for licensing/export.

3. Methods in brief

Powder fineness: graded by sieve aperture in µm: coarse (2000/355), moderately coarse (710/250), moderately fine (355/180), fine (180), very fine (125).

Sampling: ≤5 containers sample all, 6–50 sample 5, >50 sample 10%; three samples per container (top, middle, bottom) pooled and reduced by quartering to an average sample, then four final samples.

Foreign matter: 100–500 g (500 g roots/bark, 250 g leaves/flowers/seeds, 50 g cut) spread thinly, sorted with a 6–10× lens, remainder sifted through sieve 250; weighed, expressed as % of air-dried drug.

Macroscopic & microscopic: size, colour, surface, texture, fracture, odour (taste if required); microscopy: preliminary softening, preparation of mounts and sections, clarification (chloral hydrate), histochemical tests, disintegration, measurement with stage/ocular micrometer, leaf-surface data (stomatal index).

TLC: qualitative fingerprint vs reference; valuable for small amounts of impurities; Rf = distance moved by solute ÷ distance moved by solvent front.

Ash: total ash (2–4 g ignited at 500–600 °C till white); acid-insoluble ash (total ash boiled with 25 ml dilute HCl, residue ignited: silica/sand); water-soluble ash (total ash boiled with 25 ml water; ignited insoluble residue subtracted). Expressed in mg/g.

Extractable matter: hot method (4 g + 100 ml water, stand 1 h, reflux 1 h, filter, evaporate 25 ml, dry 105 °C 6 h) and cold maceration (4 g + 100 ml solvent, 6 h shaking, 18 h standing, evaporate 25 ml, dry 105 °C).

Water & volatile matter: azeotropic toluene distillation (water only; % = 100(n₁ − n)/w) and loss on drying (100–105 °C or over P₂O₅; water + volatiles).

Volatile oils: steam distillation in a graduated apparatus with xylene; oil volume = total − xylene; ml/100 g.

Bitterness value: threshold bitter concentration compared with quinine hydrochloride; units/g = 2000 × c/(a × b).

Haemolytic activity: serial dilutions of extract with 2% ox-blood suspension vs saponin R (1000 units/g); activity = 1000 × a/b.

Tannins: hide-powder method: T₁ (total extractable), T₂ (not bound to hide powder), T₀ (hide powder blank); % = [T₁ − (T₂ − T₀)] × 500/w.

Swelling index: 1 g + 25 ml water in a 25-ml cylinder, shaken every 10 min for 1 h, stood 3 h; volume in ml per g.

Foaming index: decoction of 1 g in 100 ml; 1–10 ml portions made to 10 ml, shaken 15 s, stood 15 min; index = 1000/a (tube with 1 cm foam).

Pesticide residues: from spraying, soil treatment, fumigation; chromatography (GC); total organic chlorine/phosphorus when unknown; ARL = ADI × E × 60/(MDI × 100).

Arsenic & heavy metals: arsenic by stain comparison (mercuric bromide paper vs standard stain); Pb and Cd by AAS/voltammetry after wet digestion; proposed limits Pb 10 mg/kg, Cd 0.3 mg/kg.

Microorganisms: total aerobic count, yeasts & moulds, E. coli, Salmonella, enterobacteria; limits depend on use (e.g. internal use: aerobic bacteria ≤10⁵/g, moulds ≤10³/g, E. coli ≤10/g, Salmonella absent). Aflatoxins B1, B2, G1, G2 by methanol extraction, clean-up and TLC under UV 365 nm.

Radioactive contamination: unavoidable natural background; no generalized method; no limits proposed because risk needs >20 kg/year intake; analysis by competent labs (IAEA methods).

2. Heavy metals: why determine them

Sources: polluted soil, irrigation water and air (industry, mining, traffic), traces of pesticides (lead/calcium arsenate), processing equipment, and metallic ingredients of some traditional formulations. 3. Limit test for arsenic (stain method)

The sample is digested to destroy organic matter; arsenic is reduced by zinc and stannated hydrochloric acid to arsine, which passes up a tube and produces a yellow-brown stain on mercuric bromide paper. The stain is compared with a standard stain prepared from a known quantity of arsenic (10 µg As) treated identically; the sample stain must not be deeper.

4. Determination of cadmium and lead

  1. Clean all apparatus with nitric acid, rinse, dry at 120 °C.

  3. Dissolve the inorganic residue in 2.5 ml nitric acid; run a blank in parallel.
  4. Measure Pb and Cd by atomic absorption spectrophotometry or inverse (stripping) voltammetry; verify with reference materials (olive leaves, hay powder). Mercury is measured by cold-vapour AAS; ICP-MS measures all together.

1. Definition and need

Evaluation = confirming identity, determining quality and purity and detecting adulteration. Needed because of natural variation, deterioration in storage, adulteration/substitution and the powdered state of commercial drugs.

Unintentional (name confusion, careless collection) or intentional: inferior varieties (Japanese ginger), exhausted drugs (exhausted cloves), superficially similar materials (Ailanthus for belladonna), artificial substitutes (basswood nutmeg), heavy worthless matter (limestone in asafoetida), synthetic chemicals (citral in lemon oil), powder/seed adulteration (papaya seeds in pepper, Argemone in mustard).

Q5. What is meant by foreign organic matter? How will you determine foreign organic matter?
Short 5m · KUHS Model · ~12 min
Definition with 3 types (1½m) → sample size table (1m) → procedure flow (2m) → formula + significance (½m)

Definition: Foreign (organic) matter is material other than the drug as defined in its monograph: (1) parts of the same plant or other plants not permitted (e.g. stems in leaf drugs); (2) any organism, part or product of an organism not in the specification (insects, moulds, excreta); (3) mineral admixtures not adhering to the drug (soil, stones, sand, dust). Drugs must be free of mould, insects, animal contamination, abnormal odour, slime and signs of deterioration.

Sample size: roots/rhizomes/bark 500 g; leaves/flowers/seeds/fruits 250 g; cut material (pieces < 0.5 g) 50 g.

Procedure: weigh the sample → spread in a thin layer → separate foreign matter by visual inspection, with a 6× or 10× lens or a suitable sieve → sift the remainder through sieve No. 250 (dust = mineral admixture) → weigh each portion to 0.05 g → calculate per 100 g of air-dried drug. If foreign matter closely resembles the drug, apply a critical chemical, physical or microscopic test to a pooled sample.

% foreign matter = (weight of foreign matter ÷ weight of sample) × 100

Significance: detects careless collection and adulteration; each monograph sets a limit (often 1–2%).

Q6. Define extractive value. Give the procedure for determining alcohol-soluble extractive value.
Short 5m · KUHS Model (+ MGR 2021, RGUHS 2023 brief: significance) · ~12 min
Definition + types (1m) → procedure in steps (2½m) → calculation (½m) → significance (1m)

Definition: Extractive value is the quantity of constituents extracted from a known weight of air-dried drug by a specified solvent under specified conditions, expressed as % w/w. It is used for drugs whose constituents cannot easily be assayed. Types: water-soluble, alcohol-soluble, ether-soluble (volatile and non-volatile).

Procedure (alcohol-soluble extractive):

  1. Weigh accurately about 5 g of coarsely powdered air-dried drug.
  2. Macerate with 100 ml of alcohol of the specified strength in a closed (stoppered) flask for 24 h, shaking frequently during the first 6 h and allowing to stand for 18 h.
  3. Filter rapidly, taking precautions against loss of alcohol.
  4. Evaporate 25 ml of the filtrate to dryness in a tared, flat-bottomed shallow dish on a water-bath.
  5. Dry the residue at 105 °C to constant weight, cool in a desiccator and weigh.
Alcohol-soluble extractive (%) = (weight of residue × 4 × 100) ÷ weight of drug

WHO cold maceration variant: 4 g drug, 100 ml solvent, 6 h shaking + 18 h standing, 25 ml filtrate dried at 105 °C for 6 h, result in mg/g.

Significance: evaluates resinous and other drugs without a specific assay; detects exhausted or adulterated drug (low value); indicates the nature of constituents; aids standardization of raw materials and extracts.

Q11. Explain the method to determine the aflatoxin content in herbal drugs.
Short 5m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (RGUHS 2023; Brainkart: microbial & aflatoxin)
What aflatoxins are (1m) → extraction + clean-up (2m) → TLC detection + interpretation (1½m) → limits/modern methods (½m)

Aflatoxins B1, B2, G1, G2 are hepatotoxic and carcinogenic metabolites of Aspergillus flavus/parasiticus, formed in warm, humid, badly stored drugs.

Extraction (WHO): grind ≥100 g to moderately fine powder (355/180); shake 50 g with 170 ml methanol + 30 ml water for ≥30 min; filter. If pigments interfere, treat 100 ml filtrate with zinc acetate–aluminium chloride and diatomaceous earth and re-filter. Add sodium chloride solution and light petroleum (removes lipids), then extract aflatoxins twice with 25 ml dichloromethane; evaporate. Optional silica-gel column clean-up.

Detection: dissolve residue in 0.2 ml chloroform–acetonitrile (98 : 2); spot 2.5–10 µl aflatoxin standard mixture and 10 µl sample on silica gel G; develop in chloroform–acetone–2-propanol (85 : 10 : 5) in an unsaturated tank; view under UV 365 nm.

Interpretation: standard shows four separated blue fluorescent spots; no sample spot should match them. Over-spotting confirms identity; intensity comparison estimates the amount.

Limits/modern methods: Ph. Eur.: B1 ≤ 2 µg/kg, total ≤ 4 µg/kg; HPLC-fluorescence after immunoaffinity columns, ELISA.

Q12. Write a note on any two methods for determination of moisture content in herbal drugs.
Short 5m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (RGUHS 2023; Brainkart brief: moisture vs LOD)
Why moisture matters (1m) → LOD method (1½m) → azeotropic method + diagram + formula (2m) → difference (½m)

Importance: excess water promotes bacteria, moulds and insects and causes hydrolytic deterioration; it also inflates weight.

1. Loss on drying (gravimetric): 2–5 g of prepared drug (pieces ≤ 3 mm) in a tared weighing bottle dried at 100–105 °C (or in a desiccator over P₂O₅ for drugs that melt) until two weighings differ by ≤ 5 mg; loss expressed in mg/g. Measures water + volatile matter.

2. Azeotropic (toluene distillation): toluene saturated with water by a first distillation (reading n); sample giving 2–3 ml water added and distilled (2, then 4 drops/s); water separates below toluene in the graduated receiver (reading n₁). % water = 100(n₁ − n)/w. Measures water only; ideal for drugs with volatile oils. [Draw Fig 1.5.]

Difference: LOD = water + volatiles; moisture content (azeotropic, Karl Fischer) = water only.

Q16. Describe any one basic test for detection of steroids in medicinal plant materials.

Q17. Define ash value and give its significance.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (AKTU 2023, MGR 2023 "importance of ash values")

Ash value is the inorganic residue remaining after complete incineration of a drug, expressed as % w/w or mg/g of air-dried drug (total, acid-insoluble, water-soluble, sulphated). Q18. Give the formula for the Lycopodium spore method.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (AKTU 2023, MGR 2022)
% purity = (N × W × 94,000 × 100) ÷ (S × M × P)

N = number of characteristic structures in 25 fields; W = weight of lycopodium (mg); S = number of lycopodium spores in the same 25 fields; M = weight of sample (mg); P = number of characteristic structures per mg of pure drug (e.g. about 2,86,000 starch grains per mg of ginger); 94,000 = average spores per mg of lycopodium.

Q19. Extractive value and its significance / importance.

Extractive value = % w/w of constituents extracted from air-dried drug by a specified solvent (water, alcohol, ether) under specified conditions. Q20. Define bitterness value with example.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (AKTU 2023)

Bitterness value is the bitterness of a plant material compared with quinine hydrochloride, expressed in units/g, one unit being the bitterness of 1 g quinine hydrochloride in 2000 ml. Authentication is confirming that a plant material is the correct species, variety and plant part. It is done by botanical identification against an authentic herbarium voucher specimen (genus, species, author, family recorded), macroscopy, microscopy, chemical and chromatographic (TLC/HPTLC) fingerprints and, increasingly, DNA barcoding. Q22. What are pesticides? Give two examples. / Natural pesticides / Define pesticide residue.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2021, MGR 2023, RGUHS 2023)

Pesticides are substances used to prevent, destroy or control pests (insects, fungi, weeds, rodents) in crops and stored materials, e.g. DDT, malathion, carbaryl, 2,4-D. Q24. Define palisade ratio.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2022)

Palisade ratio is the average number of palisade cells lying beneath one upper epidermal cell of a leaf. Determined by counting palisade cells under four epidermal cells (camera lucida) and dividing by four. It is constant for a species and helps distinguish related leaves (e.g. senna species), even in powders.

Q25. Different types of stomata.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2022) · draw Fig 1.7

(1) Anomocytic (ranunculaceous): irregular cells, no distinct subsidiary cells (Digitalis); (2) Anisocytic (cruciferous): 3–4 subsidiary cells, one smaller (Belladonna, Datura); (3) Diacytic (caryophyllaceous): 2 subsidiary cells with their common wall at right angles to the pore (peppermint, vasaka); (4) Paracytic (rubiaceous): 2 subsidiary cells parallel to the pore (senna).

Q26. What is quantitative microscopy?
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2021)

Quantitative microscopy is the numerical evaluation of microscopic characters of a drug for identification and purity: stomatal number and stomatal index, palisade ratio, vein-islet and vein-termination numbers, measurement of cell sizes (ocular micrometer), and the lycopodium spore method for estimating the purity of powders.

Q27. Identification test for glycosides.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2022)

Borntrager's test (anthraquinone glycosides): extract boiled with dilute acid, filtered, shaken with benzene/chloroform; the organic layer shaken with dilute ammonia gives a pink-red ammoniacal layer. Keller–Killiani (cardiac glycosides): extract in glacial acetic acid + trace FeCl₃, underlaid with conc. H₂SO₄ → reddish-brown ring at the junction and bluish-green upper layer.

Q28. Define the terms herbal medicine and crude drug.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2021)

Herbal medicine (WHO): herbs, herbal materials, herbal preparations and finished herbal products that contain as active ingredients parts of plants, other plant materials or combinations. Crude drug: a natural product of plant, animal or mineral origin, unprocessed except for collection, cleaning and drying, used as a medicine or as raw material for medicines (e.g. senna leaf, ginger rhizome).

Q29. Give the importance of heavy-metal determination in herbal drugs.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (RGUHS 2023)

Plants absorb Pb, Cd, Hg and As from polluted soil, water and air (and from pesticides or metallic ingredients). These metals are cumulative toxins (neuro-, nephro- and hepatotoxic; As is carcinogenic), and herbal products are often taken long-term. Determination (AAS, ICP-MS) against limits (Pb 10, Cd 0.3, As 3, Hg 1 ppm) protects patients and is required for licensing and export.

Q30. Define astringency content.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (RGUHS 2023)

Astringency content is the amount of tannins (astringent, protein-precipitating polyphenols) in a drug, determined by the hide-powder method: % tannins = [T₁ − (T₂ − T₀)] × 500/w, where T₁ = total water-extractable matter, T₂ = matter not bound to hide powder, T₀ = hide-powder blank, w = weight of drug.
