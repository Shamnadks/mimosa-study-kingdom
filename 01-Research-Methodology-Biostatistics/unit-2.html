1 Different Types of Data

Before WALL·E compacts anything, he decides what kind of thing it is. Statisticians do the same with data.

🎬 Story mode — sorting the treasures

WALL·E's shelves hold a Rubik's cube, a lighter, a spork, a bra he wears as goggles… Some things he can only name (a spork is not "more" than a spoon — nominal). Some he can rank (his favourite, second favourite… — ordinal). Some he can count (17 hubcaps — discrete). Some he can measure (the plant is 6.4 cm tall — continuous). The kind of data decides which statistics and graphs you may use.

📘 Exam definition

Data are facts, observations or measurements collected for a purpose, which, after processing, give information. A single value is a datum; a characteristic that varies from one individual to another is a variable.

🧠 Mnemonic — every detective film is NOIR
NominalOrdinalIntervalRatio

“Film NOIR: Names → Order → Intervals → Ratios (true zero).” Each step up adds one property.

Other ways to classify data

By source

Primary (collected first-hand) vs secondary (already collected by someone else) — see topic 2.

By time

Cross-sectional (one point in time) vs time-series (same variable over time) vs panel/longitudinal.

By arrangement

Raw / ungrouped (individual values) vs grouped (frequency distribution with class intervals).

By number of variables

Univariate (one), bivariate (two, e.g. dose & response), multivariate.

By distribution

Parametric (normally distributed, interval/ratio) vs non-parametric (skewed, ordinal/nominal).

By variable role

Independent, dependent, confounding, control, intervening variables — full answer in Unit 4.
⚠️ Common mistake

Coding a nominal variable with numbers (Male = 1, Female = 2) does not make it quantitative — you can't calculate a "mean gender". Also, temperature in °C is interval, not ratio: 40 °C is not "twice as hot" as 20 °C.

2 Primary and Secondary Data

Asked five different ways in KUHS papers — the most "guaranteed" topic of this unit.

🎬 Story mode — WALL·E's own finds vs the Axiom archives

Everything WALL·E picks up with his own claws is primary data — collected first-hand, for his own purpose. On the Axiom, Captain McCrea asks the ship computer to "define Earth" and watches old recorded videos of BnL's CEO, made 700 years earlier for a completely different purpose. That's secondary data — collected by someone else, already available, cheap and quick, but you must check whether it is still reliable and suitable.

📘 Exam definitions

Primary data are original data collected for the first time by the investigator himself/herself, for the specific purpose of the study (first-hand data).

Secondary data are data that have already been collected and processed by someone else for some other purpose, and are used by the investigator (second-hand data).

The same data are primary for the agency that collects them and secondary for anyone who uses them later — e.g. NFHS data are primary for the survey team, secondary for your thesis.

🥇 Primary data

  • Original, first-hand
  • Collected by investigator
  • For the present purpose → highly suitable
  • Costly, time-consuming, needs trained staff
  • Accuracy under researcher's control
  • Raw form → needs processing
VS

🥈 Secondary data

  • Already collected (second-hand)
  • Collected by others
  • For another purpose → may not fit
  • Cheap, quick, readily available
  • Accuracy not in researcher's control
  • Usually already processed / tabulated

  • International: WHO World Health Statistics, UNICEF, World Bank, UN reports

  • Online databases (PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, CTRI)

  • Records of companies, NGOs and research workers
  • Theses and dissertations, diaries, letters
  • Internal reports, ADR forms kept at monitoring centres

Advantages & limitations

Primary data

Advantages: original and specific to the problem; more accurate and reliable; researcher controls quality and definitions; up-to-date; confidential/proprietary.

Limitations (Nov 2022): expensive; time-consuming; needs trained investigators and planning; large samples difficult; respondents may refuse or give biased answers; risk of interviewer bias; sometimes impossible (past events).

Secondary data

Advantages: cheap; quick; readily available; large coverage (national data); useful for historical/trend analysis and as a base for primary research.

Limitations: may not suit the present purpose; definitions/units may differ; may be outdated; accuracy unknown; may be incomplete or biased.

⚠️ Precautions before using secondary data (Kothari)

Check its Reliability (who collected it, by what method, when, with what accuracy and bias?), Suitability (same definitions, units and scope as your study?) and Adequacy (enough coverage of area and time?). Mnemonic: RSA — "Really Suitable & Adequate?"

3 Different Methods for Data Collection

The tools in the evidence clerk's toolbox.

🎬 Story mode — how the Axiom gathers information

EVE observes (she scans everything silently, changing nothing). Captain McCrea interviews the ship's computer, question after question. Imagine the computer pinging every passenger's hover-chair screen with a quick poll — a questionnaire. M-O follows one contaminated robot all over the ship — a case study. And WALL·E and EVE's little plant test is an experiment. Different questions need different tools.

📘 Exam definition

Data collection is the systematic process of gathering information on variables of interest, in an established way, to answer research questions, test hypotheses and evaluate outcomes. Methods are grouped into methods for primary data and for secondary data.

1. Observation method

Observation is the systematic viewing and recording of behaviour, events or phenomena as they occur, without asking the respondent. Kothari calls it the most commonly used method, especially in studies of behavioural sciences.

Structured vs unstructured

Pre-defined units, standardised recording vs free, flexible recording.

Participant vs non-participant

Observer becomes part of the group vs observes from outside (disguised = observer's presence unknown).

Controlled vs uncontrolled

Under a pre-arranged plan / laboratory vs in natural settings.

✅ Advantages (Nov 2022 2-marker)

  • Subjective bias is eliminated if done accurately.
  • Information relates to what is happening now — not complicated by past behaviour or future intentions.
  • Independent of respondents' willingness to respond; little active cooperation needed.
  • Suitable for subjects who cannot give verbal reports (infants, animals, patients with impaired speech).
  • Records actual behaviour, not what people say they do (e.g. hand-washing by staff).

❌ Limitations

  • Expensive and time-consuming.
  • Information is limited; can't study past events, opinions or attitudes.
  • Unforeseen factors may interfere.
  • Observer bias; people may change behaviour when watched (Hawthorne effect).
💊 Pharmacy example

Observing how patients use their metered-dose inhalers in an OPD with a checklist of 8 steps; observing dispensing practices in community pharmacies; observing animal behaviour in a CNS screening study.

2. Interview method

Collection of data through oral-verbal questions and responses. Personal (face-to-face) interview — direct personal investigation or indirect oral investigation (of other persons with knowledge); telephone / online interview. Types: structured (pre-determined questions, standard order), unstructured (flexible), focused, clinical, depth interviews.

Merits: more information in greater depth; flexibility to clarify and probe; high response rate; works with illiterate respondents; non-verbal cues observed. Demerits: expensive for large, widespread samples; interviewer bias; respondent may give "socially desirable" answers; time-consuming; needs trained interviewers.

3. Questionnaire method

A set of questions printed/typed in a definite order, sent (by post, email, online form) to respondents who read and answer on their own. Detailed in topic 5.

4. Schedule method

A schedule is a proforma of questions, like a questionnaire, but filled in by trained enumerators who meet the respondents, ask the questions and record the replies. Used in census and large health surveys.

5. Case study method

📘 Definition (Nov 2022 2-marker)

A case study is an in-depth, intensive and qualitative study of a single unit — a person, family, patient, institution, event or community — studying its many aspects and the interrelationships among them over time, to understand it completely.

  • Characteristics: single unit, studied intensively, in its natural setting, holistic, often over a long time, qualitative, uses multiple sources (interviews, records, observation).
  • Importance: gives deep insight into complex, rare or new phenomena; generates hypotheses for larger studies; useful in clinical medicine (e.g. first reports of a new ADR or rare disease), in teaching, and in evaluating programmes and institutions; helps understand behaviour and causation in context; flexible.
  • Limitations: findings can't be generalised; subjective; time-consuming; researcher bias; costly.
💊 Pharmacy example

A detailed case study of one patient who developed Stevens–Johnson syndrome after carbamazepine — history, genetics, timeline of drug intake, management, outcome. Such single cases have often been the first signal of a serious drug reaction.

6. Other methods

👥 Focus group discussion

6–10 people discuss a topic guided by a moderator — attitudes, beliefs.

🧪 Experiments / measurement

Laboratory tests, clinical measurements, assays, pill counts, electronic monitoring.

🛒 Kothari's "other methods"

Warranty cards, distributor / store audits, pantry audits, consumer panels, mechanical devices (eye-camera, audiometer), projective techniques, depth interviews, content analysis.

4 Experimental and Observational Studies

Does the researcher just watch, or does she change something on purpose? That one question decides the study type.

🎬 Story mode — EVE scans, the Captain plants

When EVE lands on Earth she scans rocks, fridges, WALL·E himself — recording what she finds, never changing anything. That's an observational study. Back on the Axiom, Captain McCrea decides to plant the seedling and see whether Earth can support life again — he deliberately does something and watches the result. That's an experimental study. EVE asks "what is out there?"; the Captain asks "what happens if I do this?"

📘 Exam definitions

Observational study: a study in which the investigator only observes and measures exposures and outcomes as they occur naturally, without assigning any intervention. (Jul 2023 2-marker.)

Experimental (interventional) study: a study in which the investigator deliberately introduces or manipulates an intervention (drug, procedure, formulation variable) under controlled conditions and measures its effect, usually comparing with a control group.

Detailed epidemiological designs (cohort, case-control, RCT, phases of clinical trials) are in Unit 3.

Principles of experimental design (R. A. Fisher)

🔁

Replication

Repeat each treatment on several units → estimate experimental error, improve precision.
🎲

Randomisation

Assign treatments by chance → removes selection bias, balances unknown factors.
🧱

Local control (blocking)

Group similar units into blocks → reduces variability due to known extraneous factors.

Informal designs (Kothari): before-and-after without control · after-only with control · before-and-after with control. Formal designs: Completely Randomised Design (CRD) · Randomised Block Design (RBD) · Latin Square Design (LSD) · Factorial designs.

🧪 Design of Experiments (DoE) & Factorial Design — KUHS Jul 2023 short note

Design of Experiments is a structured, statistical approach to planning experiments so that the effects of several input variables (factors) on an output (response) can be studied together with the fewest runs. It is central to Quality by Design (QbD) in formulation and process development.

Key terms: Factor = independent variable (e.g. binder concentration) · Level = value of a factor (low/high) · Response = measured outcome (e.g. disintegration time) · Main effect = change in response when a factor goes from low to high · Interaction = when the effect of one factor depends on the level of another.

Factorial design: an experimental design in which all possible combinations of the levels of all factors are studied. Number of runs = (levels)factors.

Two-level factorial design (2k): each of k factors is studied at only two levels — low (coded −1) and high (coded +1). A 2² design needs 4 runs; 2³ needs 8; 2⁴ needs 16. It estimates all main effects and interactions, assuming the response is roughly linear between the two levels. Runs are written in Yates' notation: (1), a, b, ab, c, ac, bc, abc.

🔎 Screening designs (enlist)

  • Full two-level factorial (2k)
  • Fractional factorial (2k−p, e.g. half-fraction 24−1 = 8 runs)
  • Plackett–Burman design (N runs, N a multiple of 4, screens up to N−1 factors)
  • Taguchi orthogonal arrays
  • Definitive screening designs

Screening designs identify the few important factors out of many. Then optimisation designs — central composite, Box–Behnken, mixture designs (response surface methodology) — find the best levels.

✅ Advantages of factorial design

  • Efficient — all factors studied together; fewer runs than one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) for the same precision.
  • Estimates interactions, which OFAT completely misses.
  • Every run contributes to every effect estimate (hidden replication).
  • Conclusions valid over a range of conditions (wider inductive basis).
  • Helps locate the optimum formulation/process; saves time, material and cost.
  • Results analysed objectively by ANOVA / regression; supports QbD design space.

Limitations: runs grow exponentially (2k); two levels can't detect curvature; high-order interactions hard to interpret.

5 Questionnaires and Rating Scales

The most widely used tool in pharmacy-practice projects — and asked three times by KUHS.

🎬 Story mode — the Captain's 20 questions

When Captain McCrea first meets WALL·E, he wants to know everything about Earth. If he asked only "Is Earth nice?" he'd learn nothing. Good questions are short, clear, one idea at a time, in a sensible order — from easy ("What is this?") to deep ("Can we live there?"). And to know how much the passengers miss Earth, he'd need a rating scale: "Rate from 0 to 10".

📘 Exam definition (Jul 2023 2-marker)

A questionnaire is a structured set of written questions arranged in a definite order, which respondents read and answer by themselves (on paper, by post, email or online form). It is the main tool for collecting primary data in surveys. When the same form is filled in by an interviewer/enumerator it is called a schedule.

Types of questionnaire and questions

By structure

Structured (fixed questions and answer options) · Unstructured (open, flexible) · Semi-structured (mix).

By answer form

Closed-ended (choose an option; easy to code) · Open-ended (free text; rich but hard to analyse).

Question formats

Dichotomous (Yes/No) · multiple choice · Likert / rating · ranking · filter (contingency) · matrix · open-ended.

Characteristics of a good questionnaire

  • Short and simple; covers all objectives
  • Questions in logical sequence, easy → difficult
  • Clear instructions; attractive layout
  • Avoids ambiguous, leading, personal or embarrassing questions
  • Pre-tested, valid and reliable
  • Easy to code and analyse

Bad question → good question

❌ "Don't you agree that generic medicines are cheap and effective?" (leading + double-barrelled)

✅ "Generic medicines are as effective as branded medicines." Strongly agree … Strongly disagree

✅ "Generic medicines are affordable." Strongly agree … Strongly disagree

✅ Advantages (Kothari)

  • Low cost even for a large, widely spread population
  • Free from interviewer bias; answers in respondents' own words
  • Respondents have time to give well-thought answers
  • Reaches respondents who are not easily approachable
  • Large samples possible → more dependable results; anonymity

❌ Disadvantages

  • Low rate of return / non-response bias
  • Only for literate and cooperative respondents
  • No control once sent; ambiguous replies can't be clarified
  • Slow; omissions in some questions
  • Not certain who actually filled it

Rating scales

📘 Definition

A rating scale is a measuring instrument in which the respondent (or an observer) assigns a value, category or score to an object, person, attitude or symptom along a continuum, e.g. from "strongly agree" to "strongly disagree" or from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain). Rating scales convert qualitative judgements into (usually ordinal) numbers.

⚠️ Errors in rating (Kothari)

Error of leniency (generosity) — rating everyone too high; error of central tendency — avoiding extremes, marking everything "average"; halo effect — one good/bad trait colouring all other ratings. Reduce them with clear anchors, training of raters and balanced (positive + negative) statements.

6 Different Types of Data Distribution

How are the values spread out? Piled in the middle like a bell, or leaning to one side?

🎬 Story mode — WALL·E's cube towers

Suppose WALL·E weighs every cube he makes. Most cubes weigh about the same; a few are a bit lighter or heavier; very few are extremely light or heavy. Stack the cubes by weight class and the towers form a smooth hill — tallest in the middle, falling off evenly on both sides. That hill is the famous bell-shaped normal distribution. If instead most cubes were light with a few very heavy fridges, the hill would lean — a skewed distribution.

📘 Definition

A data (frequency) distribution is the pattern showing how often each value or class of values of a variable occurs. A probability distribution is the theoretical model giving the probability of each possible value of a random variable.

Classification of data distributions (Dec 2023 2-marker)

A. By shape of frequency distribution

  • Symmetrical — normal (bell-shaped), uniform/rectangular
  • Asymmetrical (skewed) — positively (right) skewed; negatively (left) skewed
  • J-shaped and reverse J-shaped
  • U-shaped
  • Bimodal / multimodal (two or more peaks)
  • By peakedness (kurtosis): leptokurtic (tall, thin), mesokurtic (normal), platykurtic (flat)

B. Theoretical (probability) distributions

  • Discrete: Binomial, Poisson
  • Continuous: Normal (Gaussian), standard normal (z)
  • Sampling distributions used in tests: Student's t, chi-square (χ²), F-distribution
  • Others: log-normal, exponential

The normal (Gaussian) distribution

📘 Exam definition (Dec 2023 short · Model brief)

The normal distribution is a continuous, symmetrical, bell-shaped probability distribution in which most values cluster around the mean and frequencies fall off equally on both sides. It is completely described by two parameters — the mean (μ) and the standard deviation (σ).

f(x) = [1 / (σ√(2π))] · e−(x − μ)² / 2σ² Standard normal variate: z = (x − μ) / σ, which has mean 0 and SD 1

Properties of the normal curve

  1. Bell-shaped and symmetrical about the mean.
  2. Mean = median = mode (all at the centre); unimodal.
  3. Defined completely by μ and σ.
  4. Asymptotic — tails approach but never touch the x-axis.
  5. Total area under the curve = 1 (100%).
  6. μ ± 1σ covers 68.27%, μ ± 2σ covers 95.45%, μ ± 3σ covers 99.73% of values; μ ± 1.96σ covers exactly 95%.
  7. Points of inflection at μ ± σ.
  8. Skewness = 0; it is mesokurtic.

Examples & importance

Examples: height, weight, blood pressure, haemoglobin and serum cholesterol in healthy adults; weights of tablets in a batch; fill volumes of ampoules; IQ; random errors of measurement.

Importance: basis of parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA, z-test) · used to set normal reference ranges (mean ± 2 SD) · quality-control charts (±3σ limits) · confidence intervals · sampling distributions of means tend to normal for large samples (central limit theorem).

💊 Worked example — tablet weights

Paracetamol tablets: mean 500 mg, SD 5 mg, normally distributed. → 68% of tablets lie between 495–505 mg, 95% between 490–510 mg, 99.7% between 485–515 mg. A 510 mg tablet has z = (510 − 500)/5 = +2, i.e. it is 2 SD above the mean; only about 2.3% of tablets are heavier than this.

Skewed distributions

Karl Pearson's empirical relation for moderately skewed data: Mode ≈ 3 Median − 2 Mean. Skewed data are usually summarised by the median and analysed by non-parametric tests (or after log transformation).

Binomial and Poisson distributions (discrete)

🎯 Binomial distribution

For n independent trials, each with only two outcomes (success/failure) and a constant probability of success p (q = 1 − p).

P(X = r) = ⁿCᵣ · pʳ · qⁿ⁻ʳ mean = np · variance = npq

Example: a drug cures 60% of patients. Probability that exactly 2 of 5 patients are cured = ⁵C₂ × 0.6² × 0.4³ = 10 × 0.36 × 0.064 = 0.2304.

🚨 Poisson distribution

For the number of rare events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space, when events are independent; a limiting case of binomial (n large, p very small).

P(X = r) = e−λ · λʳ / r! mean = variance = λ

Example: a hospital receives on average λ = 2 ADR reports per week. Probability of a week with no reports = e−2 = 0.135. Other uses: rare drug reactions, radioactive counts, colonies per plate.

🧠 Mnemonic

"Bi = two outcomes, Poi = rare Poisson-ous events, Normal = the bell." Binomial: mean np. Poisson: mean = variance. Normal: mean = median = mode.

Editing is examining collected raw data to detect errors and omissions and correct them where possible. Field editing is done by the investigator soon after the interview; central editing is done in the office when all forms are received.

Coding

📘 Definition (Kothari)

Coding is the process of assigning numerals or other symbols to answers so that responses can be put into a limited number of categories or classes, which makes computer entry and analysis possible.

  • Rules for categories: appropriate to the research problem; exhaustive (a class for every response); mutually exclusive (each response fits only one class); unidimensional (one concept per category set).
  • Pre-coding for closed questions (codes printed on the questionnaire) vs post-coding for open-ended answers (codes decided after reading the answers).
  • A codebook (coding frame) lists each variable, its column/name, codes and meaning; a special code (e.g. 9 or 99) is kept for missing data.
  • Data are then entered in Excel/SPSS; double data entry and range checks reduce entry errors.

Classification

Classification is the process of arranging data into groups or classes according to their common characteristics. Types: geographical (by area — district, state), chronological (by time — year, month), qualitative (by attribute — gender, blood group, drug class) and quantitative (by magnitude in class intervals — age, weight, dose).

Class-interval terms: class limits (lower/upper), class width, mid-point = (lower + upper)/2, frequency. Exclusive classes (0–10, 10–20: upper limit not included) vs inclusive classes (0–9, 10–19). Open-ended classes ("above 60"). Number of classes by Sturges' rule: k = 1 + 3.322 log₁₀N.

✅ Advantages (Dec 2023 — 5 marks)

  1. Easy and quick to understand — complex data simplified at a glance.
  2. Attractive and impressive; easily remembered.
  3. Easy comparison between groups, places or periods.
  4. Shows trends, patterns and relationships (time series, correlation).
  5. Universal — understood even by lay people; no language barrier.
  6. Saves time and effort for the reader.

Q. Discuss the various methods of data collection with their merits and demerits. Predicted
Essay · 10 marks · syllabus line (parts asked as 2-markers in Nov 2022 & Jul 2023) · ~25 min
Definition + classification chart (1½ m) → Observation (2 m) → Interview (1½ m) → Questionnaire & schedule (2 m) → Case study, FGD, experiment (1½ m) → Secondary data methods + precautions (1 m) → Conclusion (½ m)

1. Introduction

Data collection is the systematic gathering of information on variables of interest to answer research questions. Methods are classified as methods for primary data (observation, interview, questionnaire, schedule, case study, focus group, experiment) and secondary data (published and unpublished sources).

2. Observation method

Systematic viewing and recording of behaviour or events as they occur, without questioning the respondent. Types: structured/unstructured, participant/non-participant, controlled/uncontrolled. Merits: eliminates subjective bias, records current behaviour, needs no respondent cooperation, useful for infants and animals. Demerits: expensive, limited information, can't study opinions or past events, observer bias, Hawthorne effect.

3. Interview method

Oral-verbal questioning — personal (direct/indirect) or telephone; structured, unstructured, focused, clinical or depth interviews. Merits: in-depth information, flexibility, clarification possible, high response, usable with illiterate respondents. Demerits: costly, time-consuming, interviewer bias, needs trained interviewers.

4. Questionnaire method

A set of questions in a definite order answered by respondents themselves (mail/online). Merits: cheap for large, scattered samples; free from interviewer bias; time to think; anonymity. Demerits: low response rate, only for literate respondents, no clarification, incomplete answers.

5. Schedule method

Proforma filled in by trained enumerators who ask the questions. Merits: high response, usable for illiterate people, doubts clarified. Demerits: costly, enumerator bias, needs supervision.

6. Other primary methods

  • Case study — in-depth study of a single unit; gives insight and generates hypotheses but can't be generalised.
  • Focus group discussion — moderated group of 6–10 people; reveals attitudes quickly; dominated by vocal members.
  • Experiments/measurements — laboratory tests, clinical measurements, assays; accurate and objective but costly.
  • Others — consumer panels, audits, mechanical devices, projective techniques, content analysis.

7. Secondary data

From published sources (Census, NFHS, WHO reports, journals) and unpublished records (hospital records, theses). Merits: cheap, quick, large coverage. Demerits: may be unsuitable, outdated or inaccurate — check reliability, suitability and adequacy before use.

Conclusion

No single method is best; the choice depends on the objectives, nature of respondents, resources, time and accuracy required. Often methods are combined (e.g. questionnaire + record review).

Q. Describe the steps involved in construction of a questionnaire.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 · ~12 min
Definition (½ m) → 10 steps with one line each as a flow chart (3½ m) → qualities of a good questionnaire (1 m)

A questionnaire is a structured set of questions in a definite order answered by respondents themselves.

  1. Specify the information needed from objectives and variables.
  2. Define the target respondents — age, literacy, language.
  3. Select the method of administration — self-administered, mail, online, telephone.
  4. Decide the content of each question — is it necessary and answerable?
  5. Decide the response format — open-ended, closed, multiple choice, Likert scale.
  6. Word the questions — simple, short, specific; avoid leading, loaded, double-barrelled and negative questions; translate and back-translate.
  7. Arrange the sequence — general to specific (funnel), easy first, sensitive later, demographics last.
  8. Design the layout — cover letter, instructions, numbering, codes.
  9. Pre-test (pilot study) on a small similar group; establish validity and reliability (Cronbach's α).
  10. Revise and finalise the questionnaire and coding scheme.

A good questionnaire is short, simple, logically ordered, covers all objectives, is unbiased, pre-tested, valid and reliable.

Q. Explain normal distributions with examples.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Dec 2023 (2-mark version in Model paper) · ~12 min
Definition + formula (1 m) → curve diagram with 68–95–99.7 (1½ m) → 6–8 properties (1½ m) → examples + importance (1 m)

The normal (Gaussian) distribution is a continuous, symmetrical, bell-shaped probability distribution in which most values lie near the mean and frequencies decrease equally on both sides. It is defined by its mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ). Standard normal variate z = (x − μ)/σ has mean 0 and SD 1.

[Draw Fig 2.5 — bell curve with μ ± 1σ = 68.27%, ± 2σ = 95.45%, ± 3σ = 99.73%.]

Properties

  1. Bell-shaped and symmetrical about the mean.
  2. Mean = median = mode.
  3. Unimodal; points of inflection at μ ± σ.
  4. Asymptotic — tails never touch the X-axis.
  5. Total area under the curve = 1.
  6. Area rule: 68.27%, 95.45%, 99.73% within 1, 2, 3 SD; 95% within ± 1.96 SD.
  7. Skewness zero; mesokurtic.

Examples

Height, weight, blood pressure, haemoglobin of healthy adults; weights of tablets in a batch (e.g. mean 500 mg, SD 5 mg → 95% between 490 and 510 mg); IQ; measurement errors.

Importance

Basis of parametric tests (z, t, ANOVA), reference ranges (mean ± 2 SD), quality-control charts (± 3σ), confidence intervals and the central limit theorem.

Screening designs

Used to identify the few significant factors from many: (1) full two-level factorial design (2k), (2) fractional factorial design (2k−p), (3) Plackett–Burman design, (4) Taguchi orthogonal arrays, (5) definitive screening designs.

Two-level factorial design

A factorial design studies all combinations of the levels of all factors. In a two-level factorial design (2k), each of k factors is studied at a low (−1) and high (+1) level, giving 2k runs: 2² = 4, 2³ = 8 runs. Runs are written in Yates' notation (1), a, b, ab. Q. Explain case study. Write its importance.
Brief · 2 marks · Nov 2022

A case study is an in-depth, intensive, qualitative study of a single unit — a patient, family, institution or event — covering all its aspects and their interrelations, often over time.

Importance: gives deep insight into rare or complex situations (e.g. first report of a new ADR); generates hypotheses for larger studies; useful in clinical teaching and evaluating programmes; studies the unit in its natural context.

Q. Write the meaning of questionnaire.
Brief · 2 marks · July 2023

A questionnaire is a structured set of written questions arranged in a definite order, which respondents read and answer by themselves (on paper, by post or online). It is a main tool for collecting primary data in surveys. Types: structured/unstructured; open-ended/closed-ended. (If an enumerator fills it, it is a schedule.)

Q. List out methods of data collection.
Brief · 2 marks · July 2023
  • Primary data: observation, interview (personal, telephone), questionnaire, schedule, case study, focus group discussion, experiments/measurements, panels and audits.
  • Secondary data: published sources (government and international reports, journals, books) and unpublished sources (hospital records, theses, company records).
Q. Observational study.
Brief · 2 marks · July 2023

An observational study is one in which the investigator only observes and measures exposures and outcomes as they occur naturally, without assigning any intervention. Types: descriptive (case report, case series, cross-sectional) and analytical (ecological, case-control, cohort). E.g. comparing lung cancer incidence in smokers and non-smokers. It shows association but is prone to bias and confounding. (Details: Unit 3.)

Q. Classify the types of data distribution.
Brief · 2 marks · Dec 2023
  • By shape: symmetrical (normal, uniform); skewed — positive (right) and negative (left); J-shaped, U-shaped; bimodal/multimodal; by kurtosis — lepto-, meso-, platykurtic.
  • Theoretical (probability): discrete — binomial, Poisson; continuous — normal (Gaussian); sampling distributions — t, chi-square, F.

Q. What is meant by normal distribution of data?
Brief · 2 marks · Model paper

A continuous, symmetrical, bell-shaped distribution in which mean = median = mode and values cluster around the mean; defined by μ and σ; 68.27%, 95.45% and 99.73% of values lie within ±1, ±2 and ±3 SD. E.g. heights of adults, tablet weights in a batch. [Small sketch of the bell curve.]

📝 One-Page Cheat Sheet — Unit 2

Read this the night before the exam. Everything important, nothing extra.

Types of data

Qualitative (nominal, ordinal) · Quantitative (discrete, continuous → interval, ratio). NOIR. Also primary/secondary, cross-sectional/time-series, grouped/ungrouped.

Primary vs secondary

Primary: original, own purpose, costly, reliable (observation, interview, questionnaire, schedule, experiment). Secondary: existing, cheap, check RSA (Census, NFHS, WHO, hospital records).

Data collection methods

O-I-Q-S-C-E-F: Observation, Interview, Questionnaire, Schedule, Case study, Experiment, Focus group. Observation advantages: no subjective bias, current, no cooperation needed.

Experimental vs observational

Investigator assigns intervention vs only observes. Fisher: replication, randomisation, local control. Designs: CRD, RBD, LSD, factorial.

Factorial design

2k runs; levels −1/+1; Yates (1), a, b, ab. Effect A = [(a+ab) − ((1)+b)]/2. Screening: fractional factorial, Plackett–Burman, Taguchi. Advantages: efficient, interactions, optimum.

Questionnaire

10 steps: info → respondents → method → content → format → wording → sequence → layout → pilot (validity, Cronbach α) → finalise.

Rating scales

Graphic (VAS 100 mm), numerical (NRS 0–10), Likert, semantic differential, Thurstone, Guttman, ranking. Errors: leniency, central tendency, halo.

Distributions

Shape: symmetric, +skew (mode<median<mean), −skew, J, U, bimodal; kurtosis. Theoretical: binomial (np, npq), Poisson (mean = variance), normal, t, χ², F.

Normal curve

Bell, symmetric, mean = median = mode, asymptotic, area 1, 68.27/95.45/99.73%, z = (x − μ)/σ. E.g. tablet weights, height, BP.

Coding & tabulation

Edit → code → classify → tabulate. Codes: exhaustive, mutually exclusive. Table parts: N-T-H-C-S-B-F-S. Simple vs complex tables.

Graphs

Bar (gaps), pie (angle = %×3.6°), histogram (touching), frequency polygon (mid-points), ogive (median), line (trend), scatter (correlation).

Graph vs table

Graph: approximate, quick, attractive, trends, everyone. Table: exact, detailed, analysis, experts. Complementary.
