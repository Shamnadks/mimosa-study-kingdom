1 Stability Testing of Herbal Medicines

How long will our golden flower keep its magic, and under what weather?

🎬 Story mode — Olaf and the hair that turned brown

Olaf loves summer, but heat makes him melt — so Elsa gives him a personal flurry: his own controlled storage condition. In Tangled, the moment Rapunzel's magic hair is cut, it turns brown and loses its power — a visible sign of degradation. A herbal medicine behaves the same way: heat, humidity, light and air slowly "melt" its constituents, change its colour and weaken it. Stability testing deliberately places the product in known "weathers" (temperature + humidity + light) and checks it at fixed times, so we can promise how long it stays good — its shelf life.

📘 Exam definitions (write exactly these)

Stability of a herbal product is its ability to retain, within specified limits and throughout its storage and use, the same identity, strength (marker content), quality, purity and safety that it had at the time of manufacture.

Stability testing is a series of tests designed to give evidence on how the quality of a drug substance or product varies with time under the influence of environmental factors such as temperature, humidity and light, so that recommended storage conditions, re-test period and shelf life can be established. (ICH Q1A(R2) / WHO)

Shelf life (expiry dating period) is the time period during which a product is expected to remain within its approved specification, provided it is stored under the conditions stated on the label. The expiry date printed on the pack = date of manufacture + shelf life.

Why stability testing of herbals is a big challenge

For a synthetic tablet we follow one known molecule. For a herbal product we follow a whole "forest" of molecules.

🌿 Complex mixture

An extract contains hundreds of constituents; each may degrade differently and interact with others (matrix effect).

❓ Active often unknown

The constituent responsible for activity is often unknown, so we track markers and a whole chromatographic fingerprint instead of one active.

🌦️ Natural variability

Composition changes with species, plant part, season, soil, harvest time, drying and storage of raw material (GACP), so batches start from different points.

🧪 Few reference standards

Pure marker standards are costly or unavailable; markers are present in tiny amounts; validated stability-indicating methods are hard to develop.

💧 Hygroscopic & microbe-friendly

Powders (churna) and dry extracts absorb moisture → caking, hydrolysis, mould growth; natural materials carry a microbial load.

🫙 Traditional dosage forms

Asava/arishta (self-generated alcohol, sediment), ghrita/taila (rancidity), avaleha (sugar crystallisation, fermentation), bhasma (metal-mineral) — each needs its own test parameters.

🌸 Volatile & light-sensitive constituents

Essential oils evaporate; curcumin, hypericin and chlorophyll fade in light.

🧬 Active enzymes

Plant enzymes left in crude drugs can hydrolyse glycosides when moisture is present.

Types of stability studies

🕰️

Long-term (real-time)

At the recommended storage condition for the full proposed shelf life. Gives the final, most trusted data.
🔥

Accelerated

Exaggerated conditions (40 °C / 75 % RH, 6 months) to speed up degradation and predict shelf life early.
⚖️

Intermediate

30 °C / 65 % RH; done only when long-term is at 25 °C / 60 % RH and accelerated shows "significant change".
💥

Stress (forced degradation)

Harsh heat, humidity, acid/base, oxidant (H₂O₂), light — reveals degradation pathways and proves the method is stability-indicating.
☀️

Photostability (ICH Q1B)

Exposure to not less than 1.2 million lux·hours of visible light and 200 W·h/m² of near-UV energy.
🥄

In-use & cycling

After opening (syrup, asava, oil) and freeze–thaw / temperature-cycling studies for transport excursions.

⚠️ Classic confusion

ICH Q1A covers the general conditions; ICH Q1F (zones III & IV) was withdrawn in 2006 and the choice was left to WHO and the regions. WHO split Zone IV into IVa (30/65) and IVb (30/75). Don't write "India = Zone II".

Testing frequency (time points)

  • Long-term: every 3 months in year 1, every 6 months in year 2, then yearly → 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36 months …
  • Accelerated: minimum 3 points including start and end → 0, 3, 6 months.
  • Intermediate: minimum 4 points → 0, 6, 9, 12 months.

"Significant change" (triggers intermediate testing / a shorter shelf life)

  • A change of 5 % or more in assay (active / marker content) from the initial value.
  • Any degradation product exceeding its acceptance limit.
  • Failure in appearance, physical attributes or functionality (colour, phase separation, caking, hardness).
  • Failure of the pH limit, or of dissolution for 12 dosage units (as applicable).

For herbal products where only markers (not true actives) are known, regulators accept a justified, somewhat wider tolerance for marker content during shelf life.

Predicting shelf life — Arrhenius approach

k = A · e−Ea/RT  ⇒  log k = log A − Ea / 2.303 RTk = rate constant, A = frequency factor, Ea = activation energy, R = gas constant, T = absolute temperature (K). Plot log k against 1/T from 3–4 elevated temperatures → extrapolate k at room temperature.
t90 = 0.105 / k (first order)   ·   t90 = 0.1 C₀ / k (zero order)   ·   t½ = 0.693 / kt₉₀ = time for the content to fall to 90 % of the initial value (10 % loss) = shelf life

In practice, shelf life is assigned from long-term data by statistical trend analysis (ICH Q1E): the time at which the 95 % confidence limit of the degradation line meets the specification limit. Arrhenius predictions are only supportive for herbals, because many reactions happen together in a mixture — hence real-time data are always needed.

India: shelf life of ASU drugs (Rule 161-B)

  • Rule 161-B of the Drugs Rules, 1945 fixes the shelf life of Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani medicines from the date of manufacture (table revised in 2016). It ranges from about 1 year (e.g. arka) to 10 years (e.g. asava-arishta, bhasma, pishti, parpati), unless scientific stability data justify a different period.
  • The expiry date must be shown conspicuously on the label; after expiry the medicine cannot be marketed or sold.
  • Manufacturers may submit real-time stability data (as per Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia guidance) to support their own shelf life.
🆕 Update (2026)

The Drugs (Eleventh Amendment) Rules, 2026 (Ministry of Ayush) allow an initial shelf life from accelerated studies — 3-month accelerated data → 1 year, 6-month data → 2 years — provided real-time data follow; any shelf life beyond 2 years must be based on real-time data. A change of dosage form also needs stability data.

💊 Pharmacy example

Triphala churna stored at 30 °C/75 % RH: test at 0, 3, 6, 9, 12 months for colour, odour, LOD, microbial count, gallic acid content (HPTLC) and fingerprint. If gallic acid drops by 5 % or more, or mould appears, change to an aluminium laminate pouch with a desiccant. For Ashwagandha capsules track withanolides; for Dashamoolarishta track alcohol content and sediment.

⭐ Exam tip

For a 5-mark stability answer: definition → why herbals are challenging (4 points) → climatic zone table → conditions table → protocol parameters → shelf life. Draw Fig 4.1 or 4.2. Always write "India – Zone IVb, 30 °C ± 2 °C / 75 % ± 5 % RH".

⚠️ Common mistake

Accelerated = 40 °C / 75 % RH (not 40/65). Intermediate = 30 °C / 65 % RH. Long-term for India = 30/75. Accelerated data predict; long-term data prove.

2 Chromatographic Techniques in Standardization of Herbal Products

One drop of extract, a rainbow of constituents — the herb's "signature".

🎬 Story mode — the rainbow on Rapunzel's wall

Rapunzel paints every inch of her tower. Imagine she drops a blob of mixed green paint on wet paper: the colours creep outwards at different speeds and separate into yellow, blue and green rings. That is chromatography — literally "colour writing". Fittingly, the first chromatography was done on a herbal sample: the Russian botanist Mikhail Tswett (1906) separated plant pigments (chlorophylls and carotenoids) on a column of calcium carbonate. Today every herb has its own "rainbow" — a chromatographic fingerprint — that proves who it is.

📘 Exam definitions

Chromatography is a physical method of separation in which the components of a mixture are distributed between two phases — a stationary phase and a mobile phase that moves through it — and separate because of differences in adsorption, partition, ion-exchange or molecular size.

Chromatographic fingerprint: a chromatogram (TLC/HPTLC band pattern, HPLC or GC trace) that represents the characteristic chemical profile of a herbal material or product. It is used to confirm identity, detect adulteration and show batch-to-batch consistency, even when the active constituents are not fully known (accepted by WHO).

2.1 Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC)

Principle: mainly adsorption. A thin layer (about 0.25 mm) of adsorbent on a glass/aluminium plate is the stationary phase; the solvent rises by capillary action and carries constituents to different heights depending on their affinity for the adsorbent.

Rf = distance travelled by the solute ÷ distance travelled by the solvent frontalways less than 1; characteristic under fixed conditions (plate, solvent, chamber saturation, temperature)

🧱 Adsorbents (stationary phase)

  • Silica gel G (with gypsum binder), silica gel GF254 (with fluorescent indicator)
  • Alumina (aluminium oxide)
  • Kieselguhr (diatomaceous earth), cellulose (partition)
  • Polyamide (phenolics, flavonoids), reversed-phase RP-18 plates

🪄 Detection (visualisation)

  • UV 254 nm (quenching) and 366 nm (fluorescence)
  • Iodine vapour (general)
  • Dragendorff → orange spots (alkaloids)
  • Anisaldehyde–H₂SO₄ / vanillin–H₂SO₄ → terpenes, saponins, steroids
  • NP/PEG → flavonoids fluoresce; FeCl₃ → phenolics/tannins; KOH → anthraquinones (red); ninhydrin → amino acids

Procedure

🧴Prepare plateslurry → spread → dry → activate ~110 °C
✏️Spot sample & standard~1.5–2 cm above the lower edge
🫙Saturate chamberwith mobile phase
⬆️Developuntil front travels ~10–15 cm
🌬️Dry & detectUV / spray reagent
📐Calculate Rfcompare with reference

Applications of TLC in herbal standardization: identity test in pharmacopoeial monographs (API, IP, Ph. Eur.), detection of adulterants and substitutes, limit test for toxic constituents (e.g. absence of aristolochic acids), checking purity of isolated markers, monitoring column fractions and reactions, qualitative fingerprint of extracts (e.g. sennosides in senna, glycyrrhizin in liquorice, piperine in pepper), preliminary screening of phytoconstituents (alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides).

2.2 High-Performance Thin Layer Chromatography (HPTLC)

HPTLC is the advanced, instrumental and automated form of TLC: it uses pre-coated plates with finer, more uniform particles, applies samples as narrow bands with an automatic applicator, develops under controlled conditions, and scans the plate with a densitometer to give reproducible qualitative and quantitative results.

HPTLC workflow (instrumentation)

🔦 Densitometry (in-situ scanning)

After development, a TLC scanner moves a slit of monochromatic light along each track and measures the diffuse reflectance (absorbance) or fluorescence of each band without scraping it off. The output is a densitogram (peaks at each Rf); the peak area is proportional to the amount of substance, so a calibration curve of the marker standard gives the content in the sample. Spectra of bands can be recorded to confirm peak purity and identity.

🖐️ HPTLC fingerprinting

The pattern of bands (number, Rf, colour, intensity) of an extract, under fixed conditions, compared with a botanical reference material and marker standards. Several samples and references run side by side on one plate, so differences jump out visually. It is now an official identification approach: Ph. Eur. general chapter 2.8.25 (HPTLC of herbal drugs) and USP <203> (HPTLC identification of articles of botanical origin).

  • Needs costly instruments and reference standards

2.3 High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)

Principle: mainly partition (reverse-phase is most common: non-polar C18 stationary phase, polar mobile phase such as water/acetonitrile/methanol with acid). The mobile phase is pumped at high pressure through a column packed with very small particles (3–5 µm), giving high resolution. Polar compounds elute first in reverse phase.

🔍 Detectors & when to use them

  • UV-Vis / PDA (photodiode array) — most markers; PDA gives spectra for peak purity & identity
  • Fluorescence — aflatoxins, berberine (very sensitive)
  • RI / ELSD — constituents with weak UV absorbance (sugars, ginsenosides, saponins)
  • MS — identification + trace analysis (LC-MS)

⭐ Role of HPLC in herbal standardization

  • Accurate assay of markers: withanolides, curcuminoids, sennosides A & B, glycyrrhizin, silymarin, hypericin, bacosides, andrographolide, piperine, ginsenosides
  • HPLC fingerprint of extracts (with PDA) for identity and consistency
  • Stability-indicating methods; degradation products
  • Dissolution testing of herbal tablets/capsules
  • Detection of undeclared synthetic drugs (adulteration) e.g. steroids, NSAIDs, sildenafil, glibenclamide in "herbal" products
  • Aflatoxins (fluorescence detection), toxic markers (aristolochic acids)
  • Preparative HPLC to isolate pure reference standards

UHPLC/UPLC (sub-2 µm particles, very high pressure) gives faster and sharper fingerprints of complex extracts.

2.4 Gas Chromatography (GC)

Principle: partition of volatile (or derivatised) compounds between an inert carrier gas (mobile phase — helium, nitrogen, hydrogen) and a liquid stationary phase coated inside a long capillary column kept in a temperature-programmed oven. Components separate by boiling point and polarity and are recorded by a detector.

2.5 Hyphenated techniques: GC-MS and LC-MS

GC-MS

  • GC separates → mass spectrometer identifies each peak by its molecular ion and fragmentation pattern (library matching)
  • Complete profile of essential oils (e.g. tulsi, ginger, turmeric oils)
  • Confirmation of pesticide residues and residual solvents
  • Volatile markers and contaminants
VS

LC-MS / LC-MS/MS

  • HPLC separates non-volatile, polar, heat-labile compounds → MS identifies (ESI/APCI ionisation)
  • Identification of withanolides, ginsenosides, flavonoid glycosides, alkaloids
  • Trace toxic markers: aristolochic acids, pyrrolizidine alkaloids, mycotoxins
  • Detection of hidden synthetic drugs; metabolite and pharmacokinetic studies of herbal markers

Other hyphenated tools: HPLC-PDA, HPTLC-MS, LC-NMR. Advantage: separation + structural identification in one run, very high sensitivity and specificity.

2.6 Column Chromatography

Principle: adsorption (or partition) in a vertical glass column packed with adsorbent — silica gel (60–120 or 100–200 mesh), alumina, or resins. The extract is loaded on top and eluted with solvents of increasing polarity (e.g. hexane → chloroform/ethyl acetate → methanol, gradient elution). Fractions are collected and monitored by TLC; similar fractions are pooled and crystallised.

Variants: flash chromatography, vacuum liquid chromatography, ion-exchange resins (e.g. alkaloid purification).

2.7 Gel Permeation (Size-Exclusion) Chromatography

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC), also called size-exclusion chromatography or gel filtration (when water is the eluent), separates molecules according to their size (molecular weight / hydrodynamic volume) using a column of porous gel beads. Large molecules cannot enter the pores and come out first (with the void volume); small molecules wander through the pores, travel a longer path and elute later. There is ideally no chemical interaction with the gel.

🧫 Gels used

  • Sephadex (cross-linked dextran) — G-series for water; Sephadex LH-20 (hydroxypropylated) works in organic solvents too
  • Bio-Gel P (polyacrylamide), Sepharose (agarose)
  • Styrene–divinylbenzene gels (organic GPC, e.g. Styragel)

Molecular weight is found from a calibration plot of log MW vs elution volume.

🌿 Applications in herbals

  • Purification & molecular-weight distribution of plant polysaccharides (Aloe vera acemannan, Astragalus, ginseng and Tinospora polysaccharides)
  • Characterising gums & mucilages (acacia, guar, psyllium)
  • Separating tannins / proanthocyanidins and flavonoids on Sephadex LH-20 (green tea catechins, grape-seed extract)
  • Clean-up step in pesticide-residue analysis — removes lipids, waxes and chlorophyll from plant extracts
  • Purification / desalting of plant enzymes & proteins (papain, bromelain)

Seven floating lanterns of Rapunzel's birthday = seven chromatography techniques of your syllabus. Order in the essay: planar (TLC, HPTLC) → column (HPLC, GC) → hyphenated → preparative (column, GPC).

💊 Pharmacy example

A turmeric capsule batch: HPTLC fingerprint confirms Curcuma longa (identity), HPLC assays curcumin, demethoxycurcumin and bisdemethoxycurcumin (strength), GC-MS checks the essential-oil profile and pesticide residues, LC-MS screens for synthetic dyes or hidden drugs (purity).

⚠️ Common mistake

In GPC the largest molecules elute first (opposite of what students expect). In reverse-phase HPLC the most polar compounds elute first. HPTLC applies bands, not spots.

Packing and labelling of drugs other than homoeopathic medicines. Labels on packages/containers of drugs for export shall be adapted to meet the specific requirements of the law of the importing country, but must show:

  • Batch or lot number

✅ General

  • Keep all documents ready — export licence, quality certificates (CoA), shipping papers.

5 GMP Requirements — Schedule T

Rapunzel's tower is spotless because she follows the same routine every single morning.

🎬 Story mode — Rapunzel's 7 a.m. routine

Every morning Rapunzel sweeps, does the laundry, polishes, paints, reads and cooks — in the same order, every day. That is a Standard Operating Procedure! Pascal changes colour to warn her — like the colour-coded status labels (quarantine / approved / rejected) on GMP stores. The tower stands far from the dusty town (location), has one clean entrance (controlled entry) and a well of fresh water (water supply). A herbal factory that follows Schedule T works like Rapunzel's tower: clean, orderly, documented.

📘 Exam definitions

Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) is that part of quality assurance which ensures that products are consistently produced and controlled to the quality standards appropriate to their intended use and as required by the marketing authorisation (WHO).

cGMP (current GMP) means GMP using up-to-date technologies, systems and regulations — the word "current" reminds manufacturers that standards keep improving (e.g. US 21 CFR 210–211; WHO GMP for herbal medicines).

Schedule T of the Drugs Rules, 1945 lays down the GMP requirements for factory premises, plant and equipment for the manufacture of Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani (ASU) medicines. Compliance is a condition of the ASU manufacturing licence (Rule 157).

Why herbal GMP needs special care

  • Raw herbs carry soil, insects, microbes and pesticide residues; quality varies with source.
  • Fine powders spread easily → cross-contamination; dust control and dedicated areas needed.
  • Special processes: drying, fermentation (asava–arishta), bhasma preparation with high-temperature puta / furnaces, shodhana of toxic drugs (Schedule E1), oil and ghee processing.
  • Identity of plant material must be confirmed (botanist / pharmacognosist), and herbs must be stored away from pests and moisture.

Schedule T — Part I: Good manufacturing practices (general requirements)

A. Factory premises

  1. Location & surroundings — away from open sewerage, drains, public lavatories or factories producing obnoxious odour, fumes, excessive soot, dust or smoke.
  2. Buildings — designed to avoid contamination; walls/floors smooth, washable, free of cracks; adequate lighting & ventilation; screened against insects, birds, rodents; separate areas for each operation.
  3. Water supply — potable/pure water, tested; separate supply for washing and for manufacture where needed.
  4. Disposal of waste — drains and effluent treatment; safe disposal of waste and toxic residues.
  5. Container's cleaning — washing, drying and storing of containers before filling.
  6. Stores — separate stores for raw materials (with quarantine, approved, rejected areas), packing materials and finished goods; raw herbs identified, labelled, sampled and tested; protection from moisture, pests and light.
  7. Working space — adequate, orderly, to avoid mix-ups and cross-contamination.

B. People, records & control

  1. Health, clothing, sanitation & hygiene of workers — free from contagious disease, clean uniforms, head-gear, gloves, masks; no eating or smoking in work areas.
  2. Medical services — first aid, periodic medical check-up of workers.
  3. Machinery & equipment — as per Part II, suitable, clean, maintained; equipment cleaning records.
  4. Batch manufacturing records (BMR) — raw materials used, quantities, in-process checks, yield, signatures.
  5. Distribution records — where each batch went (for recall).
  6. Record of market complaints (and adverse reactions) — investigation and action.
  7. Quality control — own QC section or an approved testing laboratory; identity, purity and assay of raw materials and finished products; retained samples.
  8. SOPs for every operation.

Q. Describe chromatographic techniques in the standardization of herbal drugs. (Also: Explain the importance of HPLC & HPTLC in standardization of herbal products.)
Essay · 10 marks · MGR Aug 2023 · RGUHS Nov 2023 (HPLC & HPTLC) · MGR 2022 (5m) · write in ~25 min
Definition + why needed (1m) → classification table (1m) → TLC with plate diagram (1.5m) → HPTLC + workflow + applications (2.5m) → HPLC with block diagram + applications (2m) → GC, hyphenated, column, GPC (1.5m) → conclusion (0.5m)

1. Introduction

Standardization of herbal drugs means fixing quantitative and qualitative standards so that every batch has the same identity, purity and strength. Because a herbal drug is a complex mixture whose actives are often unknown, chromatography — separation of components between a stationary and a mobile phase — is the main tool. It gives a chromatographic fingerprint (identity and consistency, accepted by WHO) and allows assay of marker compounds.

2. Classification

Planar: paper, TLC, HPTLC · Column: column chromatography, HPLC, GC, gel permeation · Hyphenated: GC-MS, LC-MS, HPTLC-MS. By mechanism: adsorption, partition, ion-exchange, size exclusion.

3. Thin layer chromatography

Adsorption on silica gel G/GF254 or alumina (0.25 mm layer). Sample spotted, developed in a saturated chamber, detected under UV 254/366 nm or with reagents (Dragendorff for alkaloids, anisaldehyde–H₂SO₄ for terpenoids, NP/PEG for flavonoids). Rf = distance moved by solute / distance moved by solvent front. (Draw Fig 4.3.) Uses: identity tests in API/IP monographs, detection of adulterants, limit tests (aristolochic acids), monitoring isolation.

4. HPTLC

Instrumental TLC with pre-coated fine-particle plates (5–6 µm), automatic band application, short development (3–6 cm) and densitometric scanning. Steps: plate selection & activation → sample/standard preparation → band application → development in twin-trough chamber → drying & derivatization → documentation → densitometry → data analysis → validation (ICH Q2). (Draw Fig 4.4.)

Applications: fingerprint-based identification against botanical reference material (official in Ph. Eur. 2.8.25, USP <203>); detection of substitutes; quantification of markers — withaferin A (Ashwagandha), curcumin (turmeric), piperine (Trikatu), gallic acid (Triphala), glycyrrhizin (liquorice), andrographolide, bacoside A, sennosides; multi-marker analysis of polyherbal formulations; stability studies; aflatoxin/pesticide screening; bioautography.

5. HPLC

High-pressure partition chromatography, usually reverse-phase C18 with water–acetonitrile/methanol gradients. Components: solvent reservoir & degasser → pump → injector → column (with oven) → detector (UV/PDA, fluorescence, RI, ELSD, MS) → data system. (Draw Fig 4.5.)

Applications: precise assay of markers (curcuminoids, withanolides, sennosides A & B, glycyrrhizin, silymarin, ginsenosides with ELSD, hypericin); HPLC-PDA fingerprint; stability-indicating methods; dissolution; detection of undeclared synthetic drugs (steroids, NSAIDs, sildenafil); aflatoxins with fluorescence detection; preparative HPLC for reference standards.

6. Other techniques

  • GC — volatile oils (cineole in eucalyptus, menthol in peppermint, eugenol in clove), pesticide residues (ECD), residual solvents (headspace), ethanol in asava–arishta, fatty-acid profile of oils.
  • GC-MS / LC-MS — structural identification of each peak, trace toxic markers (pyrrolizidine alkaloids, aristolochic acids), adulterants, PK of markers.
  • Column chromatography — silica gel, gradient elution → isolation of marker standards (andrographolide, piperine).
  • Gel permeation — size-based separation (Sephadex) of polysaccharides, tannins; clean-up for pesticide analysis.

7. TLC vs HPTLC vs HPLC (summary)

FeatureTLCHPTLCHPLC
QuantitationSemiYes (densitometry)Yes (most precise)
Samples per run~10Many in parallelOne at a time
FingerprintVisualVisual + digitalChromatogram

Conclusion

Chromatography converts a "black-box" herbal mixture into measurable fingerprints and marker values. A combination of HPTLC (identity, many samples) and HPLC (accurate assay), supported by GC and hyphenated MS techniques, is the modern basis of herbal standardization recommended by WHO and pharmacopoeias.

Q. Write a detailed note on preparation of documents for New Drug Application.
Essay · 10 marks · MGR Mar 2023 · MGR 2022 (5m) · AKTU 2023 (2m "What is NDA") · ~25 min
Definition IND/NDA + authorities (1m) → goals (1m) → pathway diagram + phases (2m) → NDA contents/forms (1.5m) → CTD modules with herbal points (2m) → registration documents: legal/technical/regulatory (1.5m) → processing & India flow (0.5m) → conclusion (0.5m)

1. Definition

An NDA is the application submitted to the regulatory authority for permission to market a new drug. It is preceded by an IND (permission to start human trials). Authorities: US FDA, CDSCO (India; DCGI), MHRA (UK), PMDA (Japan), EMA (EU).

2. Goals of the NDA

  1. Is the drug safe and effective for its proposed use; do benefits outweigh risks?
  2. Is the proposed labelling appropriate?
  3. Are manufacturing methods and controls adequate to ensure identity, strength, quality and purity?

3. When the NDA is filed

Preclinical studies (pharmacology, toxicology, PK under GLP) → IND → Phase I (20–100 healthy volunteers, safety) → Phase II (100–1000 patients, efficacy & dose) → Phase III (hundreds to thousands, confirmation) → NDA filing → review → approval → Phase IV (post-marketing surveillance). (Draw Fig 4.7.)

4. Documentation — the drug's whole story

Clinical test results, ingredients/composition, animal study results, behaviour in the body (PK/PD), and how it is manufactured, processed and packaged. US forms: FDA 356h (application), FDA 3397 (user-fee cover sheet), FDA 3331 (NDA field alert report), DMF binder specifications, impurity data; incomplete files receive a "refuse to file".

5. CTD format (ICH M4)

(Draw Fig 4.8.) Module 1 regional administrative information (forms, licences, labels, COPP) · Module 2 summaries (quality overall summary, non-clinical and clinical overviews) · Module 3 quality — for a herbal drug: botanical identity, plant part, source and GACP, macroscopy/microscopy, TLC/HPTLC fingerprint, extraction process and drug–extract ratio, markers, contaminant limits (heavy metals, pesticides, aflatoxins, microbes), specifications, validation, container, stability · Module 4 non-clinical reports (pharmacology, toxicology) · Module 5 clinical study reports.

6. Documents for registration with CDSCO

  • Legal: Form 40 (signed by Indian agent), power of attorney attested by the Indian embassy and jointly signed, Schedule D(I) & D(II) information signed by the manufacturer.
  • Technical: Plant Master File (plant sketch, organogram, machinery, hygiene, HVAC, men–material movement); Drug Master File (process flow chart, QA procedures, contamination control, container closure, stability, toxicology/biocompatibility, clinical study report); labels & inserts.
  • Regulatory (notarized): plant registration certificate, manufacturing & marketing licence, Free Sale Certificate, GMP certificate.

7. Processing

FDA decides within 60 days whether to file (else Refuse-to-File letter); a Day-74 letter follows; standard review ≈ 10 months, priority ≈ 6 months; outcome = approval or Complete Response Letter. In India: Ethics Committee + CT application (Form CT-04 under the NDCT Rules, 2019) → New Drugs Division → Subject Expert Committee → DCGI permission → trials → application for marketing → licence granted or rejected. Herbal extracts as new drugs follow the phytopharmaceutical route (≥ 4 defined bioactive/phytochemical markers).

Conclusion

A well-organised NDA (CTD/eCTD) tells the complete, verifiable story of a drug's quality, safety and efficacy; for herbal products, strong Module 3 data on botanical identity, standardization and stability are the key to approval.

Q. Explain the infrastructural requirements under GMP for the herbal industry. (Also: Discuss GMP in detail / GMP requirement for herbal medicine.)

1. Introduction

GMP is the part of quality assurance that ensures products are consistently produced and controlled to appropriate quality standards. For Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani units in India, GMP is laid down in Schedule T of the Drugs Rules, 1945, notified on 23 June 2000 (mandatory for new units; existing units by 2002). Compliance is a condition of the licence (Form 25-D, Rule 157). Internationally, WHO GMP guidelines for herbal medicines apply.

2. Factory premises (Schedule T Part I)

  1. Location & surroundings — away from open drains, sewage, public lavatories and factories emitting smoke, dust or obnoxious odours.
  2. Buildings — built to prevent contamination; smooth, washable, crack-free walls and floors; adequate light and ventilation; protection from insects, birds and rodents; separate areas for receiving, storage, processing, packing, QC and offices; drainage.
  3. Water supply — clean potable water; purified water for preparations; periodic testing.
  4. Disposal of waste — drains, effluent treatment, safe disposal of solid and toxic waste.
  5. Container's cleaning — facilities to wash, dry and store containers.
  6. Stores — separate for raw materials, packing materials and finished goods; raw herbs identified, labelled, kept in quarantine until tested, protected from moisture and pests; status labels.
  7. Working space — adequate for orderly placement of equipment and materials, preventing mix-ups and cross-contamination.

3. Personnel, equipment and documentation

  • Health, clothing, sanitation & hygiene of workers — medical fitness, clean uniforms, caps, gloves, masks; hand-washing; no eating/smoking in work areas.
  • Medical services — first aid and periodic check-up.
  • Machinery & equipment — suitable, easily cleaned, maintained, as per Part II.
  • Records — batch manufacturing records, distribution records, market complaint records; SOPs.
  • Quality control — in-house QC section or approved laboratory (Rules 160-A to 160-J): tests on raw materials, in-process and finished products; reference samples.

4. Part II — machinery & minimum premises

Lists machinery and minimum area for each category (churna — pulveriser, sieves, mixer; vati — granulator, tablet machine; asava–arishta — fermentation vessels; taila/ghrita — heating vessels; bhasma — furnace/puta; arka — distillation unit). A minimum of about 1200 sq. ft covered area with separate cabins is commonly quoted.

5. Material flow

(Draw Fig 4.10.) Receipt → quarantine → QC sampling → approved store / rejected → dispensing → processing → packing → QC release → finished goods → distribution, with one-way flow and separate men & material entry.

Conclusion

Proper premises, hygiene, equipment, documentation and QC prevent contamination and ensure consistent quality of herbal medicines. 🆕 The Drugs (Eleventh Amendment) Rules, 2026 have rewritten Schedule T in greater detail; existing units must comply within a ~3-year transition period.

Q. What is stability testing? Explain the challenges in stability testing of herbal products and describe a suitable protocol. Predicted essay
Essay · 10 marks · question bank Qs 9, 12, 14, 20, 25, 85 · ~25 min
Definitions (1m) → objectives (1m) → challenges (2m) → factors & predictable changes (1.5m) → climatic zones + conditions tables (2m) → protocol steps + parameters + timeline figure (2m) → shelf life + conclusion (0.5m)

1. Definitions

Stability testing gives evidence of how the quality of a drug varies with time under temperature, humidity and light, to establish storage conditions and shelf life (period during which the product remains within specification when stored as labelled).

2. Objectives

Fix shelf life and expiry date; fix storage conditions; select packaging; understand degradation; ensure safety; provide regulatory data.

3. Challenges with herbal products

  • Complex multi-constituent mixtures; actives often unknown → markers and fingerprints must be followed.
  • Natural variability of raw materials; low marker levels; lack of reference standards.
  • Hygroscopic powders/extracts, microbial load, residual enzymes.
  • Volatile and light-sensitive constituents.
  • Traditional dosage forms (asava, ghrita, avaleha, bhasma) need special parameters.
  • Arrhenius predictions unreliable because many reactions occur together.

4. Factors and changes

Humidity, oxygen, temperature, light, pH, microbes/enzymes, packaging ("HOT LAMP") cause oxidation (rancid taila), hydrolysis (glycosides), racemisation (hyoscyamine → atropine), polymerisation (tannins → phlobaphenes), photolysis (curcumin), loss of volatile oil, caking, crystallisation and microbial growth.

5. Climatic zones & storage conditions

Zone I 21 °C/45 % RH; II 25 °C/60 %; III 30 °C/35 %; IVa 30 °C/65 %; IVb 30 °C/75 % (India). Long-term: 25/60, 30/65 or 30/75 (12 months at submission); intermediate 30 °C/65 % RH (6 months); accelerated 40 °C/75 % RH (6 months). Photostability as per ICH Q1B.

6. Protocol

(a) ≥ 3 batches; (b) final container–closure; (c) conditions and duration; (d) time points — long-term 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36 months; accelerated 0, 3, 6 (draw Fig 4.2); (e) parameters — organoleptic, physical (LOD, pH, viscosity, disintegration, hardness), chemical (marker assay by HPLC/HPTLC, fingerprint, degradation products), microbial (TAMC, TYMC, pathogens), dosage-form specific (alcohol content of arishta, peroxide value of taila); (f) validated stability-indicating methods and acceptance criteria ("significant change" = ≥ 5 % change in assay, etc.); (g) evaluation and shelf-life statement; (h) commitment to continue long-term testing.

7. Shelf life

From long-term trend analysis (ICH Q1E); supportive Arrhenius estimate k = A·e−Ea/RT, t₉₀ = 0.105/k. For ASU drugs, Rule 161-B gives shelf lives (1–10 years) unless stability data justify otherwise; 🆕 2026 amendment permits initial 1–2 years from accelerated data, longer only with real-time data.

Conclusion

A herbal stability study must follow both markers and the overall fingerprint under Zone IVb conditions to give a reliable, scientifically proven shelf life.

Q. What are the applications of HPTLC in herbal drug standardization?
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Model · MGR Mar 2023 · ~12 min
Definition + 3 features (1m) → workflow diagram (1m) → 8 applications with examples (2.5m) → advantages (0.5m)

HPTLC is an automated, instrumental form of TLC using pre-coated plates of fine particles (5–6 µm), band-wise sample application, controlled development and densitometric scanning, giving reproducible qualitative and quantitative results. (Draw Fig 4.4.)

Q. What is gel permeation chromatography? Give its applications.
Short note · 5 marks · AKTU 2023 · ~12 min
Definition (1m) → principle with diagram (1.5m) → gels (0.5m) → applications (2m)

Gel permeation chromatography (size-exclusion; gel filtration when aqueous) separates molecules on the basis of molecular size using a column of porous, inert gel beads.

Principle: large molecules are excluded from the pores and elute first (void volume); smaller molecules diffuse into the pores, take a longer path and elute later. Molecular weight is estimated from a calibration plot of log MW vs elution volume. (Draw Fig 4.6.)

Gels: Sephadex G-series (cross-linked dextran), Sephadex LH-20 (organic solvents), Bio-Gel P (polyacrylamide), Sepharose (agarose), styrene–divinylbenzene gels.

Applications: purification and MW distribution of plant polysaccharides (Aloe acemannan, Astragalus, ginseng); characterisation of gums and mucilages; fractionation of tannins, proanthocyanidins and flavonoids on Sephadex LH-20; clean-up of plant extracts before pesticide-residue analysis (removes lipids, chlorophyll); desalting and purification of plant enzymes (papain, bromelain); separation of oligomers/polymers in extracts.

An NDA is the application submitted by a sponsor to the regulatory authority (US FDA / CDSCO) for permission to market a new drug, after preclinical studies and Phase I–III trials. It contains preclinical and clinical data, composition, manufacturing and controls, stability and proposed labelling (CTD Modules 1–5), so that safety, efficacy and quality can be judged.

Q. Difference between TLC & HPTLC / Define TLC and HPTLC.
Brief · 2 marks · MGR 2022 · MGR Aug 2023 · bank · 3 min

Q. What are the different climatic zones for stability testing?
Brief · 2 marks · bank Q85 · 3 min

Zone I temperate (21 °C/45 % RH) · Zone II subtropical/Mediterranean (25 °C/60 % RH) · Zone III hot-dry (30 °C/35 % RH) · Zone IVa hot-humid (30 °C/65 % RH) · Zone IVb hot, very humid (30 °C/75 % RH) — India.

Q. What is accelerated stability study?
Brief · 2 marks · bank Q98 · 3 min

A study in which the product is stored under exaggerated conditions — 40 °C ± 2 °C / 75 % ± 5 % RH for 6 months (tested at 0, 3, 6 months) — to increase the rate of chemical and physical degradation, so that shelf life can be predicted early (with Arrhenius kinetics) and the effect of short excursions judged. Must be confirmed by real-time data.

Q. What is chromatographic fingerprinting?
Brief · 2 marks · bank Qs 17, 41, 69, 100 · 3 min

A chromatographic fingerprint is a chromatogram (HPTLC band pattern, HPLC or GC profile) of a herbal material or product that represents its characteristic chemical composition. Comparison with a reference fingerprint confirms identity, detects adulteration and shows batch-to-batch consistency even when actives are unknown (accepted by WHO).

Q. What are the adsorbents used in TLC?
Brief · 2 marks · bank Q65 · 3 min

Silica gel G (with calcium sulphate binder), silica gel GF254 (with fluorescent indicator), silica gel H (no binder), alumina (aluminium oxide), kieselguhr, cellulose powder, polyamide, reversed-phase (RP-18) silica, Sephadex.

Q. What is a herbal dossier?
Brief · 2 marks · bank Q97 · 3 min

A herbal dossier is the complete, organised set of documents on a herbal product — administrative data, quality (botanical identity, specifications, manufacture, markers, stability), non-clinical and clinical/traditional-use data — usually in CTD format, submitted to a regulatory authority for registration or marketing authorisation.

Q. What is Schedule T? / What is Schedule E(1)? Predicted
Brief · 2 marks · 3 min

Schedule T (Drugs Rules, 1945; notified 2000) prescribes GMP — factory premises, hygiene, machinery, records and QC — for manufacture of Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani medicines; compliance is a licence condition. Schedule E(1) lists poisonous substances of plant, animal and mineral origin used in ASU medicines (e.g. Vatsanabha, Kuchala, Dhattura, Parada, Gauripashana); such medicines carry "Caution: To be taken under medical supervision".
