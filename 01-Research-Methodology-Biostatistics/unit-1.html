1 What is Research? Definition & Characteristics

Research = "re" + "search": searching again and again, carefully, until a reliable answer appears.

🎬 Story mode — Judy's first real case

Mrs. Otterton bursts into the ZPD: her husband is missing. Everyone shrugs. Judy doesn't. She notices a question nobody has answered (where are the missing mammals?), reads the old case files, forms a hunch (they went "savage"), plans how to check it, collects evidence (traffic-camera footage, Manchas' story), analyses it, and finally reports to Chief Bogo with proof. A random guess would have been rumour. A systematic, evidence-based, checkable hunt is research.

📘 Exam definition (write this)

Research is a systematic, organised and objective search for new knowledge (or for verifying existing knowledge) about a specific problem, carried out by collecting, analysing and interpreting data using scientific methods.

Classic definitions to quote: Redman & Mory — research is a "systematized effort to gain new knowledge". Clifford Woody — research comprises defining and redefining problems, formulating hypotheses, collecting, organising and evaluating data, making deductions and reaching conclusions, and finally testing those conclusions to see whether they fit the hypothesis. (Both quoted in Kothari.)

Objectives of research (why we do it)

🔦 Exploratory / formulative

To gain familiarity with a phenomenon or new insight into it.

📝 Descriptive

To portray accurately the characteristics of an individual, situation or group.

🩺 Diagnostic

To determine how frequently something occurs or is associated with something else.

🧪 Hypothesis-testing (causal)

To test a hypothesis of a causal relationship between variables.

Characteristics of research

🎛️

Controlled

Other factors that could affect the result are kept constant.
🧠

Logical

Guided by rules of reasoning (induction, deduction).
⚖️

Unbiased (objective)

Free from personal feelings and prejudice.
👁️

Empirical

Based on real observation / data, not opinion.
🪜

Systematic

Follows ordered, planned steps.
✨

Original

Adds new knowledge or a new angle.
🔁

Replicable

Others can repeat it and get similar results.
🧾

Testable / verifiable

Conclusions can be checked with evidence.

Also: valid (measures what it should), reliable (consistent), generalisable (results apply beyond the sample), critical (procedures scrutinised), cyclical (ends with new questions), and it needs patience and accurate documentation.

🧠 Mnemonic — every detective looks for a CLUE, then SORTs it
ControlledLogicalUnbiasedEmpiricalSystematicOriginalReplicableTestable

“Judy finds a CLUE, then she SORTs it.” Eight characteristics, eight letters.

💊 Pharmacy example

A student asks "Does adding 2% croscarmellose sodium reduce the disintegration time of paracetamol tablets?" She keeps compression force, granule size and moisture constant (controlled), measures disintegration time in a USP apparatus (empirical, objective), follows a written plan (systematic) and records enough detail for a classmate to repeat it (replicable). That small project is genuine research.

⭐ Exam tip

For any 10-mark answer that starts with "Define research…": write one definition in a box, add one classic definition with the author's name (Redman & Mory or Clifford Woody), then list 6–8 characteristics as bullet points with one line each. Examiners look for the words systematic, objective, empirical, replicable.

2 Types of Research

Like ZPD cases, research can be sorted by its purpose, its approach, its data, its time frame and where it happens.

🎬 Story mode — the ZPD case board

Clawhauser sorts the files on the front desk: some cases just describe what happened (descriptive), some explain why using existing evidence (analytical), some help catch a real thief today (applied) while Judy's police-academy textbook explains general rules of crime (fundamental). Counting how many mammals vanished is quantitative; interviewing Mrs. Otterton about her feelings is qualitative.

The four classic pairs (Kothari)

PairType AType B
1. Descriptive vs AnalyticalDescriptive (ex post facto): describes the state of affairs as it exists now; the researcher has no control over variables, only reports what is happening. E.g. survey of OTC analgesic use among college students.Analytical: uses facts/information already available and analyses them to make a critical evaluation. E.g. analysing 10 years of ADR reports to find which drug class causes most skin reactions.
2. Applied vs FundamentalApplied (action): aims to find a solution for an immediate practical problem of society or industry. E.g. developing a taste-masked paediatric syrup.Fundamental (basic / pure): aims at generalisation and formulation of theory, "knowledge for knowledge's sake". E.g. studying how a receptor changes shape when a ligand binds.
3. Quantitative vs QualitativeQuantitative: based on measurement of quantity/amount; expressed in numbers; statistical analysis. E.g. % reduction in HbA1c after a drug.Qualitative: concerned with qualitative phenomena — reasons, attitudes, motivations (e.g. motivation research, depth interviews). E.g. why patients stop taking antihypertensives.
4. Conceptual vs EmpiricalConceptual: related to abstract ideas or theory; used by philosophers and thinkers to develop new concepts or reinterpret existing ones.Empirical (experimental): data-based; relies on experience or observation alone; conclusions can be verified by observation or experiment. E.g. a clinical trial.

Some other types

⏳ One-time vs Longitudinal

Confined to a single time period vs carried on over several periods (e.g. following a cohort of smokers for 10 years).

🏞️ Field / Lab / Simulation

Depends on the environment where research is done; simulation uses an artificial model (e.g. computer PK simulation).

🩺 Clinical / Diagnostic

Follows case-study methods or in-depth approaches to find basic causal relations (e.g. clinical trials, diagnostic test evaluation).

🔦 Exploratory vs Formalised

Exploratory develops hypotheses (no fixed design); formalised (hypothesis-testing) has a fixed structure and tests specific hypotheses.

📜 Historical

Uses historical sources (documents, remains) to study past events or ideas, e.g. history of the Indian Pharmacopoeia.

🎯 Conclusion- vs Decision-oriented

Researcher free to pick problem and redesign (conclusion) vs research done for a decision-maker's need (decision), e.g. operations research.

🔬 Basic (fundamental) research

  • Goal: new knowledge / theory
  • No immediate commercial use
  • Usually universities, research institutes
  • E.g. mechanism of insulin receptor signalling
VS

🏭 Applied research

  • Goal: solve a practical problem
  • Immediate application
  • Usually industry, hospitals, government
  • E.g. developing a once-weekly insulin formulation
🧠 Mnemonic — the four pairs
D/AA/FQ/QC/E

“Detectives Ask, Question, Confirm” → Descriptive/Analytical, Applied/Fundamental, Quantitative/Qualitative, Conceptual/Empirical.

⭐ Exam tip

"Enumerate types, explain any two" (5 marks): list all 4 pairs + 6 others in bullet form (1 mark), then explain Applied vs Fundamental and Quantitative vs Qualitative with a pharmacy example each — these are the easiest to write well. Finish with the tree diagram.

3 Criteria of Good Research

How Chief Bogo decides whether a case report is good enough to put a suspect behind bars.

🎬 Story mode — Bogo's checklist

Chief Bogo never accepts "I have a feeling, sir." He wants: a clearly stated purpose, every step written so another officer could repeat it, an unbiased plan, honest admission of what went wrong, proper analysis of the evidence, conclusions that stay inside what the evidence shows — and an officer known for integrity. Those are exactly Kothari's criteria of good research.

📘 Exam definition

Good research is research that uses well-defined purpose, scientific method, objective procedures, adequate analysis and honest reporting, so that its conclusions are reliable, verifiable and useful.

The criteria (Kothari)

1. 🎯 Purpose clearly defined

The purpose of research and the common concepts used should be clearly defined.

2. 🔁 Procedure described in detail

Research procedure described in sufficient detail to permit another researcher to repeat it for further advancement.

3. ⚖️ Objective design

Procedural design carefully planned to yield results that are as objective as possible.

4. 🗣️ Candid about flaws

Researcher reports with complete frankness the flaws in the design and estimates their effects on findings.

5. 📊 Adequate analysis

Analysis of data adequate to reveal its significance; methods of analysis appropriate; validity and reliability checked.

6. 🧾 Data-justified conclusions

Conclusions confined to those justified by the data and limited to what the data provide an adequate basis for.

7. 🏅 Experienced, ethical researcher

Greater confidence if the researcher is experienced, has a good reputation and is a person of integrity.

➕ Qualities of good research

Systematic · Logical · Empirical · Replicable (Kothari's four qualities). Also ethical, valid and reliable.
🧠 Mnemonic — a PRO-CADE officer
PurposeRepeatable procedureObjective designCandid flawsAdequate analysisData-justified conclusionExperienced & ethical

“Bogo only promotes a PRO-CADEt.” (PRO + CADEt — a professional cadet!)

💊 Pharmacy example

A paper claims "Herbal Drug X cures diabetes" based on 12 patients, no control group, no blinding, and does not mention that 4 patients dropped out. It fails criteria 3 (not objective), 4 (flaws hidden), 5 (inadequate analysis) and 6 (conclusion goes beyond data). Good research would use a randomised controlled design and report every dropout.

⭐ Exam tip

For 2 marks list any four criteria in one line each. For 5 marks write all seven with one-line explanations + "Qualities: systematic, logical, empirical, replicable" + a two-line conclusion.

4 The Research Process

The 11 steps of every research project — memorise this order and you can write a 10-mark essay anywhere.

🎬 Story mode — the missing-mammals case, step by step

Judy's whole investigation is the research process. Each step below has its Zootopia twin in the table. Notice the feedback loop: when Judy's first theory ("the mammals simply ran away") didn't fit the evidence, she went back and redefined the problem. Real research loops back too.

📘 Exam definition

The research process is the series of closely related, overlapping actions or steps necessary to carry out research effectively, and the desired sequencing of these steps — from formulating the problem to writing the report.

The 11 steps (with Judy's case)

StepWhat happens (exam points)🐰 Judy's case
1. Formulating the research problemSelect a broad area → narrow to a specific, researchable problem; understand it, discuss with guide/colleagues, rephrase into an operational problem. Two types of problems: about states of nature and about relationships between variables."Where is Mr. Otterton, and why are predators vanishing?"
2. Extensive literature surveyStudy journals, books, theses, databases, earlier reports to know what is already known and to locate the gap.Reading old case files and the missing-mammal reports.
3. Development of working hypothesisA tentative assumption made to draw out and test its logical consequences; it focuses and limits the research. Developed from discussions, literature, pilot data."The missing predators have all gone savage."
4. Preparing the research designConceptual structure: how, where, when data will be collected — exploratory, descriptive, experimental design; means of obtaining information; time and cost.Plan: check traffic cameras, interview witnesses, visit Cliffside.
5. Determining sample designPopulation/universe, sampling unit, sampling frame, sample size, sampling technique (probability or non-probability).Which cameras? Which witnesses? Which of the missing mammals?
6. Collecting the dataPrimary data by experiment, observation, interview, questionnaire, schedule; or secondary data.Footage, Manchas' statement, Mr. Big's records.
7. Execution of the projectCarry out the plan systematically and on time; supervise field work; ensure data quality.The actual chase through Tundratown and the Rainforest District.
8. Analysis of dataEditing, coding, tabulation, statistical analysis (descriptive and inferential).Connecting clues: all victims were predators, all exposed to "Night Howlers".

11. Preparation of the report / thesisPreliminary pages (title, acknowledgements, contents) → main text (introduction, results, discussion, summary) → end matter (references, appendices).Reporting to Chief Bogo and the press — with evidence.

💡 Spotlight: Hypothesis (step 3) — asked twice as a 2-marker

📘 Definition

A hypothesis is a tentative, testable statement or prediction about the relationship between two or more variables, formulated before the study and accepted or rejected on the basis of the data. (Kothari: a proposition or set of propositions set forth as an explanation for some phenomenon, either as a provisional conjecture to guide investigation or accepted as highly probable in light of established facts.)

Characteristics of a good hypothesis

  • Clear and precise (no vague words)
  • Capable of being tested (empirically verifiable)
  • States the relationship between variables
  • Limited in scope and specific
  • Stated in simple, understandable terms
  • Consistent with known facts / existing theory
  • Testable within a reasonable time
  • Explains the facts it claims to explain

Types of hypothesis (with examples)

  • Null (H₀): no difference/relationship. "Drug A and placebo give the same mean fall in BP."
  • Alternative (H₁/Hₐ): a difference exists. "Drug A lowers BP more than placebo."
  • Directional (one-tailed) vs non-directional (two-tailed): "more than" vs "different from".
  • Simple (1 IV, 1 DV) vs complex (≥2 IVs or DVs): "Smoking causes lung cancer" vs "Smoking and alcohol increase risk of oral and oesophageal cancer".
  • Associative vs causal: variables change together vs one causes the other.
  • Research / working hypothesis vs statistical hypothesis.

Psst, Mimosa… the null hypothesis is like a "not guilty" verdict — we keep assuming it until the evidence is strong enough to reject it. 🐾

💊 Pharmacy example — a whole research process in one breath

Problem: high non-adherence to antihypertensives in a Kannur hospital → Literature: pharmacist counselling helped in other countries → Hypothesis: counselling improves adherence → Design: randomised controlled study, 6 months → Sample: 120 patients, randomly allocated → Data: validated adherence questionnaire + pill count + BP → Analysis/test: chi-square / t-test → Interpret & report: thesis + journal paper.

⭐ Exam tip

For the Jul 2023-type essay, budget: definition (1½ m) → types table (3 m) → process flowchart + 11 steps with 2 lines each (4½ m) → conclusion (1 m). The flowchart alone can win you 1–2 marks.

⚠️ Common mistake

Writing "data collection" before "sample design", or skipping the literature survey. Literature review always comes before the hypothesis — you cannot make a sensible hunch without knowing what is already known.

5 Review of Literature

Before Judy chases a suspect, she reads every old file. Before you start a study, you read what others already found.

🎬 Story mode — Flash, the patient reviewer

At the DMV, Flash the sloth types a licence plate… one… letter… at… a… time. Nick finds it hilarious, but Flash gets the plate number exactly right — and that single record cracks the case. A literature review is the "Flash" step of research: slow, careful, complete. Rush it and you may repeat someone else's work or miss the clue that tells you what is still unknown.

📘 Exam definition

A review of literature is a systematic, critical survey and summary of published and unpublished work (books, journal articles, theses, reports, databases) related to a research problem. It shows what is already known, how it was studied, where the findings disagree, and what is still unknown (the research gap).

Significance / purposes of literature review

1. 🎯 Defines & refines the problem

Helps select a researchable topic and narrow it down.

2. 🚫 Avoids duplication

Shows what has already been done — no "re-inventing the wheel".

3. 🕳️ Identifies the research gap

Reveals unanswered questions and justifies the need (rationale) for the study.

4. 🏛️ Theoretical framework

Provides concepts, theories and definitions on which the study is built.

5. 💡 Helps frame hypothesis & objectives

Earlier findings suggest what to predict.

6. 🧰 Guides methodology

Shows designs, sample sizes, tools and statistical tests used by others — and their limitations, so you can do better.

7. 📏 Selects valid tools

Finds already-validated questionnaires, scales and assay methods.

8. 🔍 Helps interpret results

Your findings are compared with earlier studies in the Discussion chapter.

9. 🧑‍🎓 Builds expertise

Updates knowledge, terminology and identifies key authors, journals and experts.

10. ✍️ Proper citation

Gives credit to earlier work and avoids plagiarism; places your study in context.

Types of literature review

TypeKey featurePharmacy example
Narrative (traditional) reviewBroad summary and critique of a topic by an expert; no fixed search method → may be biased."Recent advances in transdermal drug delivery"
Systematic reviewFocused question (PICO); explicit, reproducible search of several databases; predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria; quality appraisal; reported using PRISMA.Cochrane review on statins for primary prevention
Meta-analysisStatistical pooling of results from comparable studies to give one summary effect (shown as a forest plot). Usually done inside a systematic review.Pooled odds ratio of GI bleeding with NSAIDs
Scoping reviewMaps the size and nature of the literature on a broad topic and identifies gaps; quality appraisal usually not done."What is known about pharmacist roles in tele-pharmacy?"
Integrative reviewCombines experimental and non-experimental (incl. qualitative) studies for a fuller picture.Patient experiences + outcomes of insulin pens
Critical reviewExtensive search plus deep critical evaluation of quality and contribution of each study.Critique of evidence for herbal hepatoprotectives
Umbrella reviewA review of existing systematic reviews / meta-analyses.Review of all reviews on probiotics
Rapid reviewSimplified, time-limited systematic review for urgent decisions.Early COVID-19 drug evidence summaries

By organisation of writing, reviews may also be chronological (by time), thematic (by theme), methodological (by method) or theoretical.

Sources of literature (Dec 2023 2-marker)

🥇 Primary sources

Original research articles in journals, theses & dissertations (e.g. Shodhganga), conference proceedings, patents, clinical-trial registries (CTRI, ClinicalTrials.gov), official reports.

🥈 Secondary sources

Review articles, textbooks, reference books, encyclopaedias, handbooks, pharmacopoeias (IP, BP, USP), Martindale, drug compendia.

🥉 Tertiary / search tools

Indexing & abstracting databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, SciFinder (Chemical Abstracts), DOAJ, INFLIBNET; library catalogues, bibliographies.

Plus grey literature (unpublished reports, WHO/government documents, company reports, theses) and personal communication with experts.

Steps in doing a literature review

🎯Define topicfocused question (PICO)
🔑Keywordssynonyms, MeSH terms
🗄️Search databasesBoolean AND / OR / NOT, filters
🧹Screen & selecttitle → abstract → full text
🧐Critically appraisequality, bias

💊 Pharmacy example

Planning a study on "self-medication with antibiotics among students"? Search PubMed with ("self-medication" OR "self medication") AND antibiotic* AND student*, add filters (last 10 years, India), screen results, record each study's design, sample size, questionnaire used and prevalence found in a review matrix. You will soon see that most studies are urban — a population gap for rural Kerala.

⭐ Exam tip

Nov 2022 essay = significance (5 marks: 10 points) + types (5 marks: 6–8 types with one-line explanation). Add the funnel diagram and the sources list as a bonus. Use the words systematic, critical, gap, duplication, framework.

⚠️ Common mistake

Confusing systematic review with meta-analysis. A systematic review collects and appraises studies using an explicit method; a meta-analysis is the statistical pooling of their numbers. Every meta-analysis should come from a systematic review, but not every systematic review has a meta-analysis.

6 Research Gap

The empty slot on Judy's case board — the one question nobody has answered yet.

🎬 Story mode — the missing piece

Judy's case board is full: 14 missing predators, photos, maps, witness statements. But one slot stays empty — what made them go savage? Everything else is known; that single unanswered question is the gap. When Judy fills it (Night Howler toxin), the whole picture makes sense. Your thesis should do the same: fill one clearly identified gap.

📘 Exam definition (2 marks — write exactly this)

A research gap is a question or problem that has not been answered, or has been answered inadequately, by existing studies — the missing, conflicting or insufficient piece of knowledge in the literature that a new study can address. It is identified through a thorough literature review and forms the rationale (justification) for the study.

Types of research gaps

TypeMeaningExample
Evidence (contradictory) gapStudies give conflicting results.Some trials show vitamin D prevents fractures; others don't.
Knowledge gapThe knowledge simply does not exist yet.Long-term safety of a newly approved drug.
Practical-knowledge (action) gapWhat practitioners do differs from what research recommends, or practice has not been studied.Pharmacists dispensing antibiotics without prescription despite guidelines.
Methodological gapA different method/design is needed (e.g. only in vitro or only small open-label studies exist).Anti-diabetic activity of a plant shown only in rats → need clinical study.
Empirical gapTheories or claims have not been tested with real data.Traditional claim of a herb never evaluated experimentally.
Theoretical gapNo theory explains an observed phenomenon.Why some patients respond to placebo strongly.
Population gapA population is under-studied (children, elderly, pregnant women, a region).Drug dosing studies in Indian paediatric patients.

How to find a research gap

  • Read the "limitations" and "future scope / recommendations" sections of recent papers and theses.
  • Read recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses — they often state "further research is needed on…".
  • Look for conflicting findings, new populations or settings, new technologies or methods, and outdated studies.
  • Discuss with guides and experts; attend conferences; check trial registries for unfinished work.
💊 Gap statement template

"Although pharmacist-led counselling has been shown to improve adherence in diabetic patients in urban hospitals, no study has evaluated its effect among elderly patients in rural Kannur. Hence the present study…" — the three magic phrases: Although… / no study has… / hence the present study…

Psst, Mimosa… no gap = no thesis. The gap is your study's ticket into the ethics committee room! 🎟️

7 Formulating & Defining the Research Problem

"A problem well put is half solved." First decide exactly what you are asking.

🎬 Story mode — from rumour to a real question

At first Judy only had a vague worry: "bad things are happening to predators." That is a topic, not a problem. Step by step she sharpened it: Who? (14 missing predators) What? (sudden savage behaviour) Where? (Zootopia) When? (the past few weeks) Why? (unknown!). Only then could she plan an investigation. Formulating a research problem is exactly this sharpening.

📘 Exam definition

A research problem is a difficulty, question or gap in knowledge that a researcher experiences in a theoretical or practical situation and wants to find a solution for through systematic investigation. Formulating means selecting and stating it clearly; defining means stating it precisely with its terms, assumptions, scope and objectives.

Components of a research problem (Kothari)

  1. An individual or group who has the difficulty or problem.
  2. Some objective(s) to be attained.
  3. Alternative means (courses of action) for obtaining the objective.
  4. Some doubt in the mind of the researcher about which alternative to select.
  5. Some environment(s) to which the difficulty pertains.

Sources of research problems

🏥 Clinical / professional experience
📚 Literature — "future research" suggestions
🧠 Theories needing testing
🏭 Industry & societal needs
🏛️ National priorities (ICMR, DBT, WHO)
🗣️ Discussions with experts, conferences
🔁 Replication of earlier studies
🆕 New technology / new drugs

Criteria for selecting a research problem

🧠 FINER criteria (Hulley) — a FINER detective picks FINER cases
FeasibleInterestingNovelEthicalRelevant

Feasible: enough subjects, expertise, time, money, manageable scope. Interesting to the researcher. Novel: confirms, refutes or extends earlier findings. Ethical: approvable by the ethics committee. Relevant: to science, clinical practice, health policy.

Kothari also advises: avoid over-done subjects; avoid controversial subjects (for an average researcher); avoid problems that are too narrow or too vague; choose a subject that is familiar and feasible with available data, cooperation, cost and time; consider its importance and the researcher's qualifications.

Steps in formulating the problem (Kothari)

🗒️State the problem in a general way
🧩Understand the nature of the problem
📚Survey the available literature
🗣️Develop ideas through discussions
🎯Rephrase into a working proposition

Defining the problem — what must be stated

  • Technical terms and words clearly defined (operational definitions — e.g. "adherence = taking ≥ 80% of prescribed doses by pill count").
  • Basic assumptions or postulates stated.
  • Criteria for selection of the problem and its value / rationale (the gap).
  • Time period and sources of data considered suitable.
  • Scope / delimitations of the investigation.
  • Objectives — general and specific, written SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound), and variables identified.

Framing a clinical question: PICO(T)

LetterStands forExample
PPopulation / PatientAdults with type 2 diabetes in a Kannur hospital
IIntervention / exposurePharmacist-led counselling
CComparisonUsual care
OOutcomeMedication adherence and HbA1c
TTime6 months

"Antibiotic misuse among youth."

"Prevalence and predictors of self-medication with antibiotics among undergraduate students in Kannur district, 2026: a cross-sectional questionnaire study."

8 Research Methods v/s Research Methodology

Asked three times (short + brief + model). Learn the table — it is free marks.

🎬 Story mode — the carrot pen

📘 Definitions (Kothari)

Research methods are all the methods or techniques used for conducting research — (1) methods of collecting data, (2) statistical techniques for establishing relationships between data and unknowns, and (3) methods for evaluating the accuracy of results.

Research methodology is a way to systematically solve the research problem — the science of studying how research is done scientifically. It includes the methods and the logic behind them: why a particular method is used and why others are not, so that results can be evaluated by the researcher or others.

9 Format for Research Protocol

Asked in May 2022, July 2023 AND the model paper. This is the single most "repeat" question of Unit 1.

🎬 Story mode — the operation plan

Before any big raid, Chief Bogo demands a written operation plan: why we're doing it, what we already know, the exact objective, who goes where, what equipment, how evidence will be handled, what could go wrong, how long, how much it costs. No plan, no raid. A research protocol is that plan — and the Ethics Committee is Bogo: no protocol, no approval.

📘 Exam definition

A research protocol is a written document (the "blueprint" of a study) that describes in detail the background, rationale, objectives, design, methodology, statistical plan, ethical considerations and organisation of a research project. It is prepared before the study starts and submitted to the guide, Institutional Ethics Committee, funding agency and (for clinical trials) the regulator and trial registry.

Why a protocol is important

  • Acts as a blueprint — every investigator follows the same procedure (uniformity, reproducibility).
  • Mandatory for ethics committee approval, funding, and CTRI registration of trials.
  • Forces clear thinking about objectives, sample size and analysis before data collection (prevents bias and "fishing").
  • Helps plan time and budget; basis for later thesis/report writing; any change must be made as a documented amendment.

10 Research Ethics & Importance of Institutional Review Boards

Science without ethics is just Bellwether with a dart gun.

🎬 Story mode — Cliffside Asylum

Remember what Mayor Lionheart did? He secretly locked the savage predators in Cliffside Asylum and had them examined without telling their families, without consent, hidden from the public. And Bellwether darted innocent mammals with Night Howler serum — harming unconsenting subjects for her own goal. Both are textbook unethical research. Real-world history had its own villains (Nazi experiments → Nuremberg Code; the Tuskegee syphilis study → Belmont Report). Ethics rules and Ethics Committees exist so that this never happens to real patients. In our bureau, Chief Bogo is the IRB: no operation without his signed approval, and he keeps checking until it's over.

📘 Exam definitions

Research ethics are the moral principles and professional standards that guide the planning, conduct and reporting of research, so that the dignity, rights, safety and well-being of participants (human or animal) are protected and the integrity of science is maintained.

Institutional Review Board (IRB) / Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) is an independent, multidisciplinary body constituted by an institution to review, approve and monitor research involving human participants, in order to protect their rights, safety and well-being. (Called IRB in the USA, IEC/EC in India, Research Ethics Committee in UK/EU.)

🙋

Respect for persons (autonomy)

Informed, voluntary consent; protect those with reduced autonomy.
💚

The Belmont Report gave the first three (respect for persons, beneficence, justice). The ICMR 2017 guidelines expand the four basic principles into 12 general principles: essentiality · voluntariness · non-exploitation · social responsibility · privacy & confidentiality · risk minimisation · professional competence · maximisation of benefit · institutional arrangements · transparency & accountability · totality of responsibility · environmental protection.

Informed consent — the heart of ethics

Informed consent is the voluntary agreement of a competent person to participate in research after being given, in a language they understand, adequate information about the study. Documented with a Participant Information Sheet (PIS) + Informed Consent Form (ICF); for minors or incapable persons, consent from a legally acceptable representative (plus assent of children where possible).

Unethical practices in research (research misconduct)

🧪 Fabrication

Making up data or results that were never obtained.

✂️ Falsification

Manipulating materials, equipment, processes, data or images so the record is not accurate (e.g. deleting "bad" values).

📋 Plagiarism

Using another person's words, ideas, data or work without proper credit.

📑 Duplicate publication & salami slicing

Publishing the same work twice, or splitting one study into many small papers.

👻 Gift / ghost authorship

Adding people who did not contribute, or hiding those who did.

💰 Undisclosed conflict of interest

Hiding funding or personal gain that could bias results.

🚫 No IEC approval / no consent

Coercing or misleading participants; enrolling without valid consent.

🔓 Breach of confidentiality

Revealing identities or medical data of participants.

🎣 Selective reporting / p-hacking

Reporting only favourable outcomes; hiding negative results; misuse of funds.

Plagiarism (Dec 2023 brief): presenting someone else's work, words, ideas or data as one's own without acknowledgement. Types: verbatim (copy-paste), mosaic/patch-writing, paraphrasing without citation, self-plagiarism, accidental. In India the UGC (Promotion of Academic Integrity and Prevention of Plagiarism in Higher Educational Institutions) Regulations, 2018 grade similarity as Level 0 (up to 10% — minor), Level 1 (10–40%), Level 2 (40–60%) and Level 3 (above 60%). Detected by software such as Turnitin, iThenticate and DrillBit.

IRB / IEC — composition (India: NDCT Rules 2019, Rule 7)

Clinical-trial ethics committees must be registered with the Central Licensing Authority (CDSCO); ethics committees for other biomedical and health research register with the Department of Health Research. The US IRB rules (21 CFR 56) require at least five members.

Functions of the IRB / IEC

  • Review the protocol for scientific validity and ethical acceptability (risk–benefit balance).
  • Review informed consent documents (PIS, ICF) in local languages.
  • Ensure fair selection of participants and extra protection for vulnerable groups (children, pregnant women, prisoners, cognitively impaired).
  • Check investigator qualifications and site facilities.
  • Decide: approve, ask for modifications, reject, or suspend/terminate a study.
  • Continuing review: progress reports, amendments, protocol deviations.
  • Review serious adverse events and decide on compensation for research-related injury or death.
  • Protect privacy & confidentiality; review advertisements and payments; keep records and SOPs.

Types of review (ICMR 2017): Exemption from review (less than minimal risk, e.g. anonymised published data) · Expedited review (minimal risk) · Full committee review (more than minimal risk, e.g. clinical trials).

Importance of IRBs

  • Protects participants — rights, safety, dignity, well-being (the primary purpose).
  • Ensures scientific quality — poorly designed research is itself unethical (wastes participants' risk).
  • Legal requirement: no clinical trial in India can start without EC approval (and CDSCO permission); journals require EC approval for publication.
  • Provides independent oversight, prevents misconduct and conflicts of interest.
  • Ensures compensation and medical management for research-related injury.
  • Builds public trust in research and protects the institution's credibility.
🐭 Animal research

Animal experiments need approval of the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) under CPCSEA. Follow the 3 Rs: Replacement (use non-animal methods where possible), Reduction (fewest animals), Refinement (minimise pain and distress). 🆕 Update: CPCSEA was renamed CCSEA (Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals) in January 2023 — same functions.

Saying the IRB "conducts" the research or "funds" it. The IRB only reviews, approves and monitors; it must be independent of the investigators and sponsor.

1. Definition

Research is a systematic, organised and objective search for new knowledge, or for verifying existing knowledge, about a specific problem, by collecting, analysing and interpreting data using scientific methods. Redman and Mory define it as a "systematized effort to gain new knowledge". According to Clifford Woody, research comprises defining and redefining problems, formulating hypotheses, collecting, organising and evaluating data, making deductions and reaching conclusions, and testing whether the conclusions fit the hypothesis.

Characteristics: systematic, logical, empirical, objective (unbiased), controlled, replicable, original, valid and verifiable.

2. Types of research

  1. Descriptive vs Analytical — Descriptive research (ex post facto) describes the state of affairs as it exists; the researcher has no control over variables (e.g. survey of self-medication among students). Analytical research uses facts already available and analyses them critically (e.g. analysis of ADR reports).
  2. Applied vs Fundamental — Applied (action) research solves an immediate practical problem (e.g. developing a taste-masked paediatric suspension). Fundamental (basic/pure) research aims at generalisations and theory (e.g. mechanism of receptor activation).
  3. Quantitative vs Qualitative — Quantitative research measures quantity and uses statistics (e.g. % fall in blood glucose). Qualitative research studies reasons, attitudes and behaviour (e.g. why patients stop medicines — depth interviews).
  4. Conceptual vs Empirical — Conceptual research deals with abstract ideas/theories. Empirical research is data-based and verified by observation or experiment (e.g. clinical trials).
  5. Other types — one-time vs longitudinal; field-setting, laboratory and simulation research; clinical or diagnostic research; exploratory vs formalised (hypothesis-testing) research; historical research; conclusion-oriented vs decision-oriented research (operations research).

[Draw Fig 1.1 — the types-of-research tree — here.]

3. Research process

The research process is the series of closely related steps necessary to carry out research effectively, in the desired sequence. [Draw Fig 1.2 — flow chart I → VII with feedback loops.]

  1. Formulating the research problem — select a broad area, narrow it to a specific researchable problem, understand it and rephrase it in operational terms.
  2. Extensive literature survey — review journals, books, theses and databases to know what is known and find the research gap.
  3. Development of working hypothesis — a tentative, testable assumption that focuses the study.
  4. Preparing the research design — the conceptual structure: exploratory, descriptive or experimental design; how data will be obtained; time and cost.
  5. Determining sample design — population, sampling frame, sample size and sampling technique (probability or non-probability).
  6. Collecting the data — primary data by experiment, observation, interview, questionnaire or schedule; or secondary data.
  7. Execution of the project — carrying out the plan systematically and in time, with quality checks.
  8. Analysis of data — editing, coding, tabulation and statistical analysis.
  9. Hypothesis testing — applying tests such as t-test, chi-square or F-test to accept or reject the hypothesis.
  10. Generalisation and interpretation — building generalisations/theory from repeatedly upheld hypotheses, or explaining findings in light of existing theory.
  11. Preparation of report / thesis — preliminary pages, main text (introduction, results, discussion, conclusion) and end matter (references, appendices).

Conclusion

Research is the backbone of scientific progress. Choosing the right type of research and following the research process in sequence — with feedback when results don't fit — makes the findings valid, reliable and useful, as in drug discovery and clinical pharmacy practice.

Q. Discuss the significance of literature review. Elaborate the types of literature review.
Essay · 10 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 (significance also Short 5m Dec 2023) · ~25 min · ~3 pages
Definition (1 m) → Significance, 10 points (4 m) → Types, 8 with examples (4 m) → Funnel diagram + sources (bonus) → Conclusion (1 m)

1. Introduction & definition

A review of literature is a systematic and critical survey and summary of published and unpublished work related to a research problem. It shows what is already known, how it was studied, where findings conflict and what remains unknown. It is the second step of the research process, done before formulating the hypothesis.

2. Significance of literature review

  1. Defines and refines the problem — helps choose a researchable topic and narrow it.
  2. Avoids duplication — prevents repeating work already done.
  3. Identifies the research gap — reveals unanswered questions and justifies the study.
  4. Provides theoretical/conceptual framework — concepts, definitions and theories.
  5. Helps formulate hypotheses and objectives.
  6. Guides methodology — designs, sample sizes, tools and statistical tests used by others, and their limitations.
  7. Helps select validated instruments — questionnaires, scales, analytical methods.
  8. Helps interpret results — findings are compared with earlier studies in the discussion.
  9. Builds the researcher's expertise — updates knowledge; identifies key authors, journals and experts.
  10. Ensures proper citation — gives credit to earlier work and avoids plagiarism.

3. Types of literature review

TypeDescription
Narrative (traditional)Broad, descriptive summary and critique by an expert; no fixed search method; may be biased.
Systematic reviewFocused question (PICO); explicit, reproducible search of multiple databases; predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria; quality appraisal; reported by PRISMA (e.g. Cochrane reviews).
Meta-analysisStatistical combination of results of comparable studies into one pooled estimate (forest plot).
Scoping reviewMaps the extent and nature of literature on a broad topic; identifies gaps.
Integrative reviewCombines experimental, non-experimental and qualitative studies.

Based on how the review is organised, it may be chronological, thematic, methodological or theoretical.

4. Sources and steps (brief)

Sources: primary (research articles, theses, patents, conference proceedings), secondary (reviews, textbooks, pharmacopoeias), tertiary (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar). Steps: define topic → keywords → search → screen → appraise → organise → synthesise → write and cite. [Draw Fig 1.3 — literature funnel.]

Conclusion

A good literature review is the foundation of any research. It saves time and money, prevents duplication, sharpens the problem and reveals the gap that a new study must fill.

Types of research (enumeration)

  • Descriptive vs Analytical

  • Quantitative vs Qualitative

1. Applied vs Fundamental research

Applied (action) research aims to find a solution to an immediate practical problem facing society or industry. Results are used directly. E.g. developing a sustained-release metformin tablet to reduce GI side effects.

Fundamental (basic/pure) research is concerned with generalisations and formulation of theory — knowledge for its own sake, without immediate application. E.g. studying how β-lactams bind penicillin-binding proteins.

Quantitative research is based on measurement of quantity or amount; data are numerical and analysed statistically. E.g. comparing mean HbA1c between two treatment groups.

Qualitative research deals with qualitative phenomena such as reasons, attitudes, beliefs and motivations, using depth interviews, focus groups and observation. E.g. exploring why elderly patients skip antihypertensive doses.

Q. What are the criteria for good research? / Mention the criteria of a good research.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Dec 2023 · Brief 2 marks · Model paper
Intro (½ m) → 7 Kothari criteria (3½ m) → four qualities (1 m). For 2 marks: write any four criteria in one line each.

Good research follows the scientific method and its findings are trustworthy. The criteria of good research (Kothari) are:

  1. Clearly defined purpose — the purpose of research and common concepts should be clearly defined.
  2. Procedure described in detail — so that another researcher can repeat it.
  3. Carefully planned, objective design — to yield results as objective as possible.
  4. Frank reporting of flaws — the researcher reports the flaws in procedural design and estimates their effects.
  5. Adequate analysis — appropriate methods of analysis that reveal the significance of data; validity and reliability checked.
  6. Conclusions justified by data — conclusions confined to what the data adequately support.
  7. Experienced researcher of integrity — greater confidence when the researcher is experienced and of good reputation.

Qualities of good research: systematic, logical, empirical and replicable; also ethical, valid and reliable.

  10. Regulatory science and industry — BA/BE studies for generics, pharmacopoeial standards, patents, process optimisation and economic growth.
  11. Public health and career — disease burden and AMR studies; evidence-based practice and professional growth.

Conclusion: research converts scientific ideas into safe, effective, affordable medicines and improves patient care.

Q. Explain research ethics. Discuss the composition, functions and importance of Institutional Review Boards (Ethics Committees). Predicted
Essay · 10 marks · syllabus line never asked yet — high chance · ~25 min
Definition + milestones (1½ m) → Principles + informed consent (2 m) → Unethical practices (1½ m) → IRB definition + composition wheel (2 m) → Functions + types of review (2 m) → Importance + conclusion (1 m)

1. Research ethics

Research ethics are the moral principles and professional standards that guide planning, conduct and reporting of research, protecting the dignity, rights, safety and well-being of participants and the integrity of science. Milestones: Nuremberg Code (1947), Declaration of Helsinki (1964; latest revision 2024), Belmont Report (1979), ICH-GCP E6, ICMR National Ethical Guidelines (2017) and New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules (2019) in India.

2. Ethical principles

  • Respect for persons (autonomy) — informed, voluntary consent.
  • Beneficence — maximise benefits.
  • Non-maleficence — minimise harm.
  • Justice — fair selection and fair distribution of burdens and benefits.

ICMR 2017 expands these into 12 general principles (essentiality, voluntariness, non-exploitation, social responsibility, privacy and confidentiality, risk minimisation, professional competence, maximisation of benefit, institutional arrangements, transparency and accountability, totality of responsibility, environmental protection).

Informed consent: voluntary agreement after full information on purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, alternatives, confidentiality, compensation and the right to withdraw, documented with PIS and ICF.

3. Unethical practices

Fabrication, falsification, plagiarism, duplicate publication, gift/ghost authorship, undisclosed conflict of interest, research without consent or EC approval, breach of confidentiality, selective reporting.

4. Institutional Review Board / Ethics Committee

An independent, multidisciplinary body constituted by an institution to review, approve and monitor research on human participants to protect their rights, safety and well-being.

Composition (NDCT Rules 2019): minimum 7 members; at least 50% not affiliated with the institution; chairperson from outside; member secretary from the institution; includes a medical scientist (preferably pharmacologist), clinician, legal expert, social scientist/ethicist/theologian, and lay person; at least one woman member. Quorum: at least 5 members representing these categories. [Draw Fig 1.7 — composition wheel.]

5. Functions

  1. Review protocols for scientific and ethical soundness, risk–benefit.
  2. Review informed consent documents.
  3. Protect vulnerable groups; ensure fair selection.
  4. Approve, modify, reject, suspend or terminate studies.
  5. Continuing review — progress reports, amendments, deviations.
  6. Review SAEs and decide compensation.
  7. Ensure confidentiality; maintain records and SOPs.

Types of review: exempt, expedited, full committee review.

6. Importance

Protects participants; ensures scientific quality; legal requirement for trials and publication; independent oversight against misconduct; ensures compensation; builds public trust.

Conclusion

Ethics committees are the guardians of research participants. Good science must also be ethical science.

Q. How do you formulate and define a research problem? Predicted
Short note · 5 marks · syllabus line · ~12 min
Definition (½ m) → sources + FINER (1½ m) → 5 steps (1½ m) → defining: terms, assumptions, scope, objectives + example (1½ m)

A research problem is a difficulty or gap in knowledge that a researcher wants to solve through systematic investigation.

Sources: clinical experience, literature (future-research suggestions), theories, industry and societal needs, national priorities, discussions, replication.

Selection (FINER): Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, Relevant. Avoid over-done, controversial, too narrow or too vague topics.

Steps in formulation (Kothari): (1) state the problem in a general way; (2) understand its nature; (3) survey the literature; (4) develop ideas through discussions; (5) rephrase it into a working proposition.

Defining the problem: define technical terms operationally; state assumptions; state the rationale and value; specify time period, data sources and scope; write SMART objectives; frame clinical questions with PICO.

Example: "Antibiotic misuse" → "Prevalence and predictors of self-medication with antibiotics among undergraduate students of Kannur district, 2026: a cross-sectional study."

Answer-briefly questions (2 marks each)

Q. Define research gap / Explain the meaning of "Research gap".
Brief · 2 marks · May 2022, Model paper

A research gap is a question or problem that has not been answered, or has been answered inadequately, by existing studies — the missing or conflicting piece of knowledge identified through literature review. It provides the rationale for a new study. Types: evidence, knowledge, practical, methodological, empirical, theoretical and population gaps. E.g. a drug studied only in adults has a population gap for children.

Q. Write difference between research methods and methodology.
Brief · 2 marks · May 2022, Model paper
  • Methods: techniques/tools used to conduct research (questionnaire, interview, HPLC, t-test) — answer "how".
  • Methodology: the science of how research is done; includes methods plus the logic for choosing them — answers "why".
  • Methods are a part of methodology; methodology is broader and covers the whole study.
Q. Mention the criteria of a good research.
Brief · 2 marks · Model paper
  • Purpose clearly defined.
  • Procedure described in enough detail to be repeated.
  • Objective, carefully planned design; flaws frankly reported.
  • Adequate analysis; conclusions justified by data; researcher of integrity.
Q. Mention / list out any four unethical practices in research.
Brief · 2 marks · Nov 2022, July 2023
  1. Fabrication — inventing data or results.
  2. Falsification — manipulating data, images or procedures.
  3. Plagiarism — using others' work without credit.
  4. Duplicate publication / gift authorship / conducting research without informed consent or ethics committee approval.
Q. Characteristics of good hypothesis.
Brief · 2 marks · May 2022
  • Clear, precise and stated in simple terms.
  • Testable (empirically verifiable) within a reasonable time.
  • States the relationship between variables; limited and specific in scope.
  • Consistent with known facts and able to explain the facts it addresses.
Q. Define hypothesis. Write their types with suitable examples.
Brief · 2 marks · July 2023

A hypothesis is a tentative, testable statement about the relationship between two or more variables, formulated before a study and accepted or rejected on the basis of data.

  • Null (H₀): no difference — "Drug A and placebo produce the same fall in BP."
  • Alternative (H₁): a difference exists — "Drug A lowers BP more than placebo."
  • Simple vs complex; directional (one-tailed) vs non-directional (two-tailed); associative vs causal.
Q. Define plagiarism.
Brief · 2 marks · Dec 2023

Plagiarism is presenting another person's words, ideas, data or work as one's own without proper acknowledgement. Types: verbatim copying, mosaic/patch-writing, paraphrasing without citation, self-plagiarism. It is research misconduct; in India it is graded by the UGC Regulations 2018 (Level 0 up to 10% similarity; Levels 1–3 for 10–40%, 40–60%, >60%) and detected with tools like Turnitin or DrillBit.

Q. Write the different sources for literature review.
Brief · 2 marks · Dec 2023
  • Primary: original journal articles, theses/dissertations, conference proceedings, patents, trial registries.
  • Secondary: review articles, textbooks, encyclopaedias, pharmacopoeias, handbooks.
  • Tertiary/databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect.
  • Grey literature: government/WHO reports, unpublished reports; expert communication.
