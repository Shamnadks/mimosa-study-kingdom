1 Quality Assurance in the Herbal Drug Industry

QC tests the flower. QA makes sure the flower never goes bad in the first place.

🎬 Story mode

In Unit 1 the healers tested every basket at the palace gate: that is quality control. But what if the flowers were mouldy because they were dried on the ground, or mixed up in the cart? Rapunzel's smarter idea: look after the flower at every step, from seed to bottle, and write everything down. That whole system of care is quality assurance. The Disney friends each guard one step: Isabela's garden (GACP), Remy's kitchen (GMP), Rapunzel's painted journal (GLP and documentation).

📘 Exam definitions

Quality assurance (QA): the sum total of all organised arrangements made to ensure that medicinal products are of the quality required for their intended use. It includes GMP, GLP, GACP and all other factors that influence quality, from design to distribution.

Quality control (QC): the part of GMP concerned with sampling, specifications and testing, and with release procedures that ensure materials and products are not released until their quality is judged satisfactory.

GMP: the part of QA which ensures that products are consistently produced and controlled to the quality standards appropriate to their intended use and as required by the marketing authorization.

cGMP (current GMP): GMP that uses current, up-to-date technologies, systems and regulatory expectations; "current" means the manufacturer must keep upgrading premises, equipment, validation and documentation as science and regulations advance.

GAP / GACP: good agricultural (and collection) practices: guidance for cultivating, collecting, harvesting and primary processing medicinal plants so the raw material is of good quality and sustainably produced.

GLP: a quality system concerned with the organisational process and the conditions under which (non-clinical) laboratory studies and tests are planned, performed, monitored, recorded, archived and reported.

SOP: an authorized written procedure giving instructions for performing an operation (WHO GACP glossary).

Quality Assurance

  • Whole system, preventive
  • Process-oriented: "build quality in"
  • Covers design, GACP, GMP, GLP, distribution
  • Responsibility of everyone
VS

Quality Control

  • Part of GMP, detective
  • Product-oriented: "test quality"
  • Sampling, specifications, testing, release
  • Done by the QC department

Why QA is harder for herbals than for synthetics

  • Raw materials come from varied geographical and commercial sources, wild or cultivated; their history (soil, pesticides, drying) may be unknown.
  • Composition varies naturally; active constituents are often unknown, so the whole herb is the "active ingredient".
  • Risk of contamination with toxic plants, wrong plant parts, microbes, pesticides, heavy metals.
  • Production and primary processing directly decide quality, so QA must start in the field (GACP), not at the factory gate.

Traditional system of medicine (TSM)

📘 Definition (WHO)

Traditional medicine is the sum total of the knowledge, skills and practices based on the theories, beliefs and experiences indigenous to different cultures, whether explicable or not, used in the maintenance of health and in the prevention, diagnosis, improvement or treatment of physical and mental illness. In India the recognised systems are grouped as AYUSH: Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa and Homoeopathy (ASU drugs = Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani).

Scope / types of herbal drug industries

🌾

Raw material sector

Cultivation, wild collection, traders, drying & grading units.
⚗️

Extract industry

Standardized extracts, oleoresins, essential oils, isolated phytochemicals (e.g. for phytopharmaceuticals).
💊

Traditional formulations

Classical and proprietary ASU & homoeopathic medicines (churna, asava–arishta, taila, vati, bhasma).
🥤

Nutraceuticals

Herbal dietary supplements and functional foods.
🧴

Herbal cosmetics

Skin, hair and oral-care products with herbal actives.
🌍

Export & R&D

Global herbal trade, contract research, testing laboratories.
💊 Pharmacy example

An Ayurvedic company in Kerala making Dashamoola Arishta needs GACP-sourced roots of ten plants, a Schedule T-compliant fermentation section, a GLP-compliant QC lab that checks identity (TLC), alcohol content, microbial load and heavy metals, and records linking every bottle back to its batch of roots. That chain of controls is its QA system.

⚠️ Common mistake

Don't write "QC = QA". QC is only one part of GMP; GMP is one part of QA. Order of size: QA ⊃ GMP ⊃ QC.

2 GAP in the Traditional System of Medicine

Isabela makes perfect flowers bloom because she controls the seed, the soil and the timing. That is GAP.

🎬 Story mode

In Encanto, Isabela can grow any flower, but even she picks the right seed, the right spot and the right moment. A medicinal plant is the same: grow the wrong variety, in polluted soil, or harvest it after rain, and the "medicine" is weak or dangerous no matter how good the factory is. Ayurveda itself insisted on collecting herbs from clean land at the proper season; modern GAP writes that wisdom down as rules.

📘 Definition

Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) for medicinal plants are the set of guidelines for the cultivation (and, with GACP, wild collection), harvesting and primary post-harvest handling of medicinal plants that ensure raw materials of consistent good quality, free from contamination, produced in an environmentally sustainable and socially responsible way.

Objectives of GAP / GACP

  1. Contribute to the quality assurance of medicinal plant materials used as the source of herbal medicines, to improve the quality, safety and efficacy of finished products.
  2. Guide the formulation of national/regional GACP guidelines, monographs and SOPs.
  3. Encourage sustainable cultivation and collection that conserves medicinal plants and the environment.
  4. Ensure correct identity, minimise contamination (pesticides, heavy metals, microbes), and improve farmers' income and traceability.

Key elements of GAP

🌱

Identity & seed

Correct species/variety (pharmacopoeial), voucher specimen, certified disease-free seed/propagation material.
🗺️

Site & soil

Unpolluted land, suitable climate; edaphic factors: soil type, texture, pH, fertility, organic matter, moisture retention, drainage, soil microbes.
💧

Water & fertilizer

Clean irrigation water; composted manure (never human excreta); approved fertilizers in correct amounts.
🐛

Plant protection

Integrated pest management; minimum approved pesticides by trained staff; record every application; respect pre-harvest intervals.
⏰

Harvest

At the stage/season/time of day of peak active constituents; dry weather; clean tools; no soil contact.
📒

Records & people

Batch numbers, cultivation records, trained hygienic workers, traceability.

Pests and pest-control methods (GAP)

MethodExamples
Mechanical / physicalHand-picking, traps, barriers, sun-drying of produce, proper storage
Agricultural (cultural)Crop rotation, resistant varieties, clean seed, timely sowing, removal of weeds and infected plants
BiologicalNatural predators and parasites, microbial agents (e.g. Bacillus thuringiensis), botanical pesticides (neem, pyrethrum)
ChemicalApproved insecticides, fungicides, herbicides (weedicides such as 2,4-D, glyphosate, paraquat) and fumigants: minimum effective dose, documented
Integrated pest management (IPM)Careful combination of all the above, keeping pesticides at economically justified, safe levels

GAP in India (traditional medicine context)

  • The National Medicinal Plants Board (NMPB), Ministry of AYUSH, with the WHO Country Office, released India-specific GAP and Good Field Collection Practices (GFCP) guidelines for medicinal plants in 2009, adapted from WHO GACP 2003.
  • Voluntary Certification Scheme for Medicinal Plant Produce (VCSMPP): run by NMPB with the Quality Council of India (launched 2017) with two certifications: GAP for medicinal plants and GFCP.
  • Classical Ayurveda also prescribed collection rules: suitable land, season (e.g. roots in certain seasons, leaves in others), avoiding diseased, insect-eaten or polluted plants: the traditional root of GAP.

3 GMP & cGMP in the Traditional System of Medicine (Schedule T)

Remy's rule in Gusteau's kitchen: clean hands, clean station, right ingredients, same recipe every time.

🎬 Story mode

In Ratatouille, Remy won't cook until he has washed his paws, and he makes the same perfect dish every night because he follows the recipe, checks every ingredient and keeps the kitchen spotless. A herbal factory making churna, arishta or taila is a big kitchen. GMP is its rule-book; cGMP means the rule-book keeps getting updated with the latest knowledge. In India that rule-book for Ayurveda, Siddha and Unani medicines is Schedule T.

Why GMP matters for herbal/traditional medicines

  • Herbal raw materials carry microbes, dust and pests; processing (drying, milling) creates dust and cross-contamination risks.
  • Active constituents are often unknown, so the only way to get the same product every time is a reproducible, validated process.
  • Prevents mix-ups, substitution, contamination and errors; builds consumer and regulator confidence; required for licence and export.

Schedule T: GMP for Ayurvedic, Siddha & Unani medicines

Under Rule 157 of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945, ASU medicines must be manufactured in premises and hygienic conditions specified in Schedule T. It was notified on 23 June 2000 (GSR 560(E)), made mandatory for existing units from June 2002, and revised in 2003 (GSR 198(E)). It has two parts:

Part I · Good Manufacturing Practices (factory premises)

  1. Location & surroundings: away from open sewage, drains, public lavatories and factories giving off odour, fumes, soot, dust or smoke.
  2. Buildings: prevent contamination; not used as residence or passage; smooth, washable walls and floors; proper light and ventilation; insect- and rodent-proof.
  3. Water supply: pure, potable water.
  4. Disposal of waste: sanitary, as per pollution control norms.
  5. Containers' cleaning: bottle/container washing facilities.
  6. Stores: raw materials (plant, animal, mineral kept separately; quarantine/under test, approved, rejected), packing materials, finished goods.
  7. Working space: enough to avoid mix-ups and cross-contamination.
  8. Health, clothing, sanitation & hygiene of workers.
  9. Medical services: first aid, periodic medical examination.
  10. Machinery & equipment.
  11. Batch manufacturing records.
  12. Distribution records.
  13. Record of market complaints and adverse reactions.
  14. Quality control: in-house QC section or an approved testing laboratory; reference books and samples.

Part II · Machinery & minimum manufacturing requirements

Lists the recommended machinery, equipment and minimum manufacturing premises for each category of medicine, separately for Ayurvedic/Siddha and Unani systems, e.g.:

  • Churna / powders: grinders, pulverizers, sieves, mixers
  • Vati / gutika (pills, tablets): mixers, granulators, pill/tablet machines
  • Kwatha (decoctions), avaleha / paka (semisolids): boiling vessels, stirrers
  • Asava–arishta: fermentation vats, filtration
  • Taila / ghrita (medicated oils, ghee): heating vessels, filters
  • Bhasma / sindura / kupipakwa: furnaces (separate, with exhaust)
  • Arka (distillates), lepa, netra-bindu, etc.
🆕 Update note

In 2026 the Ministry of AYUSH notified the Drugs (Eleventh Amendment) Rules, 2026 (G.S.R. 657(E)), which are reported to substantially rewrite Schedule T (more detailed premises, storage, documentation, QC, training and self-inspection requirements, with a multi-year compliance window). Also, the "Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945" are now cited as the Drugs Rules, 1945 since cosmetics got separate rules in 2020. For the exam, write the classic Schedule T structure above.

Personal hygiene (GMP)

  • Pre-employment and periodic medical examination; persons with infectious or skin disease or open wounds must not handle materials.
  • Protective clothing: clean uniforms, caps/hair covers, masks, gloves, work shoes, throughout processing; protection from toxic or allergenic plants.
  • Hand washing (elbow-operated taps), no eating, drinking, chewing, smoking or personal items in production areas.
  • Written hygiene SOPs and regular training.

Documentation, validation, complaints & recalls

📄 Documentation

Specifications (raw herbs, preparations, finished products), master formula, batch manufacturing records, SOPs, processing instructions (drying temperature/time, extraction solvent/time), distribution records, complaint records. Rule: "If it is not documented, it was not done."

✅ Qualification & validation

Qualification: proving premises/equipment are installed and work correctly (DQ, IQ, OQ, PQ). Validation: documented evidence that a process consistently gives the expected result (prospective, concurrent, retrospective). Critical for herbals with unknown actives, plus formal change control.

📣 Complaints

Two types: product-quality complaints (faulty manufacture, deterioration, adulteration of herb) and adverse reaction/event reports (separate register). Investigate using retained reference samples; review regularly; inform authority if a recall follows.

↩️ Recalls

Written recall SOP as per national rules; recalled stock kept in a secure, segregated area until disposal decision; records retained.

4 GLP in the Traditional System of Medicine

Rapunzel painted every day on her tower walls. A GLP laboratory "paints" every test in its records.

🎬 Story mode

When Rapunzel finally realised she was the lost princess, the proof was all there on her walls: the sun symbol painted again and again, dated, unchanged. GLP gives laboratory results the same kind of proof: qualified people, calibrated instruments, authentic reference samples, written methods, raw data kept safely, so anyone can trace and trust each result years later.

📘 Definition

Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) is a quality system concerned with the organisational process and the conditions under which laboratory (non-clinical health and environmental safety) studies are planned, performed, monitored, recorded, archived and reported. In a broader QC sense, it is the set of practices that make laboratory test results reliable, reproducible and traceable.

Background

  • OECD Principles of GLP (1981; revised 1997) are the international basis, with mutual acceptance of data among member countries.
  • India: Schedule L-1 ("Good laboratory practices and requirements of premises and equipment") was added to the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules by GSR 780(E) of 10 November 2008 and became enforceable on 1 November 2010.
  • ASU drug-testing laboratories are approved under Rules 160A–160J (application in Form 47) to test ASU drugs and raw materials for identity, purity, quality and strength; many are also NABL-accredited (ISO/IEC 17025).

OECD fundamental points of GLP

1. Test facility organisation & personnel
2. Quality assurance programme
3. Facilities
4. Apparatus, materials & reagents
5. Test systems
6. Test & reference items
7. Standard operating procedures
8. Performance of the study (study plan)
9. Reporting of results
10. Storage & retention of records and materials

⚠️ Common mistake

GLP is about the laboratory (testing, non-clinical studies); GMP is about the factory; GCP is about clinical trials on humans. Don't mix their requirements.

5 WHO Guidelines on cGMP for Herbal Medicines

WHO's add-on rule-book: the general GMP rules plus everything special about plants.

🎬 Story mode

Remy's kitchen follows the general health code, but Gusteau's also has special rules for mushrooms, herbs and fresh produce. WHO does the same: its main GMP guide covers all medicines, and a supplementary herbal guide adds rules for dusty, bug-loving, variable plant material.

History (name these in the intro)

📄1996WHO Supplementary guidelines for the manufacture of herbal medicinal products
📗2007WHO guidelines on GMP for herbal medicines (book, revised text)
📘2018Updated text: TRS 1010, Annex 2 (52nd Expert Committee report)

They supplement WHO's Good manufacturing practices for pharmaceutical products: main principles and give minimum technical requirements for QA and control of herbal medicines; each country should develop its own national GMP. They do not cover combinations with animal or mineral materials or chemicals.

Key WHO glossary terms

TermMeaning
HerbsCrude plant material (leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds, stems, wood, bark, roots, rhizomes…), entire, fragmented or powdered
Herbal materialsHerbs plus fresh juices, gums, fixed oils, essential oils, resins, dry powders; may be locally processed (steaming, roasting, stir-baking with honey)
Herbal preparationsBasis of finished products: comminuted/powdered herbs, extracts, tinctures, fatty oils, made by extraction, fractionation, purification, concentration
Finished herbal productsHerbal preparations from one or more herbs (mixture herbal product if several); may contain excipients; not "herbal" if chemically defined actives (synthetic or isolated) are added
Active ingredientsConstituents with known therapeutic activity; if unidentified, the whole herbal medicine is the active ingredient
MarkersChemically defined constituents used for QC; may or may not contribute to activity

QC categories of herbal materials (WHO §17.3.2)

(a) Active constituents identified and quantifiable as such.
(b) Main group of active components known and quantified as a total (e.g. essential oils) or as a representative substance (e.g. flavonoids).
(c) Actives not identified/quantifiable, but marker substances are.
(d) Quantification not feasible: rely on characteristic/fingerprint chromatograms.

6 WHO Guidelines on GACP for Medicinal Plants (2003)

The farm-and-forest rule-book: Isabela's garden plus Rapunzel's careful flower-picking, all on paper.

🎬 Story mode

Rapunzel's golden flower grew wild on a cliff. Mother Gothel "harvested" it by hiding it under a basket and visiting it in secret, the opposite of good collection practice! A good collector would have taken permission, picked only what was needed so the plant could regrow, kept it off the dirt, dried it in the shade and labelled the basket. That is WHO GACP: Good Agricultural and Collection Practices.

📘 About the guideline

WHO guidelines on good agricultural and collection practices (GACP) for medicinal plants (Geneva, 2003) give general technical guidance on obtaining medicinal plant materials of good quality for the sustainable production of herbal medicines. They cover cultivation and collection, including certain post-harvest operations, and were reviewed by more than 240 reviewers in over 105 countries. Objectives: (1) contribute to QA of medicinal plant materials to improve quality, safety and efficacy of finished products; (2) guide national/regional GACP guidelines, monographs and SOPs; (3) encourage sustainable cultivation and collection that conserves plants and the environment.

2.5 PersonnelKnowledge of the plant, cultivation and harvest; hygiene training; protective clothing for agrochemicals; environmental awareness

Section 3 · Good Collection Practices (wild plants)

3.1 Permission to collect

Collection permits from authorities/landowners; respect national red lists; export permits, phytosanitary and CITES certificates where needed.

3.2 Technical planning

Map distribution and population density; study taxonomy, phenology, ethnobotany; photos and herbarium aids; transport and trained team; written responsibilities; monitor ecological and social impact.

3.3 Selection of plants

Pharmacopoeial species; voucher specimens to herbaria; record the identifying botanist.

3.4 Collection

Ensure survival of wild populations; don't collect rare species; non-destructive methods (take some lateral roots, not the taproot; bark in longitudinal strips from one side, never girdle the tree); avoid polluted sites (roadsides, drains, mines, dumps, factories) and pastures; no soil contact; separate species in separate containers.

3.5 Personnel

Local experts trained in plant science; collectors able to distinguish look-alike species; protection from toxic plants, animals and insects.

Section 4 · Common technical aspects

Sub-sectionKey points
4.1.1 Inspection & sortingVisual check for other plants/parts and foreign matter; organoleptic check (appearance, damage, size, colour, odour, possibly taste)
4.1.2 Primary processingUnload and unpack promptly; protect from rain and moisture; direct sun only if needed; fresh materials delivered fast, refrigerated or otherwise conserved; avoid preservatives (declare if used); sieve/winnow out discoloured, mouldy material, soil and stones
4.1.3 DryingMoisture as low as possible to stop moulds; methods: open air in shade, thin layers on frames, sun (if appropriate), ovens, solar dryers, indirect fire, lyophilization, microwave, infrared; shade drying keeps colour of leaves/flowers; low temperature for volatile-oil drugs; never on bare ground; direct-fire heat from butane/propane/natural gas and below 60 °C; record drying conditions

⚠️ Common mistake

GACP ≠ "GAP only". The "C" (collection) part, with permission, non-destructive harvesting and conservation, is what makes it different from food GAP. Also, the 2003 WHO GACP is for medicinal plants; the EU has its own GACP guideline for starting materials of herbal origin (Unit 3).

Q1. Describe the WHO guidelines on Good Agricultural Practices.
Short 5m · KUHS Model · ~12 min · ~1.5 pages
Intro + objectives (1m) → identification & seeds (1m) → cultivation sub-points (1½m) → harvest (1m) → personnel + one-line conclusion (½m)

Introduction: WHO GACP guidelines for medicinal plants (2003) give technical guidance for obtaining good-quality plant materials sustainably. Section 2 deals with Good Agricultural Practices (cultivation). Objectives: quality assurance of raw materials (→ quality, safety, efficacy of finished products), guidance for national GAP/SOPs, and sustainable, conservation-friendly production.

1. Identification/authentication: species as per national pharmacopoeia; botanical identity (genus, species, variety, author, family, local names, chemotype) recorded; voucher specimen to a herbarium when first registered or in doubt.

2. Seeds & propagation material: specified, authentic, healthy, disease-free, resistant to stress; organic certification where needed; no counterfeit or adulterated seed.

3. Cultivation: (a) site: free from soil/air/water pollution, past land use evaluated; (b) ecology & social impact: protect biodiversity, benefit local community; (c) climate: day length, rainfall, temperature; (d) soil: proper nutrients, pH, drainage; correct fertilizers, no human excreta, composted manure; erosion control; (e) irrigation & drainage to plant needs with quality water; (f) maintenance & protection: pruning, shading; minimum approved agrochemicals by trained staff, IPM, documented, residue limits respected.

4. Harvest: at optimal season/time for active constituents; dry weather; clean tools; avoid soil contact; clean aerated containers; discard decomposed material; transport promptly.

5. Personnel: knowledge of the plant, hygiene training, protective clothing for agrochemicals, environmental awareness.

Conclusion: GAP is the first step of quality assurance: a good factory cannot rescue a poorly grown herb.

Q2. What is meant by cGMP?
Brief 2m · KUHS Model (+ Brainkart: define GACP, GLP, GMP, cGMP)

cGMP (current Good Manufacturing Practices) is the part of quality assurance which ensures that products are consistently produced and controlled to quality standards appropriate to their intended use, using current, up-to-date technologies, systems and regulatory expectations (premises, equipment, validation, documentation, hygiene). In India, cGMP for ASU drugs is Schedule T; WHO gives supplementary GMP for herbal medicines (2007; TRS 1010 Annex 2, 2018). (GMP = factory practices; GACP = cultivation/collection practices; GLP = laboratory practices.)

Q3. Describe the guidelines on GACP for medicinal plants.
Essay 10m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2021 essay; MGR 2022 short; Brainkart ×4) · ~25 min
Intro + objectives + structure (1½m) → GAP (2½m) → good collection practices (2m) → common technical aspects + Fig 2.4 (3m) → other issues + conclusion (1m)

1. Introduction

WHO published the GACP guidelines for medicinal plants in 2003 (Geneva), as the first step of QA of herbal medicines. Scope: cultivation and collection of medicinal plants and certain post-harvest operations. Objectives: QA of raw materials, guide national GACP/monographs/SOPs, sustainable cultivation and collection. Structure: introduction & glossary; GAP; good collection practices; common technical aspects; other issues; annexes (China, EU, Japan guidelines, model monograph, sample record).

2. Good agricultural practices

Identification/authentication with voucher specimens; authentic, healthy seeds; cultivation: site free of pollution, ecology and social impact, climate, soil (nutrients, pH; composted manure, no human excreta; erosion control), irrigation & drainage, plant maintenance and protection (minimum approved pesticides, IPM, records, residue limits); harvest at peak active content, in dry weather, clean tools, no soil contact; trained personnel.

3. Good collection practices

Permission to collect (permits, red lists, CITES/phytosanitary certificates for export); technical planning (distribution, population density, phenology, trained team, transport); selection of correct species with voucher specimens; non-destructive collection (no rare species, lateral roots only, bark in longitudinal strips, avoid polluted sites and pastures, no soil contact, separate containers); trained, protected personnel.

4. Common technical aspects

Post-harvest: inspection and sorting; primary processing (prompt unloading, protection from rain, remove mouldy/foreign material); drying (thin layers, shade for leaves/flowers, low temperature for volatile oils, never on bare ground, direct fire <60 °C); specific processing (peeling, boiling, steaming, roasting, fermentation, lime treatment; irradiation declared); processing facilities (clean site, separated dirty/clean areas, potable water, lighting 540/220/110 lux, ventilation). Packaging & labelling (clean dry containers; label with scientific name, part, origin, date, grower/processor, batch number; records 3 years). Storage & transport (clean ventilated vehicles; fresh 2–8 °C, frozen below −20 °C; licensed fumigation, documented). Equipment (non-toxic, cleanable). QA (audits). Documentation (SOPs, cultivation records, herbarium specimens, batch numbers). Personnel (training, hygiene, health, protective clothing). [Draw Fig 2.4.]

5. Other issues & conclusion

Intellectual property rights and benefit-sharing, protection of endangered species, research needs. GACP ensures that herbal medicines start from authentic, clean, potent and sustainably sourced raw material, on which all later GMP and QC depend.

Q4. Discuss in detail about GMP (GMP requirements for herbal medicine).
Essay 10m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (AKTU 2023 essay; MGR 2021 short "GMP requirement for herbal medicine") · for 5 marks write sections 1, 3 and 4 only
Definitions GMP/cGMP + importance (1½m) → Schedule T Part I & II (3m) → WHO herbal GMP key points (3m) → personnel hygiene, documentation, complaints/recalls (1½m) → conclusion (1m)

1. Definition & importance

GMP is the part of QA ensuring products are consistently produced and controlled to appropriate quality standards; cGMP stresses current technology. For herbals it prevents contamination, mix-ups and variability arising from natural raw materials, and is mandatory for licensing and export.

2. GMP in India: Schedule T (Rule 157)

Part I: location and surroundings; buildings; water supply; disposal of waste; container cleaning; stores (raw materials: quarantine/approved/rejected; packing materials; finished goods); working space; health, clothing, sanitation and hygiene of workers; medical services; machinery and equipment; batch manufacturing records; distribution records; record of market complaints; quality control. Part II: recommended machinery, equipment and minimum premises for each category (churna, vati, asava–arishta, taila–ghrita, bhasma, avaleha…) of Ayurvedic/Siddha and Unani medicines.

3. WHO GMP for herbal medicines (key herbal points)

  • Designate the first production step where GMP starts (after GACP primary processing).
  • Qualification and process validation (extraction time, temperature, solvent); change control.
  • Premises: quarantine of all incoming herbs, separate storage of different herbs, off-floor, FIFO, fresh 2–8 °C, frozen < −18 °C, dust extraction, exhaust.
  • Equipment: vacuum/wet cleaning, avoid wooden tools unless dedicated.
  • Materials: permitted fumigants only; reference standards (herbarium sample, extract, marker).
  • Documentation: detailed specifications of herbal materials, preparations and finished products; processing instructions.
  • Production: no washing of dried herbs with water; blending only of in-spec batches; expiry = oldest batch.
  • QC: expert staff, special sampling, fingerprint chromatography, stability studies.

4. Personnel, hygiene, complaints & recalls

Trained staff; medical checks; no sick workers; protective clothing; two kinds of complaints (quality, adverse reactions) with investigation; recall SOP with segregated storage; self-inspection by a herbal expert.

5. Conclusion

GMP turns a variable natural raw material into a reproducible, safe and effective herbal medicine.

Q5. Explain the infrastructural requirements under GMP for the herbal industry.
Essay 10m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (RGUHS 2023; Brainkart: layout of factory premises) · draw Fig 2.2
Intro (1m) → site & building (2m) → layout with one-way flow + Fig 2.2 (3m) → stores, utilities, QC lab, personnel facilities (3m) → conclusion (1m)

1. Introduction

Infrastructure (premises, utilities, equipment) must be designed so that herbal materials, which carry dust, microbes and pests, are processed without contamination, mix-up or deterioration (Schedule T; WHO herbal GMP §12–13).

2. Site and buildings

Location away from sewage, drains, lavatories, smoke, dust and odour-producing factories; not flood-prone; paved roads. Sound, insect- and rodent-proof buildings; smooth, washable, crack-free walls/floors with coved corners; screened windows; adequate lighting and ventilation; no residential use or through-passage.

3. Layout

Unidirectional flow: receiving & sampling → raw-material store (quarantine/approved/rejected; plant, animal and mineral drugs separate) → washing, sorting and drying (dirty zone, isolated) → size reduction with dust extraction → processing sections (kwatha, asava–arishta fermentation, taila–ghrita, separate bhasma furnace section with exhaust) → formulation (churna, vati, avaleha) in clean areas → packing and labelling → finished goods store → dispatch.

4. Supporting infrastructure

  • Stores: off-floor pallets, FIFO, temperature/humidity control, cold storage (2–8 °C), separate packing-material store.
  • Utilities: potable/purified water monitored; separate non-potable lines; steam; HVAC with air flowing from clean to dirty areas; effluent treatment; daily waste removal.
  • Equipment: stainless steel, cleanable, calibrated; dedicated traditional tools.
  • QC laboratory: separate, with pharmacognosy, phytochemistry, microbiology and instrument sections.
  • Personnel facilities: change rooms, hand-wash stations, toilets and canteen not opening into production; first aid and medical services.

5. Conclusion

Well-planned infrastructure prevents cross-contamination and errors and is the physical foundation of GMP compliance.

Q6. What do you understand by quality assurance in herbal industry? Explain the importance of GMP in herbal industry.
Essay 10m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2023; Brainkart)
Define QA, QC, GMP + Fig 2.1 umbrella (3m) → elements of QA in herbal industry (3m) → importance of GMP (3m) → conclusion (1m)

1. Quality assurance

QA is the sum of all organised arrangements to ensure medicines are of the quality required for their intended use. In the herbal industry it extends from the farm to the patient: GACP → GMP (with QC) → GLP → GCP/GSP/GDP, all supported by SOPs, documentation, training, validation and self-inspection. [Draw Fig 2.1.]

2. Elements of QA in the herbal industry

  • Authenticated, GACP-compliant raw materials with full traceability.
  • Specifications for herbal materials, preparations and finished products (identity, purity, assay/markers, contaminants).
  • GMP-compliant premises, equipment, validated processes.
  • GLP-compliant QC laboratory with reference standards.
  • Stability studies, complaint handling, recalls, pharmacovigilance.
  • Trained personnel and continual improvement (cGMP).

3. Importance of GMP

  • Herbal raw materials vary and may be contaminated; GMP controls every step to give consistent batches.
  • Prevents contamination, cross-contamination and mix-ups (dust, microbes, toxic plants).
  • Validated processes substitute for missing knowledge of actives.
  • Ensures safety (limits for microbes, heavy metals, pesticides) and efficacy (standardized content).
  • Legal requirement (Schedule T, licence), enables export (WHO-GMP certification) and builds consumer confidence.
  • Documentation allows traceability, investigation of complaints and recalls.

4. Conclusion

QA is the system and GMP its manufacturing backbone; together they make herbal medicines reliable.

Q7. What is cGMP? Discuss the WHO guidelines on cGMP for herbal medicines.
Predicted Essay 10m · syllabus line + Brainkart (asked twice) · ~25 min
cGMP definition (1m) → history 1996/2007/2018 + general considerations (1½m) → glossary (1½m) → 17 headings with herbal points (4m) → Fig 2.3 + QC categories (1m) → conclusion (1m)

1. cGMP

Current GMP: the part of QA ensuring consistent production and control to appropriate standards using up-to-date technology and systems.

2. Background

WHO issued supplementary herbal GMP guidelines in 1996, revised as WHO guidelines on GMP for herbal medicines (2007) and updated in TRS 1010 Annex 2 (2018). They supplement the main WHO GMP text. Herbals differ from synthetics because they come from varied sources, vary in composition, may be contaminated with toxic plants and have few defined actives; hence production and primary processing directly affect quality.

3. Glossary

Herbs; herbal materials; herbal preparations; finished herbal products (not herbal if chemically defined actives are added); active ingredients; markers.

4. Main requirements

(1) QA using HPTLC, GC, HPLC, MS, AAS; (2) GMP starts at a designated first critical step; (3) sanitation, monitored water, daily waste removal; (4) qualification, process validation, change control; (5) complaints: quality and adverse reactions; (6) recalls with segregated storage; (7) contract production with validated cleaning; (8) self-inspection by a herbal expert; (9–10) trained personnel (botany, pharmacognosy, phytochemistry, microbiology); (11) personal hygiene and protective clothing; (12) premises: quarantine, separate storage, FIFO, 2–8 °C fresh / −18 °C frozen, dust extraction; (13) equipment: vacuum/wet cleaning, dedicated wooden tools; (14) materials: permitted fumigants, reference standards; (15) documentation: detailed specifications and processing instructions; (16) production: no water-washing of dried herbs, controlled blending; (17) QC: expert sampling, identity by macro/micro/chromatography, assay of actives or markers, fingerprints, stability studies on first three commercial batches. [Draw Fig 2.3.]

5. Conclusion

The WHO herbal GMP guideline adapts general GMP to the special nature of plants and is the global benchmark for national herbal GMP codes such as Schedule T.

Q8. What is traditional system of medicine? Write a note on GLP in traditional system of medicine (aspects/requirements of GLP).
Short 5m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (AKTU 2023, MGR 2021, MGR 2022, MGR 2023, RGUHS 2023) · most-repeated short note
TSM definition (1m) → GLP definition + basis (1m) → requirements list (3m)

Traditional system of medicine: the sum total of knowledge, skills and practices based on theories, beliefs and experiences indigenous to different cultures, used to maintain health and prevent, diagnose or treat illness (WHO). India: AYUSH systems (Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, Homoeopathy).

GLP: a quality system for the organisation and conditions under which laboratory studies are planned, performed, monitored, recorded, archived and reported (OECD 1981, revised 1997). In India: Schedule L-1 (2008); ASU testing labs approved under Rules 160A–160J.

GLP requirements for a TSM testing laboratory: (1) premises with separate pharmacognosy, phytochemistry, microbiology and instrument sections, controlled environment; (2) qualified, trained personnel with defined duties; (3) calibrated, maintained equipment with log books; (4) properly labelled chemicals and reagents; (5) reference materials: authenticated herbarium voucher specimens, crude drug samples, marker standards, pharmacopoeial (API/UPI/SPI) standards; (6) maintained microbial cultures and media; (7) SOPs for all tests and operations; (8) specifications and validated methods; (9) sample receipt, coding, storage and retention; (10) raw data, worksheets and certificates of analysis with data integrity; (11) internal audits and QA; (12) housekeeping, safety and waste disposal; (13) archiving of records and specimens.

Q9. Write a note on good practices for harvesting and storage of herbal medicinal products.
Short 5m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2023; RGUHS 2023 storage) · draw Fig 2.4
Harvesting (2m) → post-harvest/drying (1m) → packaging & storage (2m)

Harvesting (WHO GACP 2.4): harvest at the season and time of day when active constituents peak (not maximum yield); avoid dew, rain and high humidity (if wet, dry immediately indoors); clean, adjusted cutting tools; avoid soil contact (clean drop cloths), remove soil from roots; exclude weeds, toxic plants and foreign matter; use clean, dry, aerated containers; avoid compaction; discard decomposed material; transport promptly.

Post-harvest: inspect and sort; dry quickly in thin layers, off the ground, shade for leaves/flowers, low temperature for volatile oils, direct-fire heat below 60 °C.

Packaging & storage: clean, dry, non-polluting containers; label with scientific name, part, origin, date, batch number; records kept 3 years; stores clean, dry, ventilated, protected from pests, livestock and light; off the floor; FIFO; quarantine of incoming material; separate storage of different herbs and of organic material; secure storage of toxic plants; fresh materials 2–8 °C, frozen below −20 °C (GACP; −18 °C in WHO GMP); fumigation only when necessary, by licensed staff, documented; humidity checked after freezing/steam treatment.

Q10. Define SOP.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2021)

A Standard Operating Procedure is an authorized written procedure giving step-by-step instructions for performing an operation (e.g. cleaning a mill, calibrating a balance, determining ash). It ensures uniformity, reduces errors, aids training and is a core GMP/GLP/GACP document; it carries a number, version, effective date and approval signatures.

Q11. Secondary (specific) processing of medicinal plants.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2022)

Processing beyond primary cleaning and drying, done to improve purity, reduce drying time, prevent mould/insect damage, detoxify toxic constituents and enhance efficacy (WHO GACP 4.1.4): pre-selection, peeling of roots/rhizomes, boiling, steaming, soaking, pickling, distillation, fumigation, roasting, natural fermentation, lime treatment, chopping and size reduction (e.g. shodhana of toxic drugs in Ayurveda). Antimicrobial treatments such as irradiation must be declared.

Q12. What are weedicides? Mention two examples.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2022; Brainkart)

Weedicides (herbicides) are chemicals used to destroy or check the growth of weeds that compete with the crop for nutrients, water and light. Examples: 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T (chlorinated phenoxyalkanoic acids), glyphosate, paraquat. Under GACP they must be approved products, applied minimally by trained staff, documented, with residue limits respected.

Q13. Write a note on personal hygiene as per GMP.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (RGUHS 2023; Brainkart)

Workers must have pre-employment and periodic medical examinations; anyone with infectious or skin disease or open wounds must not handle products; clean protective clothing (uniform, cap, mask, gloves, shoes) and protection from toxic/allergenic plants; hand washing before work and after toilet; no eating, drinking, smoking or chewing in production areas; written hygiene SOPs and training.

Q14. Scope of herbal drug industries.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (RGUHS 2023)

Cultivation and raw-material trade; standardized extracts, essential oils and isolated phytochemicals; classical and proprietary ASU and homoeopathic formulations; phytopharmaceuticals; nutraceuticals and dietary supplements; herbal cosmetics; testing, contract research and export: a growing sector driven by global demand for natural products.

Q15. What are the objectives of GAP / GACP?
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (AKTU 2023; Brainkart)

(1) Contribute to quality assurance of medicinal plant materials to improve quality, safety and efficacy of finished herbal products; (2) guide formulation of national/regional GACP guidelines, monographs and SOPs; (3) encourage sustainable cultivation and collection that conserves medicinal plants and the environment; (4) ensure correct identity, traceability and freedom from contamination.

Q16. Define processed and finished herb as per WHO.
Brief 2m · RGUHS/MGR/AKTU (MGR 2023; Brainkart)

Processed plant materials: plant materials treated according to traditional procedures to improve their safety and/or efficacy, to facilitate clinical use or to make medicinal preparations (e.g. steaming, roasting, stir-baking with honey). Finished herbal products: herbal preparations made from one or more herbs (mixture herbal product if more than one), which may contain excipients; products with added chemically defined actives (synthetic or isolated) are not considered herbal.

Q17. Explain complaints and recall as per GMP.
Brief 2m · Brainkart (RGUHS/MGR/AKTU style)

Complaints: a trained person handles them; two types: product-quality complaints (defects, deterioration, adulteration of herbal material) investigated using retained samples, and adverse-reaction reports entered in a separate register; records reviewed regularly; authority informed if recall follows. Recall: written SOP as per national rules; recalled stock stored in a secure, segregated area until its fate is decided; records kept.
