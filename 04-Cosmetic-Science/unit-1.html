1 Definition of Cosmetics (Indian Regulations)

One line of law decides whether a bottle is a cosmetic or a drug. Learn it word-perfect; it is a sure 2 marks.

🎬 Story mode

Watch the Fairy Godmother work: she sprinkles sparkle on the pumpkin, sprays a mist over the mice, rubs her wand along Cinderella's sleeve and pours starlight over her hair. Why? To clean away the soot, beautify the dress, make her attractive at the ball and alter her appearance so completely that even her stepsisters don't recognise her. The Indian law describes a cosmetic in almost exactly those words: four ways of applying and four purposes. Nothing in her spell treats a disease; that is why it is a cosmetic and not a medicine.

📘 Exam definition: Drugs & Cosmetics Act, 1940, Section 3(aaa) (write exactly this)

"Cosmetic" means any article intended to be rubbed, poured, sprinkled or sprayed on, or introduced into, or otherwise applied to, the human body or any part thereof for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, or altering the appearance, and includes any article intended for use as a component of cosmetic.

Break the definition into exam-friendly pieces

🪄

HOW it is applied

Rubbed · poured · sprinkled · sprayed on, introduced into, or otherwise applied to the human body or any part.
🎯

WHY it is applied

Cleansing · beautifying · promoting attractiveness · altering the appearance.
🧩

Includes components

Raw materials meant to go into a cosmetic (e.g. a perfume compound, a colour) are also covered.

Cosmetic or drug? (the line examiners love)

💄 Cosmetic — Sec 3(aaa)

  • Purpose: clean, beautify, attract, alter appearance
  • Does not claim to treat or prevent disease
  • Acts on the surface; no intended effect on body function
  • e.g. plain shampoo, cold cream, lipstick, toothpaste
VS

💊 Drug — Sec 3(b)

  • Purpose: diagnosis, treatment, mitigation or prevention of disease
  • Or intended to affect structure/function of the body
  • Needs drug licence, often prescription
  • e.g. 2% ketoconazole shampoo, minoxidil lotion, tretinoin cream

The claim decides the category. A shampoo that "cleans hair" is a cosmetic; the same bottle claiming to "cure fungal infection of the scalp" becomes a drug.

🆕 Update: Cosmetics Rules, 2020

Earlier, cosmetics were controlled by the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945 (BIS standards were listed in Schedule S). On 15 December 2020 the Ministry of Health notified the separate Cosmetics Rules, 2020 (G.S.R. 763(E)), made under the D&C Act 1940 and applicable to cosmetics as defined in Section 3(aaa). Key points to quote:

  • Import needs registration with the Central Licensing Authority (CDSCO); manufacture needs a licence from the State Licensing Authority.
  • "New cosmetic" = a cosmetic containing a novel ingredient not used anywhere in the world or not recognised for cosmetic use in any national or international literature; it needs prior approval.
  • Ninth Schedule: finished cosmetics must meet the listed BIS standards (37 products, e.g. toothpaste IS 6356, skin creams IS 6608, shampoo IS 7884) — the old Schedule S idea carried forward.
  • Colours must be those permitted by IS 4707 Part 1 (Tenth Schedule); permitted colours may contain ≤ 2 ppm arsenic (as As₂O₃) and ≤ 20 ppm lead.
  • No animal testing of cosmetics; lead and arsenic compounds may not be used to colour cosmetics.
  • Special rules: fluoride toothpaste ≤ 1000 ppm F; hair dyes with PPD must carry a caution label + patch-test instructions.
➕ Other definitions you may be asked (EU / US)

EU (Regulation 1223/2009): a cosmetic is any substance or mixture intended to be placed in contact with the external parts of the body (epidermis, hair, nails, lips, external genital organs) or with the teeth and oral mucosa, exclusively or mainly to clean them, perfume them, change their appearance, protect them, keep them in good condition or correct body odours.

USA (FD&C Act): almost the same wording as India's Sec 3(aaa) — India's definition was modelled on it. Note that the US excludes true soap from "cosmetic", whereas India lists toilet soap (IS 2888) among the standardised cosmetics.

⭐ Exam tip

For "Define cosmetics as per Indian regulations" (2 marks): write the Sec 3(aaa) definition in quotes, then one line: "Cosmetics are now regulated under the Cosmetics Rules, 2020." For "Define and classify" add a 4-line classification (by site of application) from the next section.

⚠️ Common mistake

Writing "Section 3(b)" for cosmetics. 3(b) = drug, 3(aaa) = cosmetic. Also, don't write "Schedule S" as the current rule without mentioning that it is now the Ninth Schedule of the Cosmetics Rules, 2020.

B. Cosmeceuticals

📘 Exam definition

Cosmeceuticals are cosmetic products that contain biologically active ingredients which are claimed to have medicinal or drug-like benefits on the skin, hair or teeth — "between a cosmetic and a pharmaceutical". The term was popularised by dermatologist Dr Albert M. Kligman (1984) (cosmetic + pharmaceutical). It is not a legal category in India or the USA: such products are sold as cosmetics as long as they make no therapeutic claim.

3 Cosmetic Excipients · Surfactants

Excipients are the Fairy Godmother's magic ingredients: not the "star" active, but without them there is no gown at all.

📘 Exam definition: cosmetic excipient

Excipients are the functional, usually inactive, ingredients of a cosmetic that give it its form, stability, texture, safety and consumer appeal, and help deliver the active ingredient. Surfactants: the soap bubbles of Cinderella's floor

🎬 Story mode

When Cinderella scrubs the floor, the bubbles rise and each one holds her reflection. Every bubble skin is made of surfactant molecules: a "head" that loves water and a "tail" that hates it. Tails grab the greasy soot, heads stay in the water, and the soot is lifted away inside tiny balls called micelles. Change the electric charge on the head and you change the job: cleaning, conditioning, gentle baby-washing or making cream.

📘 Exam definition: surfactant

Surfactants (surface-active agents) are amphiphilic molecules having a hydrophilic (polar) head and a lipophilic (non-polar hydrocarbon) tail. They adsorb at surfaces and interfaces and lower surface and interfacial tension; above the critical micelle concentration (CMC) they form micelles that solubilise oily dirt. They are classified by the charge on the hydrophilic head.

Primary vs secondary surfactants (model-paper favourite)

🥇 Primary surfactants

  • The main cleansing and foaming agent
  • Used in the largest amount (about 8–20% active in shampoos)
  • Usually anionic
  • e.g. SLES, SLS, ammonium lauryl sulphate, sodium α-olefin sulphonate
VS

🥈 Secondary (co-) surfactants

  • Support the primary surfactant
  • Smaller amounts (about 1–5%)
  • Improve mildness (less irritation), foam quality & stability, viscosity, conditioning
  • Amphoterics & non-ionics: CAPB, cocamide MEA/DEA, decyl glucoside, amine oxides

Example in a shampoo: SLES (primary, cleans) + cocamidopropyl betaine (secondary, reduces SLES irritation, creamier foam) + cocamide MEA (foam booster and thickener).

⚠️ Common mistake

Mixing a high level of anionic with cationic surfactant in one simple formula: they neutralise each other and precipitate. That is why a classic conditioner (cationic) is applied after rinsing out the shampoo (anionic). (Modern 2-in-1 shampoos use special cationic polymers that are designed to deposit on dilution.)

💊 Pharmacy example

Cetrimide (cationic) is an antiseptic; polysorbate 80 (non-ionic) solubilises drugs in injections; SLS (anionic) is the foaming agent in most toothpastes; CAPB (amphoteric) makes baby shampoo "no-tears".

4 Rheology Modifiers

They decide whether a product pours like water, flows like honey or sits like pudding.

🎬 Story mode

The Fairy Godmother wants her pumpkin potion to sit on the spoon, not run off it. She stirs in a pinch of "thickening dust". Now it stays in a blob, but when she shakes the bottle it flows out smoothly, then thickens again. That is a rheology modifier at work: it controls flow without changing what the product does.

📘 Exam definition

Rheology modifiers are excipients that alter the flow behaviour of a cosmetic — its viscosity, consistency, thixotropy and yield value — so that the product has the desired texture, stays stable (no creaming or settling) and can be dispensed and spread easily. They include thickeners, gelling agents, suspending agents and stabilisers.

Words to use in the answer

  • Viscosity — resistance to flow.
  • Pseudoplastic / shear-thinning — becomes thinner when squeezed or rubbed (shampoo leaves the bottle, then thickens in the palm).
  • Thixotropy — time-dependent thinning on shaking and recovery on standing (nail polish, some lotions).
  • Yield value — minimum force needed to start flow; a yield value lets a gel hold particles in suspension (scrub beads, ZPT, mica).

5 Humectants

The water-magnets of the lab.

🎬 Story mode

Gus-Gus the mouse can't resist collecting every corn kernel he sees and hugging it tight. A humectant does that with water: its many –OH groups hydrogen-bond with water molecules from the air and from deeper skin, and hold them in the stratum corneum (and in the product, so the cream or toothpaste does not dry out).

📘 Exam definition

Humectants are hygroscopic substances that attract and retain moisture from the atmosphere (and from the deeper layers of skin) by hydrogen bonding, thereby increasing the water content of the stratum corneum and preventing the cosmetic itself from drying out.

Ideal properties of a humectant

  • Good hygroscopicity over a wide range of humidity; non-volatile
  • Non-toxic, non-irritant, non-sensitising
  • Colourless, odourless, tasteless (important in toothpaste)
  • Low viscosity, compatible with other ingredients, chemically stable
  • Not too sticky; inexpensive

Applications of humectants (Nov 2022 brief)

🦷

🫙

💇‍♀️

Hair products

Glycerin & panthenol in conditioners and gels keep hair soft
❄️

Lower the freezing point of aqueous products
🧪

Propylene glycol dissolves actives; glycerin plasticises films
⚠️ Common mistake

A humectant alone can pull water out of the skin in very dry air (low humidity) and feels sticky at high levels. That is why good moisturisers pair humectants with occlusive emollients (petrolatum, dimethicone) that seal the water in.

💊 Pharmacy example

Glycerin is the same humectant used in glycerin suppositories and in "Glycerin + rose water", the classic home remedy for winter-dry hands.

6 Emollients

The softeners: they make skin feel like Cinderella's silk gloves.

🎬 Story mode

7 Preservatives

The palace guards: they keep bacteria and fungi out of every jar.

🎬 Story mode

At midnight the magic ends and the coach becomes a pumpkin again, and a pumpkin left in a warm kitchen soon grows mould. A cream full of water, oils and sugars is the same "warm kitchen" for microbes, especially once fingers have dipped into it. Preservatives are the guards at the palace gate that stop germs from moving in.

📘 Exam definition

Preservatives are antimicrobial substances added in small concentrations to cosmetics to prevent the growth of bacteria, yeasts and moulds during manufacture, storage and consumer use, thereby protecting the product from spoilage and the user from infection.

Why cosmetics need preservation

  • Water-containing products (creams, lotions, shampoos) and natural ingredients (gums, herbal extracts, proteins) are excellent microbial food.
  • Repeated contamination during use (fingers, wet bathrooms).
  • Spoilage signs: bad odour, colour change, gas, phase separation, mould spots, pH drift.
  • Health risk: e.g. Pseudomonas aeruginosa in contaminated eye products can damage the eye.

Ideal properties

🎯 Broad-spectrum (Gram +, Gram −, yeasts, moulds)
💧 Effective at low concentration
🧪 Stable over wide pH & temperature
🤝 Compatible; not inactivated by other ingredients/packaging
🙂 Non-toxic, non-irritant, non-sensitising
🎨 Colourless, odourless
🌊 Water-soluble (microbes live in the water phase)
💰 Cheap and legally permitted

Factors affecting preservative efficacy

  • pH — acids need pH below ~5.5; parabens work over pH 4–8.
  • Oil/water partition — preservative dissolved in the oil phase is "wasted"; the water phase needs protection.
  • Binding by non-ionic surfactants, proteins, cellulose or plastic packaging.
  • Microbial load of raw materials & water; hygiene of plant (GMP).
  • Packaging — tubes and airless pumps reduce contamination vs open jars.

8 Skin: Basic Structure & Functions

Every skin product in Units 2–5 is designed around this one diagram. Draw it until you can do it with your eyes closed.

🎬 Story mode: the skin is Cinderella's castle

The outer castle wall is the epidermis: its top row of bricks (dead corneocytes) is cemented with lipid "mortar" — the stratum corneum, the real barrier. Behind the wall lies the busy courtyard, the dermis: roads (blood vessels), messengers (nerves), fountains (sweat glands), oil lamps (sebaceous glands) and flagpoles (hair follicles), all held together by ropes of collagen and elastin. Underneath is the soft garden and moat, the hypodermis, full of fat cushions that absorb shocks and keep the castle warm.

📘 Exam definition

The skin (integument) is the largest organ of the body (about 1.5–2 m² in an adult, roughly 15–16% of body weight). It consists of three layers: an outer avascular, keratinised stratified squamous epithelium, the epidermis; a vascular connective-tissue layer, the dermis; and the fatty hypodermis (subcutaneous tissue). Its appendages are hair, nails, sebaceous glands and sweat glands.

2. Dermis

  • Papillary layer (upper, thin): loose connective tissue forming dermal papillae that interlock with the epidermis; capillary loops and Meissner's corpuscles (touch); fingerprints.
  • Reticular layer (deep, thick): dense irregular tissue of collagen (strength) and elastin (recoil); breakdown with age and UV → wrinkles and sagging.
  • Ground substance: hyaluronic acid and other glycosaminoglycans that hold water and give turgor.
  • Cells: fibroblasts (make collagen), mast cells, macrophages.
  • Contains blood vessels, lymphatics, nerves and receptors, hair follicles, sebaceous and sweat glands, arrector pili muscles.

3. Hypodermis (subcutis)

Loose connective tissue and adipose tissue; anchors the skin to muscle; acts as an insulator, shock absorber and energy store; contains Pacinian corpuscles (pressure) and larger vessels. Not strictly part of the skin, but always drawn with it.

9 Hair: Basic Structure

Rapunzel's hair was strong enough to be a ladder. Let's see what makes a hair so tough.

🎬 Story mode

Imagine Rapunzel's braid under a magnifying glass. On the outside, flat scales overlap like the roof tiles of her tower (the cuticle) and reflect light, so the hair shines. Inside is a thick bundle of twisted protein ropes (the cortex) carrying the colour granules; it gives the strength to hold a whole prince. In the very centre is a soft, sometimes missing, core (the medulla). And the whole rope is made in a tiny "factory" under the skin, the hair follicle.

📘 Exam definition

Hair is a thin, flexible, keratinised filament produced by a hair follicle in the skin. The part above the skin is the hair shaft (dead keratinised cells); the part within the follicle is the hair root, which ends in the bulb around the dermal papilla. The shaft has three layers: cuticle, cortex and medulla.

The hair follicle (the factory)

  • Hair bulb — swollen lower end of the follicle.
  • Dermal papilla — cone of connective tissue with capillaries; nourishes and "instructs" the matrix. Its size decides hair thickness.
  • Matrix cells — rapidly dividing cells around the papilla that form the hair; melanocytes here colour it (greying = melanocytes stop working).
  • Inner root sheath (cuticle, Huxley's & Henle's layers) — moulds the growing hair; ends at the sebaceous-gland level.
  • Outer root sheath — continuous with the epidermis; contains the bulge (stem cells that restart the cycle).
  • Connective tissue sheath — outermost covering.
  • Sebaceous gland — opens into the follicle; sebum lubricates hair.
  • Arrector pili muscle — attached at the bulge; contraction makes hair stand up ("goose bumps").

Chemistry of hair

🧬 Composition

  • Keratin — a fibrous protein rich in the sulphur amino acid cystine (bulk of the hair)
  • Water (varies with humidity)
  • Lipids (from sebum + internal lipids, 18-MEA on cuticle)
  • Melanin: eumelanin (brown–black), pheomelanin (yellow–red)
  • Trace elements (Zn, Fe, Cu…)

🔗 Bonds that hold keratin

  • Disulphide (–S–S–) bonds between cysteine residues — strongest; broken by thioglycolate (perms, depilatories)
  • Hydrogen bonds — broken by water, reform on drying (wet set, blow-dry)
  • Salt (ionic) links — sensitive to pH
  • Van der Waals forces

Types of hair & useful numbers

  • Lanugo — fine, unpigmented foetal hair; vellus — short, fine "peach fuzz" on most of the body; terminal — long, thick, pigmented (scalp, eyebrows, beard, axilla).
  • Scalp has roughly 100,000 hairs; hair grows about 1 cm per month; losing 50–100 hairs a day is normal.
  • Hair carries a negative charge at normal pH (its isoelectric point is around pH 3.7), more so when damaged — which is why cationic conditioners cling to it.

10 Hair Growth Cycle

Each follicle runs its own clock: grow → shrink → rest → shed → start again.

🎬 Story mode

Why could Rapunzel's hair reach the bottom of a tower? In real life, hair length is decided by how long a follicle stays in its growing phase (anagen). Her magic hair simply never left anagen! Ordinary scalp hairs grow for a few years, then catagen (a short shrinking phase), telogen (a resting phase) and finally exogen (the hair falls out) — after which the follicle starts a fresh anagen. Eyebrow hairs stay short because their anagen lasts only a few months.

Quick tooth anatomy

  • Enamel — outer covering of the crown; hardest tissue of the body (about 96% mineral, mostly hydroxyapatite); no cells or nerves, so it cannot regrow.
  • Dentine — bulk of the tooth under enamel; full of microscopic dentinal tubules containing fluid that connect to the pulp (the basis of sensitivity).

  • Cementum covers the root; the periodontal ligament anchors the root to the alveolar bone; the gingiva (gum) seals the neck of the tooth.
  • Saliva buffers acids, washes the teeth and supplies calcium and phosphate for remineralisation.

1. Introduction

Hair is a keratinised filamentous appendage of the skin produced by the hair follicle. It protects the scalp from UV and injury, helps in sensation and temperature regulation, and is important for appearance. Each hair has a shaft (the part projecting above the skin surface, made of dead keratinised cells) and a root (the part within the follicle) ending in the bulb.

2. Diagram

3. Structure of the hair shaft

  • Cuticle — outermost 6–10 layers of thin, flat, transparent overlapping cells arranged like roof tiles with free edges pointing to the tip; covered by a lipid layer (18-methyleicosanoic acid). It protects the cortex, controls water uptake and, when the scales lie flat, reflects light to give shine. Bleaching, heat and harsh combing lift and break the scales.
  • Cortex — the main bulk (about 80–90%) of the hair; elongated cells filled with α-keratin organised into macrofibrils and microfibrils. It contains the melanin granules (eumelanin – brown/black, pheomelanin – yellow/red) that give natural colour, and gives the hair its strength and elasticity. Permanent dyes, bleaches and perms act here.
  • Medulla — the central core of loosely packed cells and air spaces; often discontinuous or absent in fine hair; of little cosmetic importance.

4. Structure of the hair follicle

  • Hair bulb — the expanded lower end, lying in the deep dermis/hypodermis.
  • Dermal papilla — connective tissue with blood capillaries that nourishes and controls the matrix; its size determines hair thickness.
  • Matrix — actively dividing epithelial cells over the papilla that form the hair; melanocytes here add pigment.
  • Inner root sheath (cuticle, Huxley's and Henle's layers) moulds the hair; outer root sheath is continuous with the epidermis and contains the bulge (stem cells).
  • Sebaceous gland opens into the follicle and lubricates the hair with sebum; the arrector pili muscle attached at the bulge raises the hair.

5. Chemical nature

Hair is mainly keratin, a fibrous protein rich in the sulphur amino acid cystine, with water, lipids, melanin and trace metals. Keratin chains are held by disulphide bonds (strongest; broken in perming), hydrogen bonds (broken by water), salt linkages (pH-sensitive) and van der Waals forces. Hair has an isoelectric point of about pH 3.7, so it is negatively charged in normal use, which explains the binding of cationic conditioners. Types of hair: lanugo, vellus and terminal.

1. Introduction

The skin is the largest organ of the body, covering about 1.5–2 m² and forming roughly 15–16% of body weight. It is made of three layers — epidermis, dermis and hypodermis — with appendages (hair, nails, sebaceous and sweat glands).

2. Diagram

Draw Fig 1.2 and label: stratum corneum, granulosum, spinosum, basale, melanocyte, dermal papilla, dermis, collagen, blood vessels, nerve ending, hair shaft, follicle, bulb, papilla, sebaceous gland, arrector pili, sweat gland and pore, hypodermis with fat cells.

3. Epidermis

Outer, avascular, keratinised stratified squamous epithelium (0.05 mm on eyelids to 1.5 mm on soles). Layers from deep to surface:

  1. Stratum basale — single layer of columnar dividing cells; contains melanocytes (melanin) and Merkel cells (touch).
  2. Stratum spinosum — several layers of polygonal cells joined by desmosomes; contains Langerhans cells (immunity).
  3. Stratum granulosum — flattened cells with keratohyalin granules and lamellar bodies that release barrier lipids.
  4. Stratum lucidum — clear layer present only in thick skin of palms and soles.
  5. Stratum corneum — 15–20 or more layers of dead keratin-filled corneocytes embedded in lipids (ceramides, cholesterol, fatty acids) — the "bricks and mortar" barrier; contains NMF; surface pH 4.5–5.5 (acid mantle).

Cells move from basale to corneum and are shed in about 28 days.

4. Dermis

Vascular connective-tissue layer: upper papillary layer (loose tissue, dermal papillae with capillaries and Meissner's corpuscles) and deeper reticular layer (dense collagen and elastin fibres giving strength and elasticity). Ground substance of hyaluronic acid holds water. It contains fibroblasts, mast cells, blood vessels, lymphatics, nerves, hair follicles, sebaceous glands, sweat glands and arrector pili muscles.

5. Hypodermis and appendages

Hypodermis: loose connective tissue with fat cells — insulation, cushioning, energy store; Pacinian corpuscles. Appendages: eccrine sweat glands (thermoregulation, open on skin), apocrine glands (axilla/groin, open into follicles, odour), sebaceous glands (sebum: triglycerides, wax esters, squalene, fatty acids), hair and nails.

6. Functions of skin

  1. Protection — mechanical barrier; chemical and microbial barrier (acid mantle); UV protection by melanin.
  2. Prevention of water loss — stratum corneum lipids control TEWL.
  3. Thermoregulation — sweating, vasodilation/vasoconstriction, fat insulation.
  4. Sensation — receptors for touch, pressure, pain and temperature.
  5. Excretion — sweat removes water, salts, urea.
  6. Synthesis of vitamin D — UVB converts 7-dehydrocholesterol to vitamin D₃.
  7. Immunity — Langerhans cells.
  8. Absorption — of lipid-soluble substances and drugs (transdermal delivery).
  9. Storage — fat and water; communication — expression, appearance.

Conclusion

An intact stratum corneum and balanced sebum and sweat keep skin healthy and attractive; most skin cosmetics (cleansers, moisturisers, sunscreens) are designed to support exactly these structures and functions.

Excipients are functional ingredients that give a cosmetic its form, stability, texture and safety and help deliver its actives. Definition: hygroscopic substances that attract and hold water from the air and deeper skin, raising stratum-corneum hydration and preventing products from drying out. They act by hydrogen bonding through –OH, –COOH or amide groups.

Q. Describe / explain in detail the hair growth cycle.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Jul 2023, Dec 2023 (+ Model: structure of hair & growth cycle) · ~12 min
Introduction (0.5m) → cycle diagram (1.5m) → 4 phases with duration/events (2.5m) → factors & disorders (0.5m)

Each hair follicle undergoes repeated, independent cycles of growth, regression, rest and shedding, so that scalp hair is replaced continuously without baldness. (Draw Fig 1.4.)

  1. Anagen (growth phase) — 2–7 years; 85–90% of scalp hairs. Matrix cells around the dermal papilla divide rapidly; melanocytes pigment the hair; hair grows about 1 cm per month. The length of anagen decides the final length of hair (long on scalp, short on eyebrows).
  2. Catagen (transition/regression phase) — about 2–3 weeks; ~1% of hairs. Cell division stops, the lower follicle regresses by apoptosis, the hair separates from the papilla and becomes a keratinised club hair; pigment production stops.
  3. Telogen (resting phase) — about 3 months; 10–15% of hairs. The club hair rests in the follicle while a new hair germ forms below.
  4. Exogen (shedding phase) — the club hair is actively released and shed (normally 50–100 hairs/day) and the follicle re-enters anagen.

Factors: hormones (androgens, oestrogens, thyroid), nutrition, stress, illness and drugs. Disturbances: telogen effluvium, anagen effluvium (chemotherapy), androgenetic alopecia (short anagen, miniaturisation), alopecia areata.

Primary = main cleanser/foamer, high amount, anionic (SLES). Secondary = mildness/foam/viscosity booster, low amount, amphoteric or non-ionic (CAPB, cocamide MEA).
Name two rheology modifiers
Carbomer 940 (needs neutralising with TEA) and xanthan gum. Also CMC, HEC, bentonite, carrageenan.
Classes of humectants (IMON)
Inorganic (CaCl₂), Metal-organic (sodium lactate, sodium PCA), Organic polyols (glycerin, sorbitol, PG), Natural (hyaluronic acid, urea).
Emollient classes (HOW FEeLS)
Hydrocarbons, Oils/butters, Waxes, Fatty acids/alcohols, Esters, Lanolin, Silicones.
Classic paraben combination?
Methylparaben ~0.18% + propylparaben ~0.02% (≈ 9 : 1). Popular paraben-free alternative: phenoxyethanol (≤ 1%).
Epidermis layers, surface → deep
Corneum, Lucidum (thick skin only), Granulosum, Spinosum, Basale — "Cinderella Loves Getting Shiny Bling".
"Bricks and mortar" of stratum corneum?
Bricks = corneocytes with keratin + NMF; mortar = ceramides, cholesterol, free fatty acids. Surface pH 4.5–5.5 (acid mantle).
Three layers of the hair shaft
Cuticle (scales, shine) → Cortex (keratin + melanin, strength, colour; dyes act here) → Medulla (soft core).
Hair cycle phases + durations
Anagen 2–7 yr (85–90%) → Catagen 2–3 wk (~1%) → Telogen ~3 mo (10–15%) → Exogen (50–100 hairs shed/day).
Gingivitis vs periodontitis?
Gingivitis: gum inflammation, bleeding, reversible. Periodontitis: ligament + bone destruction, pockets, loose teeth, irreversible.
Cause of dentine hypersensitivity?
Exposed dentinal tubules → fluid movement stimulates pulp nerves (hydrodynamic theory). Treat: KNO₃, SrCl₂, arginine.
Main cause of halitosis?
Volatile sulphur compounds (H₂S, methyl mercaptan) made by anaerobic bacteria on the tongue.

📝 One-Page Cheat Sheet — Unit 1

Read this the night before the exam. Everything important, nothing extra.

📜 Definition

Sec 3(aaa) D&C Act 1940: rubbed/poured/sprinkled/sprayed/introduced/applied → cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, altering appearance; includes components. Now: Cosmetics Rules 2020 (Ninth Schedule = BIS standards; no animal testing; fluoride ≤ 1000 ppm).

🗂️ Classification

By site (skin, hair, oral, eye, lip, nail, body, fragrance) · by function (cleansing, care, protective, decorative, corrective) · by form (liquid, semisolid, solid, aerosol). Cosmeceutical = cosmetic + bioactive (Kligman 1984): retinol, vitamin C, niacinamide, AHA, ceramides.

🫧 Surfactants

Amphiphile, lowers surface tension, micelles above CMC. Anionic SLS/SLES (clean) · Cationic quats (condition) · Amphoteric CAPB (mild) · Non-ionic polysorbates (emulsify). Primary = anionic main cleanser; secondary = CAPB, cocamide MEA. HLB 3–6 w/o, 8–18 o/w, 15–18 solubiliser.

🍯 Rheology modifiers

Natural (xanthan, guar, carrageenan, alginate) · semi-synthetic (CMC, HEC, HPMC) · synthetic (carbomer + TEA) · inorganic (bentonite, Veegum, silica) · NaCl, fatty alcohols. Thicken, gel, suspend, stabilise, bind toothpaste.

💧 Humectants · 🧈 Emollients

Humectant: hygroscopic, pulls water in — CaCl₂ / Na-lactate, Na-PCA / glycerin, sorbitol, PG / hyaluronic acid, urea. Emollient: softens, fills gaps, ↓ TEWL — liquid paraffin, almond oil, beeswax, cetyl alcohol, IPM, lanolin, dimethicone.

🛡️ Preservatives

Acids (benzoate, sorbate) · parabens (methyl 0.18 + propyl 0.02) · alcohols (phenoxyethanol ≤ 1%) · formaldehyde releasers (DMDM hydantoin) · isothiazolinones · quats · phenolics · mercurials (eye only). Broad-spectrum, water-soluble, non-toxic. Challenge test.

🏰 Skin

Epidermis (C-L-G-S-B, avascular, 28-day turnover; keratinocytes, melanocytes, Langerhans, Merkel) · Dermis (papillary + reticular; collagen, elastin, vessels, glands) · Hypodermis (fat). SC = bricks + mortar, pH 4.5–5.5. Functions: Protection, Temperature, Sensation, Excretion, Vit D, Immunity, Absorption, Storage.

💇‍♀️ Hair + 🔄 cycle

Shaft: cuticle (scales) · cortex (keratin, melanin) · medulla. Follicle: bulb, papilla, matrix, IRS, ORS, bulge, sebaceous gland, arrector pili. Bonds: S–S, H, salt. Anagen 2–7 y (85–90%) → Catagen 2–3 wk (1%) → Telogen ~3 mo (10–15%) → Exogen (50–100/day).

🦷 Oral problems

Plaque → calculus; caries (S. mutans, acid, pH < 5.5); gingivitis (bleeding, reversible) → periodontitis (bone loss, irreversible); sensitivity (open tubules, hydrodynamic); halitosis (VSCs); stains (extrinsic/intrinsic).
