5.1 Introduction to Community Health & Community Services

Book section 5.1 · The words examiners expect you to define before anything else.

🏝️ Story mode — Moana

Motunui doesn't wait for a doctor to sail in from far away. Gramma Tala knows the old remedies, the fishermen share their catch, the village decides together where to plant and build. That is a community caring for its own health — with a little expert help when needed. Community services are exactly this: health care taken to where people live, with the people taking part.

📘 Exam definitions

Community — a group of people living in a common geographical area, sharing common values, culture and interests, and organised into social units.

Community health — the health status of the members of a community, and the organised efforts (preventive, promotive, curative and rehabilitative) made by society to protect and improve it.

Community health services — the health services delivered at or near people's homes, villages, towns and schools through the primary health-care network and outreach workers, with community participation. They are grouped as rural, urban and school health services.

Primary health care (Alma-Ata, 1978, in short) — essential health care based on practical, scientifically sound and socially acceptable methods, made universally accessible to individuals and families in the community through their full participation, at a cost the community and country can afford; it is the first level of contact with the national health system.

4 principles of primary health care

  1. Equitable distribution
  2. Community participation
  3. Intersectoral coordination
  4. Appropriate technology
(+ focus on prevention)

8 elements — mnemonic “ELEMENTS”

  • Education about health problems
  • Locally endemic disease control
  • Expanded programme of immunisation
  • Maternal & child health incl. family planning
  • Essential drugs
  • Nutrition & food supply
  • Treatment of common diseases & injuries
  • Safe water & sanitation
⭐ Exam tip

Start every Unit-5 answer with one line on primary health care (Alma-Ata 1978, “Health for All”). It frames PHC, rural, urban and school services in one stroke — and the Essential drugs element is your pharmacist hook.

Ayushman Bharat (2018) has two pillars: (1) Health & Wellness Centres — sub-centres and PHCs upgraded to deliver comprehensive primary health care (first one opened on 14 April 2018 at Jangla, Bijapur, Chhattisgarh); (2) PM-JAY — ₹5 lakh per family per year hospital insurance. 5.3 Primary Health Centre — Establishment, Objectives & Functions

Book section 5.3 · The PHC is the cornerstone (the “heart of Te Fiti”) of rural health care.

❤️ Story mode — the heart of Te Fiti

When Te Fiti's heart is stolen, darkness spreads island to island; when Moana puts it back, everything blooms. A PHC works like that heart for about 30,000 people: six sub-centres (little islands) send it their patients and records, and it pumps back doctors' care, medicines, vaccines, lab tests and health education. Remove the PHC and the whole network goes dark.

📘 Exam definition

A Primary Health Centre (PHC) is the first contact point between the village community and a medical officer. It is a 4–6-bedded health institution serving about 30,000 rural population in the plains (20,000 in hilly, tribal and difficult areas), acting as a referral unit for about six sub-centres, and providing integrated curative and preventive health care with emphasis on preventive and promotive aspects.

Establishment — history

📜1946Bhore Committee (Health Survey & Development Committee) recommends PHCs for rural India
🏥1952First PHCs set up, alongside the Community Development Programme
🔧1961–62Mudaliar Committee — strengthen existing PHCs before opening new ones
🌍1978Alma-Ata Declaration — “Health for All”; PHC network expanded
⚙️2005 → 2018NRHM: 24×7 PHCs, IPHS norms (2007, 2012, 2022); PHCs → Ayushman Arogya Mandir

Objectives of PHC (IPHS)

  1. To provide comprehensive primary health care to the community through the PHC and its sub-centres.
  2. To achieve and maintain an acceptable standard of quality of care (IPHS).
  3. To make services more responsive and sensitive to the needs of the community.

Functions of PHC (the 10 you must write)

  1. Medical care — OPD, emergency (24×7 in upgraded PHCs), 4–6 in-patient beds, normal deliveries.
  2. Maternal & child health including family planning — ANC, PNC, institutional delivery, immunisation, contraception.
  3. Safe water supply & basic sanitation — chlorination of wells, promotion of latrines.
  4. Prevention & control of locally endemic diseases — malaria, TB, leprosy, filaria, diarrhoea.
  5. Collection & reporting of vital statistics — births, deaths, disease surveillance (IDSP).
  1. Health education & behaviour change communication.
  2. Implementation of national health programmes — UIP, NTEP, NLEP, NVBDCP, NPCDCS, NPHCE…
  3. Referral services to CHC/district hospital.
  4. Training of health workers, ASHAs, local dais, health assistants.
  5. Basic laboratory services — Hb, urine, blood smear/RDT for malaria, sputum for TB.

⚠️ Common mistake

Don't swap the numbers: a PHC serves 30,000 (plain), not 1,20,000 (that's the CHC). And PHC ≠ “primary health care” — one is a building, the other is a concept (Alma-Ata).

5.4 Improvement in Rural Sanitation

Book section 5.4 · Safe water + safe excreta disposal + clean surroundings + clean habits.

🌊 Story mode — Moana & the ocean

The ocean in Moana is alive, kind and spotless — it even helps her. But imagine Motunui's lagoon full of village waste: the fish die, children fall sick with diarrhoea, flies swarm the coconut huts. Rural sanitation is about keeping the lagoon, the wells and the soil clean — so the “ocean” stays a friend, not an enemy.

📘 Exam definitions

Sanitation (WHO): the means of promoting health by preventing human contact with the hazards of wastes — through safe collection, treatment and disposal of human excreta, sewage, waste water and solid waste.

Environmental sanitation (classic): control of all those factors in man's physical environment that exercise, or may exercise, a harmful effect on his physical development, health and survival.

Rural sanitation = applying these measures in villages: safe water, sanitary latrines, refuse and sullage disposal, clean housing and cattle sheds, food hygiene, fly control and health education.

Why villages need it — the F-diagram

Diseases linked to poor rural sanitation: diarrhoeal diseases, cholera, typhoid, bacillary & amoebic dysentery, hepatitis A & E, poliomyelitis, worm infestations (roundworm, hookworm, threadworm), fly-borne eye infections — plus child stunting from repeated gut infections.

Methods for improvement of rural sanitation

1 · Safe water supply 💧

  • Protect sources; prefer hand-pumps/tube-wells and piped water.
  • Sanitary well: lined, with parapet wall, cover, platform and drain; no common rope-and-bucket.
  • Disinfect wells with bleaching powder (aim: residual free chlorine ≈ 0.5 mg/L after 1 hour contact); household boiling/chlorine tablets.
  • Jal Jeevan Mission (15 Aug 2019) — functional household tap connection, 55 litres/person/day; extended to 2028.

2 · Excreta disposal 🚽

  • A sanitary latrine for every household; end open defecation.
  • Rural types: pit (dug-well) latrine, bore-hole latrine, water-seal latrines (PRAI, RCA types), twin-pit pour-flush (promoted under Swachh Bharat), septic tank for isolated houses.
  • Site latrines away from and downhill of wells (Park: at least 15 m / 50 ft).

3 · Refuse (solid waste) disposal 🗑️

  • Segregate wet/dry waste; composting (manure pits, Bangalore trench method), vermicomposting.
  • Biogas (gobar-gas) plants for cattle dung → fuel + manure.
  • Don't throw refuse in drains or ponds.

4 · Sullage (waste-water) disposal 💦

  • Soakage pits and kitchen gardens for bathing/kitchen water.
  • Covered drains; prevent stagnant pools (mosquito breeding).

5 · Housing & cattle sheds 🏡

  • Ventilation, light, smokeless chulha / LPG (PM Ujjwala) against indoor smoke.
  • Cattle sheds separate from living rooms; manure pits away from houses.

6 · Food hygiene & vector control 🪰

  • Cover food and water; clean utensils; safe milk.
  • Fly control by proper waste disposal; anti-larval measures for mosquitoes.

7 · Health education & behaviour change 📢

  • Handwashing with soap after defecation, before cooking/eating, after cleaning a child.
  • Community-Led Total Sanitation (CLTS) — the village itself decides to become open-defecation free.

8 · Government programmes 🏛️

  • Central Rural Sanitation Programme (1986) → Total Sanitation Campaign (1999) → Nirmal Bharat Abhiyan (2012) → Swachh Bharat Mission–Gramin (2 Oct 2014).
  • Rural India declared ODF on 2 Oct 2019; SBM-G Phase II (from 2020–21) — ODF Plus: sustain ODF + solid & liquid waste management.

WaterLatrinesRefuseSullageHousingFood & fliesEducationGovt schemes

Requirements of a sanitary latrine (Park — 7 points)

  1. Surface soil must not be contaminated.
  2. No contamination of ground water that may enter wells or springs.
  3. No contamination of surface water.
  4. Excreta must not be accessible to flies or animals.
  5. No handling of fresh excreta.
  6. Free from odour and unsightly conditions.
  7. Simple and inexpensive to build and operate.
💊 Pharmacist's role

Supply and teach correct use of ORS–zinc, chlorine tablets and bleaching powder; demonstrate handwashing; counsel on deworming (albendazole) and worm prevention (footwear); join village sanitation drives and Swachhata campaigns.

⚠️ Common mistake

“Rural sanitation” is not only toilets — always list at least 5 components (water, excreta, refuse, sullage, housing/food/flies, education). And don't call the bucket “service latrine” sanitary — it involves handling fresh excreta.

Psst, Mimosa… 5 F's in the F-diagram — Fluids, Fingers, Flies, Fields, Food. A latrine stops all five at once! 🚽✨

5.5 National Urban Health Mission (NUHM) & Urban Community Services

Book section 5.5 · Approved 1 May 2013 as a sub-mission of the National Health Mission (alongside NRHM).

🦊 Story mode — Zootopia

Zootopia looks perfect — shiny towers, a fast train, districts for every climate. But Judy and Nick soon find the hidden side: tiny cramped flats, workers living on the margins, shady corners nobody checks. Big Indian cities are similar: beside smart hospitals live slum dwellers, rickshaw-pullers, street vendors, construction workers and homeless people who cannot reach or afford care. NUHM is the plan to bring primary health care to these “hidden Zootopians”.

Urban health problems (why NUHM)

🏚️ Slums

Overcrowding, poor water & sanitation, TB, diarrhoea, dengue.

🏭 Pollution

Air & noise pollution → respiratory disease, stress.

🍔 Lifestyle

Obesity, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, substance use.

🚗 Injuries & access

Road accidents; migrants and informal workers lack access; high private costs.
📘 Exam definition

NUHM (approved by the Union Cabinet on 1 May 2013) is a sub-mission of the National Health Mission that aims to improve the health status of the urban population, particularly the urban poor and other vulnerable sections, by facilitating equitable access to quality primary health care, reducing out-of-pocket expenditure, and partnering with the community and urban local bodies.

Coverage & target groups

State capitals, district headquarters and other cities/towns with population above 50,000 (smaller towns continue under NRHM). Focus: slum dwellers and other vulnerable groups — homeless people, rag-pickers, street children, rickshaw-pullers, street vendors, railway/bus-station porters, construction and other daily-wage workers, sex workers and temporary migrants.

Objectives

  1. A need-based, city-specific urban health-care system for the urban poor.
  2. Strengthen urban primary health care infrastructure and outreach.
  3. Partnership with community groups (Mahila Arogya Samiti) and urban local bodies for planning and monitoring.
  4. Reduce out-of-pocket expenditure; make services free/affordable at the point of care.
  5. Involve NGOs, charitable and private providers; use IT/e-health for efficiency.

5.6 Health Promotion & Education in School

Book section 5.6 · Schools reach crores of children at the age when lifelong habits are formed.

👾 Story mode — Monsters University

At Monsters University, Mike Wazowski learns more than scaring — he learns teamwork, grit and how to bounce back from failure, with Dean Hardscrabble keeping the campus disciplined. A school is the perfect place to shape health habits too: a clean campus, a hot lunch, a nurse's check-up, a teacher who explains handwashing and puberty, and friends who say no to tobacco. That whole package is school health — and when the school itself is built around health, WHO calls it a health-promoting school.

📘 Exam definitions

School health service — the health services provided to school children (and staff) to promote their health, prevent disease, detect and treat defects early, and create a healthy school environment.

Health-promoting school (WHO) — a school that is constantly strengthening its capacity as a healthy setting for living, learning and working. (WHO's Global School Health Initiative began in 1995.)

Health education in school — planned learning experiences that give pupils knowledge, attitudes and skills to make healthy choices.

Objectives of school health service

  1. Promotion of positive health.
  2. Prevention of diseases.
  3. Early diagnosis, treatment and follow-up of defects.
  4. Awakening health consciousness in children.
  5. Provision of a healthful school environment.
🧠 Objectives — “Please Protect Every Adorable Head”
Promote positive healthPrevent diseaseEarly diagnosis & follow-upAwake health consciousnessHealthful environment

In India the School Health Committee (1960, chaired by Smt. Renuka Ray; report 1961) studied the health and nutrition of school children and recommended organised school health services.

Aspects (components) of school health — the wheel

Health promotion in schools (WHO health-promoting school components)

📜 Healthy school policies

Tobacco-free campus, no junk food, anti-bullying, menstrual-hygiene policy.

🏫 Physical environment

Safe building, clean drinking water, separate toilets for girls, handwashing stations, playground.

🤗 Social environment

Supportive teachers, inclusion, no corporal punishment, counselling.

🧑‍🎓 Personal health skills

Life-skills & health education: hygiene, nutrition, puberty, saying no to drugs.

🏘️ Community links

Parents, PTA, local health centre, NGOs, panchayat.

🩺 Health services

Screening (RBSK), immunisation, deworming, IFA, first aid, referral.

Health education in schools — what & how

📚 WHAT to teach

  • Personal hygiene, handwashing, oral & eye care
  • Nutrition, balanced diet, anaemia
  • Prevention of communicable diseases, immunisation
  • Safety, first aid, road safety
  • Adolescence, menstrual hygiene, reproductive health
  • Tobacco, alcohol & drug abuse; mental health
  • Environment & sanitation
+

🎭 HOW to teach

  • Class talks with demonstrations (handwashing, brushing)
  • Posters, charts, models, videos
  • Role-play, puppet shows, skits, quizzes
  • Health clubs, peer educators, school health days
  • Teachers as role models; involve parents
  • Principles: interest, participation, known → unknown, learning by doing, reinforcement

Introduction: The Primary Health Centre is the first contact point between the village community and the medical officer, recommended by the Bhore Committee (1946) and first set up in 1952. One PHC serves 30,000 population in plains and 20,000 in hilly/tribal areas and is the referral unit for about 6 sub-centres; 4 PHCs refer to one CHC.

Functions

  1. Medical care — OPD, emergency care, 4–6 beds, normal deliveries (24×7 PHCs).
  2. MCH including family planning — ANC, PNC, institutional delivery, immunisation, contraceptive services.
  3. Safe water supply and basic sanitation.
  4. Prevention and control of locally endemic diseases (malaria, TB, leprosy, diarrhoea).
  5. Collection and reporting of vital statistics and disease surveillance.
  6. Health education.
  7. National health programmes as relevant (UIP, NTEP, NLEP, NVBDCP, NCD).
  8. Referral services to CHC/district hospital.
  9. Training of health workers, ASHAs, local dais and health assistants.
  10. Basic laboratory services.

[Draw Fig 5.1 pyramid or Fig 5.2 hub.]

Staff: Medical officer + 14 paramedical/other staff (pharmacist, nurse-midwife, ANM, health assistants M & F, health educator, lab technician, clerks, driver, class IV). Update: PHCs are now upgraded to Ayushman Arogya Mandir delivering 12 packages of comprehensive primary health care. Conclusion: the PHC is the cornerstone of rural health care in India.

Q. National Urban Health Mission.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Model paper · ~12 min
Intro + date (0.5m) → Need/target groups (1m) → Objectives (1m) → Structure flow (1.5m) → Components/funding + conclusion (1m)

Introduction: NUHM was approved by the Union Cabinet on 1 May 2013 as a sub-mission of the National Health Mission (NHM = NRHM + NUHM). It covers state capitals, district headquarters and cities/towns with > 50,000 population.

Need & target groups

Urban poor face overcrowded slums, poor sanitation, communicable diseases (TB, diarrhoea, dengue), rising NCDs and high private costs. Target groups: slum dwellers, homeless, rag-pickers, street children, rickshaw-pullers, street vendors, porters, construction workers, sex workers, temporary migrants.

Objectives

  1. Need-based city-specific health care for the urban poor.
  2. Strengthen urban primary health care and outreach.
  3. Community partnership (MAS) and involvement of urban local bodies.
  4. Reduce out-of-pocket expenditure.
  5. Partnership with NGOs/private sector; IT-enabled services.

Structure

Mahila Arogya Samiti (50–100 households) + urban ASHA (1 per 1,000–2,500) → Urban PHC (1 per ~50,000–60,000, near slums) → Urban CHC (for 5–6 UPHCs; ≈ 2.5 lakh non-metro / 5 lakh metro) → district hospital/medical college. Outreach: Urban Health & Nutrition Days, slum camps by ANMs.

Funding at launch 75:25 Centre–State (90:10 for NE & special-category states). Update: UPHCs and urban HWCs now function as urban Ayushman Arogya Mandirs. Conclusion: NUHM brings the primary health care network — once only rural — into India's cities.

Q. Briefly write on National urban health mission.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Dec 2023

NUHM — sub-mission of NHM approved on 1 May 2013 to improve the health of the urban population, especially the urban poor and slum dwellers, in cities with > 50,000 population. Strategy: Urban PHC per ~50,000–60,000, Urban CHC for 5–6 UPHCs, Mahila Arogya Samiti (50–100 households), urban ASHA (1,000–2,500 population), outreach camps, reduced out-of-pocket costs, convergence with urban local bodies.

Q. Mention the community services in rural health.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS May 2022
  • Three-tier network: Sub-centre/AAM (5,000), PHC (30,000), CHC (1,20,000).
  • Village workers: ASHA, Anganwadi worker (ICDS), ANM & MPW; VHSNC and Village Health & Nutrition Days.
  • MCH & immunisation, national disease-control programmes, school health (RBSK).
  • 108/102 ambulances, mobile medical units, safe water (JJM) and sanitation (SBM-G), health education.
Q. Rural sanitation.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Nov 2022

Rural sanitation is the control of environmental factors in villages that harm health — through (1) safe water supply (sanitary wells, hand-pumps, chlorination, JJM taps), (2) sanitary excreta disposal (water-seal/twin-pit latrines, end of open defecation), (3) refuse disposal by composting/biogas, (4) sullage disposal by soakage pits, (5) clean housing & cattle sheds, food hygiene and fly control, and (6) health education (handwashing). Programme: Swachh Bharat Mission–Gramin (2014) → ODF (2019) → ODF Plus.

Q. Discuss various methods for improvement in rural sanitation.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Jul 2023
  1. Water — protect sources, sanitary wells, hand-pumps, chlorination, piped tap water (JJM).
  2. Excreta — household sanitary latrines (twin-pit pour-flush, water-seal) placed away from wells.
  3. Refuse — composting, manure pits, biogas plants; sullage — soakage pits, kitchen gardens.
  4. Housing, cattle sheds, food hygiene, fly & mosquito control.
  5. Health education & CLTS; schemes: SBM-G (2014), ODF Plus.
Q. Health education in schools.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Nov 2022

Planned teaching that gives pupils the knowledge, attitudes and skills for healthy living. Topics: personal hygiene & handwashing, oral/eye care, nutrition, prevention of communicable diseases, first aid & safety, adolescence & menstrual hygiene, avoidance of tobacco/alcohol/drugs, mental health. Methods: class talks, demonstrations, posters, role-play, health clubs, peer educators; teachers as role models. Example: Ayushman Bharat School Health & Wellness Programme (2020) — 2 Health & Wellness Ambassador teachers per school, 11 themes.

Q. What are the health promotions in schools?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Jul 2023

A health-promoting school (WHO) constantly strengthens its capacity as a healthy setting for living, learning and working. Health promotion in schools includes: (1) healthy school policies (tobacco-free campus, anti-bullying); (2) safe physical environment — clean water, separate toilets, handwashing; (3) supportive social environment; (4) life-skills health education; (5) school health services — RBSK screening, immunisation, deworming, IFA, mid-day meal (PM POSHAN); (6) links with parents and community.

Q. Mention the community services in school health.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Dec 2023
  • Health appraisal (periodic medical examination — RBSK teams) with remedial care & follow-up.
  • Prevention of communicable diseases (immunisation), deworming (NDD) and WIFS iron-folic acid.
  • Nutrition — PM POSHAN mid-day meal; healthful school environment (water, toilets).
  • First aid, dental & eye care, mental-health counselling, health education (AB-SHWP), school health records.
Q. Explain the health care system in India with special reference to the establishment, objectives, functions and staffing of PHCs. Predicted
Essay · 10 marks · not yet asked — the obvious Unit-5 essay · ~25 min · ~3 pages
Intro: primary health care (1m) → Levels of care + organisation (1.5m) → Pyramid with norms (2m) → PHC establishment & objectives (1.5m) → Functions (2.5m) → Staffing (1m) → Ayushman Arogya Mandir + conclusion (0.5m)

1. Introduction

India's health care system aims at “Health for All” through primary health care (Alma-Ata 1978) — essential care, universally accessible, with community participation, at affordable cost; elements: health education, endemic disease control, immunisation, MCH-FP, essential drugs, nutrition, treatment of common illness, safe water & sanitation.

2. Levels & organisation

Primary (village, sub-centre, PHC), secondary (CHC/FRU, district hospital), tertiary (medical colleges, AIIMS). Centre: MoHFW & DGHS; State: health directorate; District: DHO/CMO. Other agencies: private sector, AYUSH, NGOs, ESI, CGHS, insurance (PM-JAY).

3. Rural three-tier network

Sub-centre 5,000 / 3,000 (ANM, MPW, CHO) → PHC 30,000 / 20,000 (MO + 14) → CHC 1,20,000 / 80,000 (4 specialists, 30 beds) — plain / hilly-tribal. Village level: ASHA (1/1000), AWW, VHSNC. [Draw Fig 5.1 pyramid.]

4. PHC — establishment & objectives

Recommended by the Bhore Committee (1946); first PHCs 1952 with the Community Development Programme; strengthened after the Mudaliar Committee (1961–62) and Alma-Ata (1978); IPHS norms under NRHM. Objectives: comprehensive primary health care; acceptable standard of quality (IPHS); services responsive to community needs.

5. Functions

Medical care · MCH & family planning · safe water & sanitation · control of local endemic diseases · vital statistics · health education · national programmes · referral · training of health workers · basic lab services. [Fig 5.2 hub.]

6. Staffing

Medical officer 1, pharmacist 1, nurse-midwife 1, ANM 1, health educator 1, health assistants (M) 1 and (F) 1, UDC 1, LDC 1, lab technician 1, driver 1, class IV 4 = 15 (IPHS adds AYUSH MO, 3+ staff nurses for 24×7 care).

7. Recent reforms & conclusion

Ayushman Bharat (2018): HWCs (renamed Ayushman Arogya Mandir, 2023) with 12 service packages and CHOs, and PM-JAY insurance; Kerala's Aardram Family Health Centres. Conclusion: the PHC remains the heart of India's rural health care, now being transformed into a comprehensive wellness centre.

Q. Describe the measures for improvement of rural sanitation. Predicted
Essay 10 / Short 5 · asked twice as a 2-marker — likely to grow · ~12–25 min
Definition (1m) → Problems & diseases + F-diagram (2m) → 8 measures with sub-points (5m) → Sanitary latrine requirements + diagram (1m) → Programmes + conclusion (1m)

Definition: sanitation prevents human contact with the hazards of wastes; rural sanitation applies this to villages. Problems: open defecation, unsafe wells/ponds, refuse heaps, stagnant sullage, cattle near houses, flies → diarrhoea, cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A/E, polio, worms, stunting. [Fig 5.3 F-diagram]

Measures: (1) safe water — sanitary wells, hand-pumps, chlorination (residual 0.5 mg/L after 1 h), JJM taps; (2) excreta disposal — household sanitary latrines (twin-pit pour-flush, water-seal), sited away from wells; (3) refuse — composting, manure pits, biogas; (4) sullage — soakage pits, kitchen gardens, drains; (5) housing & cattle sheds — ventilation, smokeless kitchens, separate sheds; (6) food hygiene & fly/mosquito control; (7) health education — handwashing, CLTS; (8) community participation & panchayat role.

Sanitary latrine requirements (7 points — no soil, ground- or surface-water contamination, inaccessible to flies/animals, no handling, no odour, cheap & simple). [Fig 5.4 twin-pit latrine]

Programmes: CRSP 1986 → TSC 1999 → NBA 2012 → SBM-Gramin 2014 (ODF declared 2019; Phase II ODF Plus — solid & liquid waste management) + Jal Jeevan Mission 2019. Conclusion: sanitation is the cheapest “vaccine” against faecal-oral disease.

Q. Community health services in urban areas. Predicted
Short note · 5 marks · frequent in question banks

Urban health problems: slums & overcrowding, poor water/sanitation, pollution, NCDs, injuries, migrants without access, high private costs. Services: (1) NUHM network — Urban PHCs/urban AAMs, Urban CHCs, MAS, urban ASHAs, outreach camps & Urban Health & Nutrition Days; (2) municipal & government hospitals, maternity homes, medical colleges; (3) social health insurance — ESI, CGHS, PM-JAY; (4) Jan Aushadhi Kendras for low-cost generics; (5) municipal services — safe water, sewerage, solid waste (SBM-Urban), vector control, food safety; (6) 108 ambulances, blood banks; (7) school health, anganwadis, NGO/private clinics; (8) registration of births & deaths. Conclusion: NUHM is the backbone that links these services for the urban poor.

Staff pattern of PHC

Adverse health effects of open defecation

Faecal-oral spread (diarrhoea, cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A/E, polio), worm infestations & anaemia, child stunting, fly breeding, contamination of water sources, loss of dignity and safety for women.

Dental health education in schools

Brushing twice daily with fluoride paste, rinsing after meals, limiting sweets/sticky snacks, no tobacco/areca, regular dental check-ups; demonstrations with models; school dental screening.

Four factors affecting urban health

Overcrowding & slums; air/water pollution; sedentary lifestyle & junk food (NCDs); road traffic injuries and stress — plus poor access for migrants.

Elements of primary health care

ELEMENTS — Education, Locally endemic disease control, EPI, MCH-FP, Essential drugs, Nutrition, Treatment of common diseases, Safe water & sanitation.
