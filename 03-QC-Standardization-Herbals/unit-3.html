1 The EU Kingdoms: EMA, HMPC, Directives & Definitions

One carpet, 27 member states, one set of herbal rules. This is the "EU guidelines" half of the syllabus line.

🎬 Story mode

Imagine the golden flower wants to be sold in Paris, Berlin and Rome. Without a common law, it would need 27 different passports. The EU solved this like Aladdin's carpet: one law (Directive 2001/83/EC), a special herbal chapter added in 2004 (Directive 2004/24/EC), a wise council of herbal experts (HMPC, inside EMA) and three doors a herbal product can walk through, depending on how much proof it carries.

📘 Exam definitions (Article 1, Directive 2001/83/EC as amended by Directive 2004/24/EC)

Herbal medicinal product (HMP): any medicinal product exclusively containing as active ingredients one or more herbal substances, or one or more herbal preparations, or one or more herbal substances in combination with one or more herbal preparations.

Herbal substances: mainly whole, fragmented or cut plants, plant parts, algae, fungi or lichens in an unprocessed state, usually dried but sometimes fresh. Certain exudates not subjected to specific treatment also count. They are defined by the plant part used and the botanical name (binomial system: genus, species, variety and author).

Herbal preparations: obtained by subjecting herbal substances to treatments such as extraction, distillation, expression, fractionation, purification, concentration or fermentation, e.g. comminuted or powdered herbal substances, tinctures, extracts, essential oils, expressed juices and processed exudates.

Traditional herbal medicinal product (THMP): a herbal medicinal product that fulfils the conditions of Article 16a(1) (long traditional use, safe, OTC-type indication; see below).

⚠️ The word that wins the 2 marks: "exclusively"

If a product mixes a herb with a chemically defined drug (e.g. senna + a synthetic laxative), it is not a herbal medicinal product in EU law. Vitamins and minerals are allowed in a THMP only if their action is ancillary (supporting) to the herb.

Who's who in the EU herbal kingdom

🏛️

EMA

EU agency (founded 1995, now in Amsterdam) that evaluates and supervises medicines. Its committees give scientific opinions; the European Commission grants centralised marketing authorisations.
🌿

HMPC

Created by Directive 2004/24/EC and set up within EMA in September 2004. Prepares EU herbal monographs, the EU list of herbal substances for traditional use, and herbal guidelines; helps harmonise herbal assessment across member states.
📖

Ph. Eur. (EDQM)

Legally binding quality monographs: "Herbal drugs", "Herbal drug preparations", "Herbal drug extracts" + individual herb monographs, plus methods for pesticides, aflatoxins, heavy metals.
⚠️ Classic confusion: two kinds of "monograph"

EU herbal monograph (HMPC) = safety and efficacy: indications, posology, contraindications, for well-established and/or traditional use. Ph. Eur. monograph (EDQM) = quality: identity, purity, assay. Examiners love this difference.

The 5 conditions for a Traditional Herbal Medicinal Product (Art 16a(1))

🙋

1. Self-care indication

Indications suitable for use without a doctor's supervision for diagnosis, prescription or monitoring.
⚖️

2. Fixed strength & posology

Only for use in a specified strength and dosage.
👄

3. Route

Oral, external and/or inhalation only (never injections).
⏳

4. Long use

At least 30 years of medicinal use before the application, including at least 15 years in the EU.
🍏

5. Safe + plausible

Traditional-use data show it is not harmful under the stated conditions, and its effect is plausible from long-standing use.
🏷️

Label must say

It is a traditional herbal medicinal product for the stated indication, exclusively based on long-standing use, and to consult a doctor if symptoms persist.

🆕 Update notes

The transitional period for traditional herbal products already on the EU market ended on 30 April 2011; since then they need registration. "Community herbal monographs" are now called European Union herbal monographs, and the "Community list" is the EU list. Since Brexit the EU has 27 member states.

⭐ Exam tip

For "significance of EU guidelines" (2m): (1) one harmonised legal framework for herbal medicines across all member states, (2) simplified registration lets safe traditional herbs reach the market legally with guaranteed quality, (3) HMPC monographs and Ph. Eur. standards give uniform quality, safety and efficacy benchmarks that other countries (including India's exporters) must meet.

2 EU Guidelines for Quality Control of Herbal Drugs

Whatever door a product uses, the quality check never gets easier. This is the EMA/HMPC "quality" toolbox.

🎬 Story mode

Before the carpet takes off, the Sultan's palace pharmacist checks the cargo: Is it really chamomile and not a look-alike? How was it grown? Which solvent made the extract, and how much herb went into each drop? Will it still be good after two years in a hot cupboard? Each question has its own EU guideline.

📘 Key idea

In the EU the herbal substance or herbal preparation as a whole is regarded as the active substance. Its quality is assured by the European Pharmacopoeia plus a set of EMA/HMPC quality guidelines, and every application must carry a full quality dossier (CTD Module 3) whatever the regulatory route.

The EMA herbal quality guidelines (enumerate these)

📗 Main guidelines (5)

  1. Quality of herbal medicinal products / traditional HMPs: the master guideline (current revision: Rev. 3, 2022).
  2. Specifications: test procedures and acceptance criteria for herbal substances, herbal preparations and HMPs/THMPs.
  3. Good Agricultural and Collection Practice (GACP) for starting materials of herbal origin.
  4. Declaration of herbal substances and herbal preparations in HMPs (how to name them on the label).
  5. Quality of combination herbal medicinal products.

📙 Reflection papers (7)

  1. Markers used for quantitative and qualitative analysis
  2. Stability testing of HMPs/THMPs
  3. Microbiological aspects of HMPs
  4. Use of fumigants
  5. Quality of essential oils as active substances
  6. Level of purification of extracts to be considered herbal preparations
  7. Use of recovered or recycled solvents in manufacture

+ a "Questions & answers on quality of HMPs" document, and EU GMP Annex 7 (manufacture of HMPs).

Three kinds of extract (Ph. Eur.)

🏷️

Declaration guideline

Name the herb by its binomial scientific name + plant part; for extracts state the DER (drug-to-extract ratio, e.g. 4–6 : 1 means 4–6 kg herb gives 1 kg native extract) and the extraction solvent.
🎯

Markers reflection paper

Active markers contribute to the therapeutic activity; analytical markers are used only for identification/assay when the actives are unknown. (Details in Unit 5.)
⏳

Stability

Fingerprint chromatogram + assay of markers over shelf-life. For products with constituents of known therapeutic activity, the assay should not vary by more than ±5 % of the declared value at end of shelf-life (unless justified). Conditions: Unit 4.
🦠

Microbiology & fumigants

Herbal teas, powders and extracts need microbial limits that fit their use; fumigants such as ethylene oxide are not acceptable for treating herbal substances in the EU.
🍄

Mycotoxins

Ph. Eur. limits for herbal drugs: aflatoxin B1 ≤ 2 µg/kg, total aflatoxins (B1+B2+G1+G2) ≤ 4 µg/kg (method in Unit 1).
🧴

Combinations

Each herbal ingredient in a combination must be identified and, where possible, assayed in the finished product; if not possible, justified fingerprints are used.
💊 Pharmacy example: an EU-style label for a valerian tablet

"Each tablet contains 300 mg of extract (as dry extract) from Valeriana officinalis L. root (DER 3–6 : 1). Extraction solvent: ethanol 70 % V/V." (Illustrative label showing the declaration rules: binomial name, part, extract type, DER, solvent.)

3 ICH: The Magic Carpet Linking Three Kingdoms

Why should a medicine be tested three times for Europe, the USA and Japan? ICH said: test once, well, and share.

🎬 Story mode

In the 1980s every country had its own rules, so companies repeated the same animal and human studies for each regulator: wasteful, slow and unkind to animals. In April 1990 regulators and industry from three "kingdoms" (EU, USA, Japan) met in Brussels and wove one carpet: the ICH. Today a single Common Technical Document flies to many regulators.

📘 Exam definition

ICH (originally the International Conference on Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Registration of Pharmaceuticals for Human Use; since 2015 the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use) is a joint initiative of drug regulatory authorities and pharmaceutical industry that develops harmonised technical guidelines on the quality, safety and efficacy of medicines, so that safe, effective and high-quality medicines are developed and registered in the most resource-efficient manner.

Objectives of ICH (KUHS Model Q17)

🧠 Mnemonic: HARMONY
Harmonise technical requirementsAvoid duplicate testingReduce cost & delayMaintain quality, safety, efficacyOne dossier (CTD)New medicines fasterYoke regulators together

"The genie's carpet plays one HARMONY over every kingdom."

🤝

Harmonise

Achieve greater harmonisation in interpreting and applying technical guidelines and registration requirements.
🐁

Avoid duplication

Prevent unnecessary repetition of clinical trials in humans and minimise animal testing without compromising safety and effectiveness.
💰

Save resources

Reduce development cost and time, so medicines reach patients sooner.
✅

Protect standards

Maintain safeguards on quality, safety and efficacy, and the regulatory duty to protect public health.
📦

One dossier

Common Technical Document (ICH M4) accepted by member regulators.
🌍

Global convergence

Promote international regulatory cooperation and use of the same scientific standards.

🆕 Update notes

Q2(R1) → Q2(R2) plus the new Q14 (analytical procedure development) were finalised in November 2023. GCP E6(R2) → E6(R3) was finalised in January 2025. A single consolidated ICH Q1 stability guideline (merging Q1A–Q1E and Q5C) was released as a draft in 2025; until it is final, quote Q1A(R2) in the exam.

Significance of ICH for herbal medicines (2-mark favourite)

🌿 For herbal QC

  • Gives validated, internationally accepted methods (Q2) for HPTLC/HPLC fingerprints and marker assays.
  • Stability rules (Q1) set shelf-life and storage of herbal products.
  • Limits for residual solvents (Q3C) in extracts and for toxic elements (Q3D) such as Pb, Cd, As, Hg.
  • GMP for herbal extracts used as APIs (Q7).

🌏 For trade & research

  • One CTD dossier (M4) for export registration (Unit 4).
  • GCP (E6) for herbal clinical trials; S-series for toxicity studies.
  • Avoids repeating animal and human studies.
  • Raises Indian AYUSH/herbal products to global standards.

4 ICH Quality Guidelines Applied to Herbal Drugs

The heart of the MGR 2022 essay. Learn the table, then the three "zoom-ins": stability, validation, impurities.

🎬 Story mode

The Genie can grant wishes, but even he follows rules: Q1 (keep it stable), Q2 (prove your test works), Q3 (no nasty extras), Q6 (agree what "pass" means), Q7 (make it cleanly). The herbal extract in Rapunzel's bottle must pass every one before the carpet will carry it.

⚠️ Honest scope note (write one line of this, it impresses examiners)

ICH quality guidelines were written mainly for new chemical (and biotech) medicines, and some (e.g. Q3A and Q6A) expressly leave herbal products outside their formal scope. But their principles (stability design, method validation, residual solvents, elemental impurities, GMP, risk management) are applied to herbal drugs by EMA's herbal guidelines, WHO and national regulators. Q7 directly covers herbal extracts used as APIs.

Universal tests: description · identification · assay · impurities. Quality management, personnel, buildings, equipment, documentation, materials management, production & in-process controls, packaging & labelling, storage, laboratory controls, validation, change control, rejection/returns, complaints & recalls, contract manufacturers. 💊 Pharmacy example

An Indian company exporting a standardised Ashwagandha root extract to Europe would: validate its HPLC assay of withanolides (Q2), run stability at 30 °C/75 % RH and 40 °C/75 % RH (Q1A), test hexane/methanol residues (Q3C) and Pb/Cd/As/Hg (Q3D), make it under Q7-type GMP, and file it in CTD format (M4).

5 WHO Research Guidelines for Evaluating the Safety & Efficacy of Herbal Medicines

Snow White's apple looked perfect. The dwarfs needed a method to find out whether it was safe. This WHO book is that method.

🎬 Story mode

The Evil Queen's apple was natural, beautiful and deadly. Grumpy says "It's a plant, it can't hurt!", Doc says "Let's test it first." WHO took Doc's side: before a herbal medicine is used widely or developed into a product, it needs quality checks, pharmacology, toxicity tests in animals and clinical trials in people, done with ethics.

📘 Exam definition / introduction

The Research guidelines for evaluating the safety and efficacy of herbal medicines were published by the WHO Regional Office for the Western Pacific, Manila, in 1993, as the consensus of 17 experts in pharmacology, biochemistry and traditional medicine. They give standard methods for non-clinical and clinical research on herbal medicines so that their safety and efficacy can be proved scientifically. WHO later reproduced their definitions and toxicity guidelines (Annex II) in the General guidelines for methodologies on research and evaluation of traditional medicine (WHO, 2000), together with the Guidelines for the assessment of herbal medicines (finalised in Munich, 1991).

Definitions given in the guidelines (WHO 2000, Annex II)

TermMeaning (in simple words)
Herbal medicinePlant-derived material or preparation with therapeutic or other human health benefits, containing raw or processed ingredients from one or more plants (some traditions also include inorganic or animal materials).
Characterizing compoundA natural constituent used to confirm identity or quality of a plant preparation; it need not be the one responsible for the activity (≈ analytical marker).
Biological activityA change in the baseline function of an animal (or part of it) caused by giving a test substance.
Therapeutic activityAn intervention that improves (ameliorates) the manifestations of human disease.
Processed plant materialsPlant materials treated by traditional procedures to improve safety and/or efficacy, ease clinical use or make preparations (e.g. detoxification "shodhana" of aconite in Ayurveda).
Medicinal preparations of plant materialsPreparations containing powdered plant material, extracts, purified extracts or partially purified actives (sometimes with animal/mineral materials).

The golden rule: how much testing does a herb need?

📜 Long, well-documented traditional use

  • Start with a literature review (classical texts, pharmacopoeias, reviews, databases, related species).
  • Safety may rest on documented experience; a limited set of toxicity tests may be enough.
  • Focus on effects that are hard to see clinically: allergy/immunotoxicity, genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, reproductive toxicity.
  • For minor or non-specific indications, less stringent efficacy proof (e.g. observational studies) may be acceptable.
VS

🆕 New herbal medicine / new indication / new dosage form or route

  • Treated like a conventional new drug.
  • Full quality specification + pharmacology + toxicity studies.
  • Clinical trials Phase I → IV under GCP and the Declaration of Helsinki.
  • New preparation methods can change chemistry, toxicity and pharmacology, so old safety data may not apply.

6 Quality Specifications of Plant Materials & Preparations

Part III, set ①. You cannot test the safety of an apple if you are not sure it IS an apple. Quality comes first.

🎬 Story mode

Remember Flynn Rider's wanted poster with the wrong nose? Everyone was searching for the wrong man! Herbal research has the same danger: if the plant is misidentified, every toxicity and clinical result belongs to the wrong plant. WHO therefore says botanical verification is the first stage in assuring quality, safety and efficacy.

📘 Key principle

A clinical or toxicity study is meaningful only if the test material is botanically authenticated, consistently produced and specified, so that the same quality can be supplied throughout the study and later in the market. Where a pharmacopoeial monograph exists, reference to it is enough; if not, a monograph must be written in pharmacopoeial style.

7 Research Guidelines for Evaluating SAFETY: Toxicity Investigation

Part III, set ③. The dwarfs test the apple on lab animals before Snow White ever takes a bite.

🎬 Story mode

Doc's plan: first see what one big bite does (acute toxicity), then what a small bite every day for months does (long-term toxicity), then whether it harms babies (teratogenicity), genes (mutagenicity) or causes cancer (carcinogenicity). Bashful insists the animals are treated kindly, and Happy records everything.

📘 Exam definitions

Acute toxicity: adverse effects occurring soon after a single dose (or several doses within 24 hours) of a test substance, observed for 7–14 days; gives the approximate lethal dose and signs of toxicity.

Long-term (repeated-dose, sub-acute/sub-chronic/chronic) toxicity: adverse effects of daily repeated administration over a period related to the expected duration of clinical use; identifies target organs, a no-effect dose and reversibility.

WHO says not every test is needed for every herb: choose tests according to the history of use and the intended clinical use.

⚠️ Why two species? Thalidomide!

In India, Schedule Y has been replaced by the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019.

8 Research Guidelines for Evaluating EFFICACY: Pharmacology & Clinical Trials

Part III, set ② + Part II (Phases I–IV). Safe is not enough: the remedy must actually work.

🎬 Story mode

The dwarfs' grandmother swears her honey-and-herb syrup cures coughs. Doc wants proof: does it relax the airways of animals (pharmacodynamics)? Does it help ten coughing miners (Phase II)? Does it beat a dummy syrup in three hundred (Phase III)? And is anyone harmed once the whole kingdom uses it (Phase IV)?

A. Pharmacodynamic & general pharmacological studies

🧫

Choose test systems

In vitro (enzymes, receptors, isolated organs, cell lines) and in vivo animal models that match the traditional claim (e.g. carrageenan paw oedema for an anti-inflammatory herb).
📈

Design

Several doses for a dose–response relationship; vehicle (negative) control and standard drug (positive) control; same route as clinical use.
🫀

General pharmacology

Effects on CNS, cardiovascular, respiratory, GI and renal systems to spot unwanted actions.
⚠️ WHO caution

An effect seen in vitro or in animals is not necessarily applicable to humans. In vitro data mainly support the mechanism; efficacy must be confirmed by clinical studies.

D. Special design issues in herbal trials (WHO 2000)

🧪

Same product throughout

Botanically verified, standardised preparation from defined batches, with quality control and dose standardisation.
🎭

Placebo & blinding

Strong smell/taste (e.g. essential oils) makes a matching placebo hard; use a placebo with similar flavour, a low dose of the same herb, or a positive control. If treatment can't be blinded, at least blind the outcome assessment.
🎲

Randomisation

Essential to control bias, but sometimes impossible or unethical; then choose control groups carefully.
⚖️

Control-group options

Well-established treatment · no treatment · different doses of the same treatment · sham/placebo · full-scale treatment · minimal treatment · alternative treatment.
🧭

Study designs

RCT (highest evidence) · observational studies (good for safety) · single-case designs (patient is own control) · "black-box" (whole traditional package) · ethnographic studies.
📊

Outcomes

Appropriate primary/secondary outcomes, quality of life (WHO-QOL), enough follow-up (e.g. acute pain within 24 h, chronic pain several months), seasonal factors, practitioner skill.
⭐ Efficacy evidence should match the illness

For minor disorders, non-specific indications or prevention, WHO accepts less stringent proof (e.g. well-designed observational studies) together with traditional use and supportive pharmacology. For serious illnesses, randomised controlled trials are expected. Patients already on essential conventional therapy (e.g. cancer) should not have it withdrawn: the herb is studied as an add-on, watching for interactions.

F. Clinical assessment of fixed combinations of herbal preparations

Many traditional remedies (e.g. Triphala, Trikatu, Dashamoola) are fixed combinations. WHO (1991) says old and new combinations must be assessed differently: for traditional combinations, classical texts (Ayurveda, TCM, Unani, Siddha) and experience can serve as evidence; for a new combination of known herbs, the effective dose range and compatibility must be explained and each active ingredient must contribute to efficacy. The EMA/HMPC Guideline on the clinical assessment of fixed combinations of herbal substances/herbal preparations (adopted January 2006) sets the detailed rules:

✅ Requirements

  • Applicant must justify the rationale of the combination.
  • The function of each constituent must be clarified (active substance or excipient, e.g. a taste corrector).
  • Each active must contribute to the claimed indication; symptoms targeted by different herbs must regularly occur together.
  • Dose of each herb and its contribution justified in the clinical overview; very small amounts (e.g. < 10 % of the single dose) are unlikely to contribute.
  • Possible pharmacodynamic/pharmacokinetic interactions between components must be discussed.
  • Well-established combinations: benefit/risk of the combination must equal or exceed that of each herb alone.
  • Traditional combinations: efficacy only needs to be plausible within the traditional system, so safety becomes more critical; vitamins/minerals only ancillary (around RDA levels).

⚖️ Advantages vs disadvantages

  • + Better benefit/risk: additive or potentiated effect allowing lower doses, or higher efficacy with acceptable safety.
  • + One herb counteracts a common adverse effect of another.
  • + Simpler therapy, better compliance (fewer doses, better taste).
  • − A fixed ratio cannot suit every individual patient.
  • − Adverse reactions of each component add up.
  • ✗ Irrational if durations of action differ greatly (unless justified, e.g. one herb enhances absorption of another).
  • ✗ Substances with a narrow therapeutic index are unsuitable.
💊 Pharmacy example

Trikatu (black pepper, long pepper, ginger): piperine increases the absorption (bioavailability) of other drugs, a classic "one component helps another" rationale. But the same property means it may raise blood levels of co-administered drugs, so interactions must be discussed.

🎬 Story mode

📘 Core rule (WHO 2000)

Every clinical trial of herbal/traditional medicine must follow international ethical guidelines for biomedical research on human subjects (Declaration of Helsinki, GCP), be reviewed by an ethics committee, and be conducted within the prevailing law of the country. Where a placebo or unproven treatment is used, rescue treatment should be available.

Voluntary, written, in the patient's language, after explaining purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, alternatives and the right to withdraw at any time.
🏛️

Independent review and approval before starting; monitoring of progress and serious adverse events.
🛡️

Risks minimised and justified by benefits; prior quality and toxicity data; emergency facilities; rescue therapy.
⚖️

🔒

Confidentiality

Patient identity and records protected.
💊

Q. Explain ICH guidelines for quality control of herbal drugs.

1. Introduction

ICH, the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (originally the International Conference on Harmonisation…), brings together drug regulators and the pharmaceutical industry to prepare harmonised scientific and technical guidelines on the quality, safety and efficacy of medicines. Its quality (Q) guidelines provide internationally accepted standards for stability, analytical validation, impurities, specifications and GMP, which are applied to the quality control of herbal drugs.

2. History and members

  • Founded in April 1990 at a meeting in Brussels hosted by EFPIA.
  • Six founders: regulators — European Commission/EU, US FDA, MHLW (Japan); industry — EFPIA, PhRMA, JPMA. Observers: WHO, Health Canada, EFTA.
  • On 23 October 2015 it became an international association (Council) under Swiss law, open to regulators worldwide.

3. Objectives

  • Harmonise technical requirements for registration among regulatory regions.
  • Avoid unnecessary duplication of animal and human studies.
  • Reduce cost and time of drug development; make medicines available sooner.
  • Maintain safeguards on quality, safety and efficacy to protect public health.
  • Common Technical Document (M4) for a single dossier.

4. Categories of ICH guidelines

Q – Quality (chemical and pharmaceutical QA), S – Safety (non-clinical), E – Efficacy (clinical), M – Multidisciplinary (MedDRA, CTD, etc.). [Draw the family tree, Fig 3.5.]

Q. Write about European Medicines Agency (EMA). Enumerate the European guidelines on quality of herbal medicinal products.
Essay · 10 marks · MGR 2023 · ~25 min · ~3 pages

1. European Medicines Agency (EMA)

  • Decentralised agency of the European Union, founded in 1995 (originally EMEA), located in Amsterdam.
  • Functions: scientific evaluation of medicines (centralised procedure), supervision and safety monitoring (pharmacovigilance), scientific advice, guideline development, coordination of national authorities.
  • Scientific committees: CHMP (human medicines), PRAC (pharmacovigilance risk assessment), CVMP (veterinary), COMP (orphan), PDCO (paediatrics), CAT (advanced therapies) and HMPC (herbal medicinal products).

2. Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products (HMPC)

  • Established by Directive 2004/24/EC; set up in September 2004.
  • Prepares EU herbal monographs (therapeutic uses and safe conditions for well-established and/or traditional use).
  • Prepares the EU list of herbal substances, preparations and combinations for traditional use.
  • Drafts guidelines on quality, safety and efficacy of herbal medicines; gives opinions on request.

3. Legal framework and routes

Directive 2001/83/EC (Community code for human medicines) as amended by Directive 2004/24/EC (Traditional Herbal Medicinal Products Directive). A herbal medicinal product is any medicinal product exclusively containing as active ingredients herbal substances and/or herbal preparations. Routes: full MA (Art 8(3)), well-established use ≥ 10 years (Art 10a), traditional use registration ≥ 30 years incl. 15 in EU (Art 16a). [Draw Fig 3.2.] All routes need a full quality dossier.

4. European guidelines on quality of herbal medicinal products

  1. Guideline on quality of herbal medicinal products/traditional herbal medicinal products (current Rev. 3) — requirements for herbal substance, herbal preparation and finished product in CTD Module 3 format.
  2. Guideline on specifications: test procedures and acceptance criteria for herbal substances, herbal preparations and HMPs/THMPs — identity, purity (foreign matter, ash, water, heavy metals, pesticides, mycotoxins, microbes) and assay.
  3. Guideline on Good Agricultural and Collection Practice (GACP) for starting materials of herbal origin.
  4. Guideline on declaration of herbal substances and herbal preparations — binomial name, part, DER, extraction solvent on the label.
  5. Guideline on quality of combination herbal medicinal products.
  6. Reflection papers: markers for quantitative/qualitative analysis; stability testing; microbiological aspects; use of fumigants; quality of essential oils; level of purification of extracts; recovered/recycled solvents.
  7. Supporting standards: European Pharmacopoeia monographs (herbal drugs, herbal drug preparations, extracts — standardised, quantified, other) and EU GMP Annex 7 (manufacture of herbal medicinal products).

5. Key quality principles

  • The herbal substance/preparation as a whole is the active substance.
  • Constituents with known therapeutic activity are standardised; otherwise markers and fingerprints assure consistency.
  • For constituents with known therapeutic activity, assay variation at end of shelf-life should not exceed ±5 % of the declared value unless justified.

Conclusion

EMA, through HMPC and the Ph. Eur., provides a harmonised scientific and legal framework that ensures herbal medicines in all EU member states meet the same standards of quality, safety and efficacy.

Q. What are the guidelines for quality specifications of plant materials and preparations?

1. Introduction

Quality specification is the first of three sets of research guidelines in the WHO Research guidelines for evaluating the safety and efficacy of herbal medicines (WHO Western Pacific Region, Manila, 1993), and also part of the WHO Guidelines for the assessment of herbal medicines (1991). Without an authenticated and consistent test material, no safety or efficacy result is reliable. Where a pharmacopoeial monograph exists, reference to it is sufficient; otherwise a monograph in pharmacopoeial style must be supplied. All procedures follow GMP.

2. Specifications for crude plant material

  • Botanical identity: Latin binomial with authority, family, synonyms, vernacular names.
  • Part used (leaf, root, flower…) and state: fresh, dried or traditionally processed.
  • Source: geographic origin, cultivated or wild, GACP, time and stage of harvest, post-harvest handling.
  • Voucher specimen for each lot, authenticated by a qualified botanist and stored for at least 10 years; lot number on the label.
  • Description: organoleptic, macroscopic and microscopic characters.
  • Identification: chemical tests, TLC/HPTLC fingerprint.
  • Purity: foreign organic matter, total and acid-insoluble ash, extractive values, moisture/LOD, heavy metals, pesticide residues, microbial contamination, aflatoxins, radioactive contamination.
  • Assay: active or characteristic constituents with content limits.

3. Specifications for plant preparations

  • Include powdered material, extracts, tinctures, fatty or essential oils, expressed juices, and fractionated/purified/concentrated preparations.
  • Detailed manufacturing procedure: solvent, drug-to-extract ratio, temperature, duration, concentration steps.
  • Substances added to adjust the content of actives must be declared.
  • Identification and, where possible, assay; if actives are unknown, a characteristic substance or chromatographic fingerprint.
  • Residual solvents, water content, microbial quality.

Q. Describe the WHO research guidelines for evaluating the safety and efficacy of herbal medicines. Predicted
Essay · 10 marks · predicted (asked as 5-mark notes by MGR 2021, 2022, RGUHS 2023) · ~25 min
Introduction (1m) → structure diagram (1m) → definitions (1m) → quality specs (1m) → pharmacodynamics (1m) → toxicity with duration table (2.5m) → clinical evaluation Phase I–IV (2m) → ethics + conclusion (0.5m)

1. Introduction

Published in 1993 by the WHO Regional Office for the Western Pacific (Manila) as the consensus of 17 experts, these guidelines provide standard methods to establish the safety and efficacy of herbal medicines scientifically, both to protect users of widely used remedies and to support development of new products. WHO reproduced the key parts in the General guidelines for methodologies on research and evaluation of traditional medicine (2000). [Draw Fig 3.8.]

2. Structure

Part I: special properties of herbal medicines; Part II: objectives, research protocol and methods for non-clinical studies and Phase I–IV trials; Part III: (a) quality specifications of plant materials and preparations, (b) pharmacodynamic and general pharmacological studies, (c) toxicity investigation.

3. Definitions

Herbal medicine, characterizing compound, biological activity, therapeutic activity, processed plant materials, medicinal preparations of plant materials (see section 5 table).

4. Quality specifications

Botanical identity, voucher specimen, part used, purity tests, assay/fingerprint, manufacturing details of preparations, stability.

5. Pharmacodynamic studies

Test systems relevant to the traditional claim (in vitro and animal models), dose–response, positive and negative controls, general pharmacology on vital systems; animal results must be confirmed in humans.

6. Toxicity investigation

  • Acute: rodent + non-rodent; ≥ 5/sex rodents, ≥ 2/sex non-rodents; oral route; doses within 24 h; observe 7–14 days; autopsy.
  • Long-term: ≥ 10/sex rodents, ≥ 3/sex non-rodents; clinical route; ≥ 3 dose levels + vehicle control; dosed 7 days/week; observations: general signs, body weight, food intake, haematology, liver and kidney function, ECG/ophthalmology, autopsy and histopathology, recovery group.
  • Duration: clinical use < 1 week → 2 weeks–1 month; 1–4 weeks → 4 weeks–3 months; 1–6 months → 3–6 months; > 6 months → 9–12 months.
  • Special tests as needed: reproductive/teratogenicity, genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, immunotoxicity, local tolerance.

7. Clinical evaluation

Phase I (safety, tolerability, dose), Phase II (efficacy and dose in a small patient group), Phase III (large randomised controlled multicentre trials vs placebo/standard), Phase IV (post-marketing surveillance). Herbal issues: standardised product, matching placebo, blinding, choice of controls, observational and single-case designs where appropriate.

8. Ethics

Declaration of Helsinki, GCP, ethics committee approval, informed consent, rescue treatment, humane animal use.

Conclusion

These guidelines convert traditional claims into scientific evidence: herbal medicines must meet the same principles of safety and efficacy as modern drugs, adapted sensibly to their long history of use.

Q. Explain the research guidelines for evaluating the SAFETY of herbal medicines.

Introduction: WHO (WPRO, Manila, 1993) research guidelines; non-clinical toxicity studies indicate the safety of herbal medicines before human use. Q. Research guidelines for evaluation of EFFICACY of herbal medicines.
Short · 5 marks · MGR 2022 · ~12 min

1. Literature review: traditional use (classical texts, pharmacopoeias), published pharmacology and clinical studies, closely related species; evaluate study design (number of patients, diagnosis, dose, duration, criteria, statistics).

2. Pharmacodynamic & general pharmacology studies: test systems relevant to the claim (in vitro: enzymes, receptors, isolated organs; in vivo animal models), dose–response, positive and negative controls. Animal results are indicators only; must be confirmed in humans.

3. Level of evidence matched to indication: minor/non-specific/preventive claims → less stringent evidence (observational studies + traditional use); serious diseases → randomised controlled trials.

4. Clinical trials (Phase I–IV) under GCP: Phase II dose-finding and efficacy, Phase III confirmatory RCTs vs placebo or standard drug, Phase IV surveillance.

5. Herbal design issues: standardised product; placebo with similar taste/smell or low-dose herb or positive control; blinded outcome assessment; randomisation where ethical; controls (standard, no treatment, other doses, placebo, minimal, alternative); outcomes incl. quality of life; adequate follow-up.

6. Combinations: therapeutic rationale for each herb in a mixture.

Q. Give the clinical guidelines on assessment of safety and efficacy of herbal medicine / protocol for clinical trials in herbal medicine / how clinical trials can be performed for herbals (with example).
Short · 5 marks · MGR 2023 · MGR 2021 · AKTU 2022-23 · ~12 min

Prerequisites: botanically authenticated, standardised product (fingerprint/marker); literature and traditional-use review; pre-clinical pharmacology and toxicity data (limited if long safe use is documented); ethics committee approval; registration (CTRI in India); GCP (ICH E6, WHO GCP) and Declaration of Helsinki.

Phases: I — safety, tolerability, dose in a small group; II — efficacy and dose in a few hundred patients; III — large randomised, double-blind, multicentre trials vs placebo or standard drug; IV — post-marketing surveillance.

Protocol headings: title & sponsor; background (product, traditional use, non-clinical data, risks/benefits); objectives; design (randomised, blinded, controls); subject selection (inclusion, exclusion, withdrawal); treatment (dose, regimen, concomitant drugs, compliance); efficacy parameters; safety parameters & AE reporting; statistics (sample size, significance); data handling, monitoring, QA; ethics, informed consent, insurance; publication.

Herbal-specific points: same batch throughout; matching placebo difficult (strong taste/smell) → low-dose herb or active control; blinded assessment; outcomes in traditional and modern terms; quality-of-life measures; possible interactions with concurrent conventional drugs (add-on design).

Example: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of a standardised Ashwagandha root extract for stress — capsules identical in look and smell, primary outcome a validated stress scale, safety by liver/kidney tests and AE reports, 8–12 weeks with follow-up.

Q. Guidelines on clinical assessment of fixed combination of herbal preparations.
Short · 5 marks · MGR 2022 · ~12 min

Source: EMA/HMPC guideline on the clinical assessment of fixed combinations of herbal substances/herbal preparations (adopted 2006); WHO assessment guidelines (1991).

1. General: combinations already in widespread, well-documented use → bibliographic data may suffice; essentially new combinations → data like a new product. The rationale of the combination must be justified. Well-established combinations must rest on valid therapeutic principles; traditional ones must be plausible within the traditional system.

2. Role of each constituent: must be clarified (active or excipient); each active must contribute to the claimed effect.

3. Advantages accepted: better benefit/risk (additive effect at lower doses, or higher efficacy), one herb counteracting a common ADR of another, simpler therapy and compliance. Disadvantages: fixed ratio not ideal for every patient; additive ADRs.

4. Not rational: herbs with very different durations of action (unless justified); herbs with a narrow therapeutic index.

5. Indications: symptoms treated by different herbs must occur together; for traditional combinations, the traditional system can be named in the indication.

6. Dosage: dose of each herb justified; very small amounts (< 10 % of single dose) unlikely to contribute; vitamins/minerals only ancillary (around RDA).

7. PK/PD: interactions between components discussed; for traditional products efficacy is only plausible, so safety evaluation is more critical.

Q. What are the ethical considerations in the research of herbal medicine?
Short · 5 marks · RGUHS 2023 · ~12 min

Basis: Declaration of Helsinki (WMA, 1964, periodically revised), CIOMS international ethical guidelines, ICH/WHO GCP; in India ICMR National Ethical Guidelines (2017) and New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019. WHO (2000): every trial reviewed by an ethics committee and conducted within the law of the country.

  • Ethics committee approval and ongoing monitoring.
  • Informed consent: voluntary, written, in local language, right to withdraw.
  • Risk–benefit: prior quality, pharmacology and toxicity data; qualified investigators; emergency facilities.
  • Placebo use: only when no effective treatment is denied; rescue treatment available; placebo may be unethical/illegal in serious diseases such as cancer; conventional treatment not withdrawn.
  • Justice: fair subject selection; protection of vulnerable groups (pregnant women, children).
  • Confidentiality of patient data; honest reporting; trial registration (CTRI).
  • Compensation/insurance for trial-related injury.
  • Traditional knowledge: prior informed consent of communities and benefit-sharing (Biological Diversity Act 2002, Nagoya Protocol); acknowledge traditional healers.
  • Animal ethics: 3Rs (Replace, Reduce, Refine), institutional animal ethics committee approval.

Mnemonic: CONSENT.

Q. Herb–drug interactions: mechanisms and examples. Predicted
Short · 5 marks · predicted (asked 3× as a 2-mark brief by MGR) · ~12 min

Definition: alteration of the effect of a drug (or herb) when taken together.

Why important: herbs are often self-medicated with prescription drugs; patients rarely tell doctors; drugs with a narrow therapeutic index (cyclosporine, warfarin, digoxin, phenytoin) are dangerous to disturb.

A. Pharmacokinetic: absorption (fibre binding — psyllium + lithium), P-glycoprotein induction/inhibition (St John's wort ↓ digoxin), CYP450 induction (St John's wort ↓ cyclosporine, oral contraceptives) or inhibition (piperine ↑ phenytoin, propranolol; grapefruit ↑ felodipine).

B. Pharmacodynamic: additive/synergistic (ginkgo/garlic + warfarin → bleeding; karela + sulfonylureas → hypoglycaemia; kava + benzodiazepines → sedation; St John's wort + SSRIs → serotonin syndrome) or antagonistic (liquorice raises BP against antihypertensives; ginseng ↓ INR).

Q. What is the EU definition of herbal medicinal product?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Model Q10 · 3 min

As per Directive 2001/83/EC, as amended by Directive 2004/24/EC, a herbal medicinal product is any medicinal product exclusively containing as active ingredients one or more herbal substances, or one or more herbal preparations, or one or more herbal substances in combination with one or more herbal preparations.

  • Herbal substances: whole, fragmented or cut plants, parts, algae, fungi, lichens, mainly unprocessed and dried (e.g. senna leaf).
  • Herbal preparations: obtained by extraction, distillation, expression, fractionation, purification, concentration or fermentation (e.g. tinctures, extracts, essential oils).
Q. What is the objective of ICH guidelines?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Model Q17 · 3 min

ICH (founded 1990; EU, USA, Japan) aims to:

  • Harmonise technical requirements for registration of medicines across regions.
  • Avoid unnecessary duplication of animal and clinical studies.
  • Reduce development cost and time so safe, effective, high-quality medicines reach patients faster.
  • Maintain safeguards on quality, safety and efficacy (Q, S, E, M guidelines; one CTD dossier).
Q. Why is safety evaluation more complex in herbal medicines?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Model Q18 · 3 min
  • Herbs are multi-component mixtures; the active/toxic principle is often unknown.
  • Variable raw material (soil, season, harvest, storage) and misidentification/adulteration.
  • Contaminants: heavy metals, pesticides, microbes, aflatoxins.
  • Herb–drug interactions; self-medication and the belief "natural = safe" cause under-reporting; few toxicity studies, so chronic effects (e.g. aristolochic acid nephropathy) may be missed.
Q. Significance of ICH (guidelines).
Brief · 2 marks · MGR 2021 · RGUHS 2023 · 3 min
  • Single set of internationally accepted standards for quality (Q), safety (S) and efficacy (E) → one CTD dossier for many regulators.
  • Avoids repeated animal and human studies; saves time and cost.
  • For herbals: stability (Q1), validated HPLC/HPTLC methods (Q2), residual solvents (Q3C), heavy metals (Q3D), GMP (Q7), GCP for herbal trials (E6) → global acceptance and export.
Q. Significance of EU guidelines / EU guidelines for quality control of herbal drugs.
Brief · 2 marks · AKTU 2022-23 · RGUHS 2023 · 3 min
  • Legal basis: Directive 2001/83/EC + 2004/24/EC (THMPD); HMPC of EMA issues EU herbal monographs and the EU list.
  • Three routes: full MA, well-established use (≥ 10 years), traditional use registration (≥ 30 years incl. 15 in EU).
  • Quality: Ph. Eur. + EMA guidelines on quality, specifications, GACP, declaration, combinations, markers, stability — a full quality dossier for every route.
  • Significance: uniform quality, safety and efficacy across all member states; legal market for safe traditional herbs; benchmark for exporters.
Q. Examples of herbal drug interaction.
Brief · 2 marks · MGR 2021 · 2022 · 2023 · 3 min
  • St John's wort + cyclosporine/oral contraceptives → ↓ drug levels (CYP3A4 + P-gp induction) → rejection / contraceptive failure.
  • Ginkgo or garlic + warfarin/aspirin → bleeding (antiplatelet).
  • Liquorice + digoxin/diuretics → hypokalaemia, digoxin toxicity.
  • Piperine + phenytoin/theophylline → ↑ blood levels (enzyme inhibition); karela + sulfonylureas → hypoglycaemia.
Q. Test for teratogenicity.
Brief · 2 marks · MGR 2022 · 3 min

Teratogenicity = ability to cause structural malformations in the developing fetus. Segment II (embryo-fetal development) study: pregnant rats (and rabbits) receive the test drug at 3 dose levels + control during organogenesis (rats ≈ GD 6–15; rabbits ≈ GD 6–18); caesarean section a day before term; count corpora lutea, implantations, resorptions, live/dead fetuses, fetal weight; examine fetuses for external, visceral (Bouin's fixation, Wilson's sections) and skeletal (alizarin red S) malformations. Q. Give the protocol for evaluation of drug safety in animals.
Brief · 2 marks · RGUHS 2023 · 3 min
  • Acute toxicity: rodent + non-rodent, both sexes (≥ 5/sex rodents, ≥ 2/sex non-rodents), single/24-h dosing by clinical route, observe 7–14 days, approximate lethal dose, autopsy.
  • Long-term toxicity: ≥ 10/sex rodents, ≥ 3/sex non-rodents, ≥ 3 doses + control, daily dosing for a period linked to clinical use (e.g. 1–6 months use → 3–6 months study); haematology, biochemistry, organ function, histopathology, recovery group.
  • Special: reproductive, genotoxicity, carcinogenicity tests as needed (WHO 1993 guidelines).
Q. Define HMPC and EMA.
Brief · 2 marks · Brainkart bank · 3 min

EMA (European Medicines Agency): EU agency founded in 1995, based in Amsterdam, responsible for scientific evaluation, supervision and safety monitoring of medicines in the EU. HMPC (Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products): EMA committee set up in 2004 under Directive 2004/24/EC; prepares EU herbal monographs, the EU list of traditional herbal substances and herbal guidelines.

Q. Define acute and chronic toxicity studies.
Brief · 2 marks · Brainkart bank · 3 min

Acute toxicity study: evaluates adverse effects after a single dose (or doses within 24 h), with observation for 7–14 days, to find the approximate lethal dose and target signs. Chronic (long-term) toxicity study: evaluates effects of daily repeated dosing for a long period (up to 9–12 months when clinical use exceeds 6 months) in rodent and non-rodent species, to identify target organs, no-effect dose and reversibility.

Q. What is a traditional herbal medicinal product (EU)? Predicted
Brief · 2 marks · predicted · 3 min

A herbal medicinal product registered under the simplified procedure of Directive 2004/24/EC (Art 16a) when: used without medical supervision; fixed strength and posology; oral, external or inhalation route; at least 30 years of medicinal use including 15 years in the EU; shown not harmful, with efficacy plausible from long use. No clinical trials needed, but full quality data are required; label states use is based exclusively on long-standing use.
