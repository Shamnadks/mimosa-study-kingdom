1 What is Health? — Baymax's Body Scan

Health is the starting point of the whole subject. Learn the WHO definition word-perfect — it scores in at least three different questions.

🎬 Story mode

Baymax scans Hiro: temperature, heart rate, a bruise on the toe — that is the physical check. Then he notices Hiro's mood has dropped after losing Tadashi — that is the mental check. Finally his “treatment” is to bring Hiro's friends Go Go, Wasabi, Honey Lemon and Fred around him — that is social health. Baymax never says “no disease found, you are healthy”. He only stops when Hiro is well in all three ways. That is exactly how WHO thinks about health.

📘 Exam definition (write exactly this)

Health is “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.” — World Health Organization (WHO Constitution, 1948)

In recent years a fourth dimension — the ability to lead a socially and economically productive life — and a spiritual dimension have been added to this idea.

⚠️ 1947 or 1948?

Your textbook prints “WHO 1947”. The WHO Constitution was signed in 1946 and came into force on 7 April 1948 (that is why 7 April is World Health Day). Park and most examiners write 1948. Write “WHO (1948)” — nobody will cut marks either way.

✨ Positive health

Perfect functioning of body and mind in their social setting. It is an ideal — a goal we keep moving towards but never fully reach, because life keeps changing.

🌿 Wellness

A multidimensional, active state of positive health shown by good quality of life and a sense of well-being. Health is a state; wellness is the daily lifestyle that builds it.

Changing concepts of health (how the idea grew)

ConceptCore ideaWeakness / what came next
1. BiomedicalHealth = absence of disease. Based on the germ theory; body is a machine, doctor repairs the broken part.Ignores environment, social and cultural causes.
2. EcologicalHealth = dynamic equilibrium between man and his environment; disease = maladjustment of the human to the environment.Better, but still not enough on social factors.
3. PsychosocialHealth is shaped by social, psychological, cultural, economic and political factors — it is both a biological and a social phenomenon.Brought sociology into medicine.
4. HolisticA synthesis of all the above; health is multidimensional. Every sector — agriculture, food, education, housing, industry, public works — affects it. Emphasis on promotion and protection of health.The modern concept (basis of primary health care).

📘 Concept of mental health (asked Nov 2022)

Mental health is a state of well-being in which a person realises his or her own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and can contribute to the community (WHO). It is a state of balance between the individual and the world around — harmony between oneself and others.

A mentally healthy person: is free from internal conflicts · is well adjusted and gets along with others · has a strong sense of self-esteem · knows himself, his needs and goals (self-actualisation) · has good self-control (balances reason and emotion) · faces problems and tries to solve them sensibly · accepts criticism without getting upset.

Determinants of health (asked Nov 2022 — short note)

Determinants are the factors — inside and outside the person — that influence the health of an individual or a community. Health is the result of many of them acting together.

🧬 1. Biological (genetic)

Genes, age, sex, blood group, inherited conditions — e.g. haemophilia, sickle-cell anaemia, Down syndrome. Fixed at conception.

🚬 2. Behavioural & socio-cultural (lifestyle)

Diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol, sleep, hygiene, sexual behaviour; customs and beliefs. Lifestyle diseases (CHD, diabetes, obesity, lung cancer) come from here.

🌳 3. Environment

Physical (air, water, soil, housing, climate, radiation), biological (microbes, vectors, animals) and psychosocial (stress, social support, family).

💰 4. Socio-economic conditions

Economic status (income/GNP), education (especially female literacy), occupation, political system (commitment to health for all).

🏥 5. Health services

Availability, accessibility, affordability and acceptability of care — immunization, antenatal care, safe water, essential medicines.

👵 6. Ageing of the population

More elderly → more chronic diseases and disability (cataract, arthritis, dementia, cancer).

⚧️ 7. Gender

Social roles affect nutrition, education and access to care (e.g. women eating last → anaemia).

📡 8. Other factors

Science & technology, information & communication, food & agriculture, water & sanitation, transport, housing — other sectors shape health too.
📖 Your textbook's 3-box version (also accepted)

1. Host factors (intrinsic) — “host is the soil, the agent is the seed”: age, sex, ethnicity, genetics, blood group; socio-economic status, education, occupation, stress; lifestyle — personality, drugs, alcohol, smoking.
2. Environmental factors (extrinsic) — everything outside the person that he constantly interacts with: physical, biological and psychological components.
3. Risk factors — attributes or exposures significantly associated with developing a disease. They may be causative (smoking → lung cancer), contributory (no exercise → CHD) or predictive (illiteracy → perinatal mortality). A risk factor raises the chance of disease but does not guarantee it.

2 Public Health — Definition, Concepts & Evaluation

Clinical medicine cares for one patient. Public health cares for the whole population — and then checks whether its programmes actually worked.

🎬 Story mode

Baymax can heal one person at a time. But in San Fransokyo, Hiro's microbots only become powerful when thousands of them work together. Public health is the microbot swarm of medicine: doctors, nurses, pharmacists, engineers, teachers and the government all acting together for the city's health — and then, like a good scientist, Hiro tests every invention to see if it really works. That testing step is evaluation.

📘 Exam definition (write exactly this)

Public health is “the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health and efficiency through organised community effort.” — C.E.A. Winslow (1920)

Other short versions: “what we, as a society, do collectively to assure the conditions in which people can be healthy” (Institute of Medicine, USA); the process of mobilising local, state, national and international resources so that people can be healthy.

Public health is interdisciplinary — epidemiology, biostatistics, social sciences, health management, environmental health, community health, behavioural health, health economics, health policy, mental health, health education, occupational safety, gender issues and reproductive health all belong to it. Its tools are surveillance of cases and health indicators and promotion of healthy behaviour (hand-washing, breastfeeding, vaccination, condom distribution, tobacco cessation, obesity education).

Concepts of public health — the four related terms

🛡️ Preventive medicine

“Prevention is better than cure.” Began with protective measures (vaccines, nutrition) for healthy people; later extended to early diagnosis and treatment, and then to treatment aimed at limiting damage.

🏛️ Social medicine

Studies the social determinants of health and fights ill health through social policy. Assumes: health is a birth right · the State is responsible · health and development go together · education promotes health · health begins at home · poverty is a major cause of ill health.

🏘️ Community health

Services that protect the health of a community — sanitation, vector control, immunization, health education — including community diagnosis (surveying and monitoring a community's health needs).

👩‍⚕️ Community medicine

Care delivered at community level — now largely called primary care — by community physicians, nurses and other workers at clinics, health centres and homes.

Modern public health — 4 features

👥

Multidisciplinary

Doctors, nurses, pharmacists, dentists, economists, sociologists, communicators, managers.
🔗

Multisectoral

Health works with water & sanitation, education, transport, agriculture.
📊

Policies decided after objective analysis of data and research.
⚖️

Equity-oriented

The highest possible health for every member of society.

Evaluation of public health (CDC Framework for Program Evaluation)

📘 Definition

Evaluation is the systematic assessment of the merit, worth or significance of a public-health programme — a planned way of learning from experience and using the lessons to improve present activities and plan better for the future (WHO idea).

Why evaluate? To find out whether a programme reached its objectives, to improve it while it runs, to use scarce money wisely, to be accountable to the public and to guide future policy. CDC's guiding principles: use science as the basis for decisions, pursue social equity, perform effectively as a service agency, be outcome-oriented and be accountable.

StepWhat it meansKey elements
1. Engage stakeholdersInvolve people who run, are served by, or will use the evaluationProgramme staff, patients/community, funders, officials
2. Describe the programmeExplain what the programme is and how it is supposed to workNeed, expected effects, activities, resources, stage, context, logic model
3. Focus the evaluation designDecide what to ask and howPurpose, users, uses, questions, methods, agreements
4. Gather credible evidenceCollect information stakeholders will trustIndicators, sources, quality, quantity, logistics; confidentiality

Serves the information needs of intended users.

🧰 Feasibility (3)

Realistic, prudent, diplomatic and frugal.

⚖️ Propriety (8)

🧠 Mnemonic — 6 steps & 4 standards

“Every Doctor Follows Good Judgement Everyday.” Standards: “U F P A — Useful, Feasible, Proper, Accurate.”

📏 What exactly is judged? (Park's evaluation parameters)

Relevance (is the programme needed?) · Adequacy (enough to meet the need?) · Accessibility · Acceptability · Effectiveness (did it achieve objectives?) · Efficiency (results vs cost/effort) · Impact (overall change in health). Park's steps: decide what to evaluate → set standards & criteria → plan methods → gather information → analyse → take action → re-evaluate.

💊 Pharmacy example

Evaluating a hospital's “antibiotic stewardship” drive: stakeholders = doctors, pharmacists, patients; logic model = guidelines → fewer inappropriate prescriptions → less resistance; evidence = prescription audits; conclusion = % reduction; use = share results and update the hospital formulary.

3 Concept of Disease, Prevention & Control — Baymax's Armour Upgrades

This is an essay favourite (Nov 2022) and a 2-marker twice. The natural-history diagram with the levels of prevention is the single most valuable drawing in the whole subject.

🎬 Story mode — the armour levels

Level 0 (Primordial): Hiro teaches the little kids in Aunt Cass's café healthy habits before any risk appears. Level 1 (Primary): Baymax puts on his red armour plates — protection before the fight (vaccines!). Level 2 (Secondary): his scanner spots the villain early and he stops him fast. Level 3 (Tertiary): after the battle he helps the injured walk again — rehab. Level 4 (Quaternary): Baymax refuses to give Hiro medicine he does not need. The earlier the level, the cheaper and kinder the cure.

📘 Definitions

Disease — a condition in which the structure or function of the body (or part of it) is disturbed or deranged, not due to an immediate external injury, usually with specific signs and symptoms. Ecologically, disease is a maladjustment of the human organism to its environment.

Disease prevention — measures not only to prevent the occurrence of disease (such as risk-factor reduction) but also to arrest its progress and reduce its consequences once established (WHO, Health-for-All glossary, 1984).

Disease control — ongoing operations aimed at reducing the incidence, the duration (and so the risk of transmission), the effects of infection (physical and psychosocial) and the financial burden to the community.

🌱 Health promotion interventions

Health education · environmental modifications (safe water, sanitation, housing) · nutritional interventions · lifestyle & behavioural change. Not aimed at a single disease — it strengthens the host.

🛡️ Specific protection interventions

Immunization · specific nutrients (iodine, vitamin A, iron) · chemoprophylaxis · protection against occupational hazards, accidents, carcinogens · avoiding allergens · controlling environmental hazards (air, noise) · quality control of foods, drugs and cosmetics.

“Hiro Saves Everyone, Drives Robots.” And the levels are just numbers: 0 = primordial, 1 = primary, 2 = secondary, 3 = tertiary, 4 = quaternary.

Successful prevention depends on: knowledge of causation · dynamics of transmission · identification of risk factors and risk groups · development and evaluation of preventive procedures · facilities to deliver them · availability of prophylactic, early-detection and treatment measures.

Control of disease — from control to eradication

📉Controlreduce incidence/prevalence to a level where it is no longer a major public-health problem
🚧Eliminationinterrupt transmission in a defined large region, or bring cases below a set threshold (“elimination as a public-health problem”)
🏆Eradicationtermination of all transmission worldwide by exterminating the agent — permanent
🌍

Eradicated

Smallpox — the only human disease eradicated (WHO declaration, 1980). India became smallpox-free in 1977.
🇮🇳

Eliminated in India

Polio (last case Jan 2011; certified polio-free 2014), maternal & neonatal tetanus, yaws; leprosy eliminated as a public-health problem (<1 per 10,000) in 2005.
🎯

Targeted next

TB, malaria, kala-azar, lymphatic filariasis, measles–rubella (see Units 2 & 3).

🚪 Isolation

  • For the sick (infected) person
  • Separated for the period of communicability
  • e.g. an Ebola patient in a special ward
VS

⏳ Quarantine

  • For well persons who were exposed
  • Movement restricted for up to the longest usual incubation period
  • e.g. contacts of an Ebola case watched for 21 days
⚠️ Classic confusions

(1) Elimination is regional; eradication is global and permanent. (2) Screening is secondary prevention, not primary. (3) Rehabilitation is tertiary. (4) Immunization is specific protection (primary), not health promotion.

💊 Pharmacy example — one disease, all levels

Type 2 diabetes: primordial — school programmes against junk food; primary — diet, exercise, weight control; secondary — fasting sugar screening at a pharmacy camp + metformin; tertiary — foot care, retinal checks, insulin to prevent complications; quaternary — the pharmacist's medication review removes duplicate or unnecessary drugs.

4 Social Causes of Disease & Social Problems of the Sick

Germs and genes are only part of the story. Where people live, learn, work and play decides who falls sick — and sickness, in turn, damages a family's social life.

🎬 Story mode

After the fire, Hiro shut himself in his room: no school, no friends, no food. Nothing was wrong with his lungs or heart, yet he was clearly unwell. Baymax's medicine was social — friends, purpose, a team. In real life the arrow also points the other way: poverty, loneliness and discrimination cause disease, and disease causes poverty, loneliness and discrimination.

Social causes of disease

📘 Social determinants of health

The conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age — shaped by money, power and resources — such as income, education, employment, housing, gender, social support and access to health care. They are the main drivers of health inequities.

🏚️RISK CONDITIONSpoverty · low education/occupational status · dangerous, stressful work · polluted environment · discrimination · low political & economic power · large income gaps · poor housing · no access to cheap healthy food
⚠️RISK FACTORSPhysiological: hypertension, high cholesterol, weak immunity, genetic factors · Behavioural: smoking, alcohol, poor diet, no exercise · Psychosocial: isolation, no social support, low self-esteem, self-blame, low perceived control, hopelessness
🩺HEALTH STATUSmore disease, disability and early death

Examples of social determinants (textbook list): income level · educational opportunities · occupation, employment and workplace safety · gender inequity · racial/caste segregation · food insecurity · housing and utilities · early-childhood experiences · social support and inclusion · crime and violence · transport · neighbourhood conditions · safe water, clean air, toxin-free surroundings · recreation and leisure.

8 Nutritional Deficiencies & Malnutrition — and How to Prevent Them

Essay in Dec 2023, short notes in May 2022 (PEM) and Nov 2022 (malnutrition). Learn the kwashiorkor–marasmus table cold.

🎬 Story mode

Remy's father and brother Emile happily eat garbage — lots of food, little nutrition. Some rats are thin and starving; Emile is round but still poorly nourished. That is malnutrition in both directions: under-nutrition and over-nutrition, and even “hidden hunger” where calories are enough but vitamins and minerals are missing.

📘 Definitions

Nutritional deficiency — a state in which the body does not get or absorb enough of a nutrient, leading to specific disease (digestive problems, skin disorders, defective bone growth, anaemia, even dementia).

Malnutrition — deficiencies, excesses or imbalances in a person's intake of energy and/or nutrients (WHO). It covers undernutrition (wasting, stunting, underweight), micronutrient-related malnutrition (deficiency or excess of vitamins/minerals) and overweight/obesity with diet-related NCDs.

📉

Wasting

Low weight-for-height — recent, acute food shortage or illness.
📏

Stunting

Low height-for-age — chronic, long-term undernutrition.
⚖️

Underweight

Low weight-for-age — a mix of both.

India today (NFHS-5, 2019–21), children under 5: stunted 35.5% · wasted 19.3% · underweight 32.1%; about two-thirds of young children (≈67%) and over half of women (≈57%) are anaemic — so the “double burden” (undernutrition + rising obesity) is very real.

General symptoms of nutritional deficiency

Pallor · fatigue & weakness · breathlessness · unusual food cravings (pica) · hair loss · light-headedness/fainting · constipation · sleepiness · palpitations · depression · tingling/numbness · menstrual problems · poor concentration.

Causes

🍽️ Direct

  • Inadequate intake of food or a specific nutrient
  • Malabsorption (diarrhoea, worms, coeliac/Crohn's disease)
  • Increased need — pregnancy, lactation, growth, illness
  • Increased loss — bleeding, infections

🏘️ Underlying (social)

  • Poverty & food insecurity
  • Ignorance, food taboos, poor feeding practices
  • Large families, short birth spacing
  • Alcoholism, mental illness, frailty in the elderly
  • Poor sanitation → repeated infections

Types of deficiency (textbook): mineral · vitamin · protein · lipid (essential fatty acid) deficiency.

Protein–energy malnutrition (PEM)

PEM is a group of conditions in young children (mostly 6 months – 3 years, after weaning) caused by a deficiency of protein and/or calories, usually worsened by infections. Its two ends are marasmus and kwashiorkor; in between lies marasmic kwashiorkor.

🦴 Marasmus — “skin and bones”

  • Deficiency of calories (energy) + protein
  • Usually <1 year (early weaning, bottle-feeding)
  • No oedema
  • Severe wasting of muscle & fat; weight <60% of expected
  • “Old man” wrinkled face
  • Alert, hungry, good appetite
  • Hair & skin changes rare
  • Liver not enlarged; albumin near normal
VS

🎈 Kwashiorkor — “the sickness of the displaced child”

  • Mainly protein deficiency (carbohydrate-rich diet)
  • 1–4 years (after a new baby arrives)
  • Pitting oedema (feet → face) — “moon face”
  • Weight 60–80% (oedema hides the wasting)
  • Apathetic, irritable, poor appetite
  • Flaky-paint dermatosis; flag sign in hair (light & dark bands)
  • Fatty, enlarged liver; low serum albumin

Grading (Gomez, weight-for-age): normal >90% of reference · 1st degree 75–89% · 2nd degree 60–74% · 3rd degree <60%. WHO severe acute malnutrition (6–59 months): weight-for-height < −3 SD, or MUAC < 11.5 cm, or bilateral pitting oedema — treated in Nutritional Rehabilitation Centres (NRCs).

11 Impact of Urbanization on Health & Disease — San Fransokyo

A favourite 2-marker (May 2022 + model paper). Remember: the city is a two-edged sword.

🎬 Story mode

San Fransokyo has the best robotics lab in the world and a hospital on every block — but also crowded alleys, bot-fight gangs, traffic and smog. Cities give health with one hand and take it away with the other. That two-edged picture is your whole answer.

📘 Definitions

Urban area (India) — all places with a municipality, corporation, cantonment board or notified town-area committee, and other places meeting census criteria (population ≥5,000, density ≥400 per km² and ≥75% of male main workers in non-agricultural work).

Urbanization — the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas, together with the physical and social changes this brings. Movement to cities is one of the main population changes of the 21st century.

Urban population grows through three factors: (1) natural increase (births) · (2) rural-to-urban migration for jobs, education, health care, transport and entertainment · (3) inclusion of new areas as “urban”.

👍 Positive impacts

  • Easy access to hospitals, specialists, emergency care & pharmacies
  • Better water supply, sanitation, electricity
  • Education, jobs, higher income
  • Better health awareness; lower IMR and higher life expectancy on average
VS

👎 Negative impacts

  • Slums, overcrowding, poor sanitation
  • Pollution, traffic, noise, heat
  • Junk food, sedentary life, stress
  • Crime, addiction, social isolation

The 8 health effects of rapid urbanization (textbook order)

1. 🦠 Infectious diseases

Polluted water → diarrhoea, typhoid, hepatitis A/E; stagnant water → dengue, chikungunya, urban malaria (Anopheles stephensi); crowding → TB, respiratory infections; HIV/STIs.

2. 🍔 Non-communicable diseases

Junk food, tobacco, alcohol, inactivity, stress → obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, cancer.

3. 💉 Substance abuse

Among slum children (to dull hunger) and affluent teenagers (fashion, peer pressure); adults with frustration and unemployment.

4. 🚗 Road accidents, injuries, violence & crime

More vehicles, reckless and drunk driving; unemployment and high cost of living push some towards crime.

5. 🏚️ Housing, security & sanitation

Unregulated slums on riverbanks, hillsides or near factories → floods, landslides, injuries, respiratory and mental illness, malnutrition.

6. 🚱 Water, food & nutrition crisis

13 Hygiene & Health — Personal Hygiene and Health Care

Asked in three of the four KUHS papers. Easy marks if you organise it body-part by body-part.

🎬 Story mode (a guest from the forest)

In Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, Snow White refuses to serve supper until the dwarfs wash — faces, hands, behind the ears — while Grumpy protests. She was running a little public-health campaign! Personal hygiene is exactly that: small daily habits that keep germs out and confidence in.

📘 Definitions

Hygiene — from Hygieia, the Greek goddess of health; the science of health and its preservation — “the art of healthy living”.

Personal hygiene — the practices an individual follows to keep the body clean and well-groomed and to preserve health: bathing, hand-washing, oral care, clean clothes, and safe, hygienic behaviour around others.

Why it matters: prevents infections (diarrhoea, worms, skin and eye infections, respiratory illness) · stops spread to others · prevents bad odour · improves appearance, self-esteem and social acceptance · good habits learnt early last a lifetime.

Components of personal hygiene

🙌

Hand hygiene

Hands carry the most germs to eyes, nose, mouth, wounds. Wash with soap and running water — palms, between fingers, under nails, up to wrists — rinse and dry with a clean towel.
💅

Nail hygiene

Dirt under nails enters with food. Keep nails short and clean; don't bite them or share clippers.
😊

Face hygiene

Sweat and dust clog pores → pimples, tired skin. Wash face on waking and whenever sweaty.
🦷

Oral hygiene

Brush twice daily (morning & bedtime), floss, mouthwash, clean the tongue, dentist every 6 months — prevents caries, gingivitis, bad breath.
🚿

Body (skin) hygiene

Sweat and dead cells → odour, scabies, ringworm, pimples. Bathe daily with soap, dry well, clean underwear.
💇‍♀️

Hair hygiene

Excess oil and dandruff. Shampoo regularly, massage the scalp, rinse well; don't share combs (lice).
👂

Ear hygiene

Wax collects dust and can block hearing. Clean the outer ear after a bath; never push cotton buds deep; see a doctor if blocked.
🦶

Foot hygiene

Shoes all day → sweat, odour, fungal infection. Wash and dry feet, air shoes, wear footwear outdoors (hookworm!).
🍱

Food hygiene

Check expiry dates, wash fruits and vegetables, refrigerate properly, keep the kitchen clean, cover food from flies.
🤧

Respiratory hygiene

Cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue or elbow, wear a mask when ill, wash hands after coughing.
🛌

Sickness hygiene

When sick, stay home and avoid crowds until recovered — respect others' health.
👕

Clothes hygiene

Inner clothes absorb sweat. Change daily, wash with detergent, sun-dry and iron (heat kills germs).

Also include: menstrual hygiene (clean sanitary pads changed every 4–6 hours, safe disposal) · eye care (no rubbing, screen breaks) · sleep 7–8 hours · good posture · toilet hygiene (use sanitary latrines, no open defecation).

Q. Explain in detail on various vitamin deficiency disorders.
Essay · 10 marks · KUHS May 2022 (also Short “Vitamin deficiencies” Nov 2022) · ~25 min · ~3 pages
Introduction + definition (1m) → classification fat/water-soluble (1m) → fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K (3m, with xerophthalmia staircase) → water-soluble B-complex & C (3.5m, table) → general prevention + conclusion (1.5m)

1. Introduction

Vitamins are organic compounds required in very small amounts in the diet for normal growth, metabolism and maintenance of health. The body cannot synthesise most of them in adequate quantities, so they must come from food. When a vitamin is lacking in the diet for a long period, or is not absorbed or utilised, a specific vitamin deficiency disorder develops. The term was coined by Casimir Funk from “vital amine”.

2. Classification

  • Fat-soluble vitamins — A, D, E, K: absorbed with fat, stored in liver and adipose tissue; deficiency develops slowly; excess may be toxic.
  • Water-soluble vitamins — B-complex and C: not stored (except B12), lost in urine and cooking water; must be taken daily.

3. Deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins

(a) Vitamin A (retinol) → night blindness and xerophthalmia. Earliest symptom is night blindness (XN). Progression (WHO): conjunctival xerosis (X1A) → Bitot's spots (X1B) → corneal xerosis (X2) → corneal ulceration/keratomalacia (X3A, X3B) → corneal scar (XS); xerophthalmic fundus (XF). Also follicular hyperkeratosis, poor growth and lowered resistance to infection. Sources: liver, fish-liver oil, eggs, milk, carrots, papaya, mango, green leafy vegetables. Prevention: Vitamin A prophylaxis — 1 lakh IU at 9 months with measles–rubella vaccine, then 2 lakh IU every 6 months up to 5 years (9 doses); diet education.

[Draw the xerophthalmia staircase — Fig 1.6.]

(b) Vitamin D (calciferol) → rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults. Rickets: delayed closure of fontanelles, frontal bossing, rickety rosary, pigeon chest, Harrison's groove, bow legs/knock knees, delayed teething. Osteomalacia: bone pain, muscle weakness, fractures. Sources: sunlight on skin, fish-liver oil, egg yolk, fortified milk. Prevention: sun exposure, fortified foods, vitamin D and calcium supplements.

(c) Vitamin E (tocopherol) → haemolytic anaemia in premature infants, peripheral neuropathy; reduced fertility in experimental animals. Sources: vegetable oils, wheat germ, nuts.

(d) Vitamin K → defective clotting (low prothrombin) and bleeding; haemorrhagic disease of the newborn. Prevention: vitamin K 1 mg IM to every newborn; green leafy vegetables.

Q. Discuss the concept of prevention and control of diseases with examples.
Essay · 10 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 (Brief: Dec 2023 & Model) · ~25 min · ~3 pages
Definitions (1.5m) → natural history diagram (2m) → 4+1 levels of prevention with examples (3.5m) → disease control: control/elimination/eradication + methods (2m) → conclusion (1m)

1. Definitions

Disease prevention covers measures not only to prevent the occurrence of disease, such as risk-factor reduction, but also to arrest its progress and reduce its consequences once established (WHO, 1984). Disease control is the ongoing operation aimed at reducing the incidence of disease, its duration and risk of transmission, its physical and psychosocial effects, and the financial burden on the community.

Successful prevention depends on knowledge of causation, dynamics of transmission, identification of risk factors and risk groups, and availability of prophylactic, early-detection and treatment measures.

2. Natural history of disease

Every disease passes through a pre-pathogenesis phase (interaction of agent, host and environment before the person is affected) and a pathogenesis phase (early changes → clinical disease → recovery, disability or death). Prevention can be applied at each stage.

[Draw Fig 1.4 — the natural-history bar with the levels of prevention below.]

3. Levels of prevention

(a) Primordial prevention — prevents the emergence of risk factors in populations where they have not yet appeared. Intervention: individual and mass education, national policies. Example: school programmes against junk food and smoking; promoting physical activity.

(b) Primary prevention — action taken before the onset of disease (pre-pathogenesis). Modes: health promotion (health education, environmental modification, nutrition, lifestyle change) and specific protection (immunization, specific nutrients, chemoprophylaxis, protection from occupational hazards, accidents and carcinogens). Examples: measles–rubella vaccine, iodised salt, chloroquine prophylaxis, helmets.

(c) Secondary prevention — halts the disease at its early stage and prevents complications. Modes: early diagnosis (screening, case-finding) and adequate treatment. Examples: Pap smear/VIA for cervical cancer, BP and sugar screening, sputum testing and treatment of TB, MDT for leprosy.

(d) Tertiary prevention — all measures to limit impairment and disability and help the patient adjust (late pathogenesis). Modes: disability limitation and rehabilitation (medical, vocational, social, psychological). Examples: physiotherapy after stroke, reconstructive surgery and MCR footwear in leprosy, cardiac rehabilitation.

(e) Quaternary prevention — protects patients from over-medicalisation and suggests ethically acceptable interventions. Example: avoiding unnecessary antibiotics and scans; deprescribing.

4. Control of disease

Control is a continuum: control (reduce to a level where it is no longer a major problem) → elimination (interrupt transmission in a large region or reduce below a defined threshold — e.g. leprosy eliminated as a public-health problem in India in 2005; polio-free certification in 2014) → eradication (termination of all transmission worldwide by extermination of the agent — smallpox, declared eradicated in 1980).

Methods (break the chain of transmission):

  • Against the source/reservoir: early diagnosis, notification, isolation, treatment, quarantine, disinfection.
  • Against the route: safe water, sanitation, food hygiene, vector control, hand hygiene.
  • Protecting the susceptible host: immunization, chemoprophylaxis, nutrition, health education.

Example — cholera: isolate and treat cases with ORS and antibiotics (source), chlorinate water and promote hand-washing (route), oral cholera vaccine in high-risk areas (host).

Conclusion

Prevention is cheaper and kinder than cure. Applying the right level of prevention at the right stage of the natural history, together with sustained control programmes, has already eradicated smallpox and eliminated polio — and can do the same for TB, malaria and filariasis.

Definition: Protein–energy malnutrition (PEM) is a range of pathological conditions arising from a lack of protein and/or energy (calories) in varying proportions, seen mostly in children 6 months–3 years and often precipitated by infections.

Q. Determinants of health.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 · ~12 min

Definition: Determinants of health are the predisposing factors — personal, social, economic and environmental — that influence the health of an individual or a community.

  1. Biological (genetic): genes, age, sex, blood group; inherited disorders like haemophilia, sickle-cell anaemia.
  2. Behavioural & socio-cultural: lifestyle — diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol, hygiene; customs and beliefs.
  3. Environmental: physical (water, air, housing), biological (microbes, vectors) and psychosocial (stress, social support).
  4. Socio-economic: income, education, occupation, political system.
  5. Health services: availability, accessibility and quality of care.
  6. Ageing of the population — more chronic diseases.
  7. Gender — differential access to food, education and care.
  8. Other factors — food & agriculture, water & sanitation, information and communication, science and technology.

Textbook model: host factors (intrinsic: age, sex, genetics, socio-economic status, lifestyle), environmental factors (extrinsic: physical, biological, psychological) and risk factors (causative, contributory, predictive).

Conclusion: health is the result of the interaction of all these determinants, so improving health requires action by many sectors, not by medicine alone.

Q. Malnutrition.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 · ~12 min

Definition: Malnutrition refers to deficiencies, excesses or imbalances in a person's intake of energy and/or nutrients (WHO) — it results from nutrient deficiency or over-consumption.

Types: (1) Undernutrition — wasting (low weight-for-height), stunting (low height-for-age), underweight (low weight-for-age); (2) micronutrient deficiency — iron, iodine, vitamin A, zinc (“hidden hunger”); (3) overnutrition — overweight and obesity.

Signs: undernutrition — weight loss, loss of fat and muscle, hollow cheeks, sunken eyes, swollen abdomen, dry hair and skin, delayed wound healing, fatigue, irritability, poor concentration, depression; overnutrition — overweight, obesity, often with low vitamin levels.

Causes: food insecurity · digestive problems and malabsorption · excessive alcohol · mental-health disorders · inability to obtain and prepare food (elderly, frail) · infections · poverty and ignorance.

Prevention: modern agriculture, nutrition education, enrichment and fortification of food, biofortified crops (golden rice), government feeding programmes (ICDS, PM POSHAN, POSHAN Abhiyaan), cheap staple foods (PDS), dietary variety, probiotics, IFA and vitamin A supplementation, deworming, and control of infections.

Conclusion: India faces a double burden — NFHS-5 shows 35.5% of under-5s stunted while adult obesity rises — so both under- and over-nutrition must be tackled.

Q. Evaluation of public health · Explain on concepts and evaluation of public health.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 & Dec 2023 · ~12 min

Public health (Winslow, 1920) is the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health and efficiency through organised community effort.

Concepts: four related terms — preventive medicine, social medicine, community health and community medicine. Modern public health is multidisciplinary, multisectoral, evidence-based and equity-oriented. Its functions are assessing population health, planning and evaluating programmes, dealing with environmental hazards and communicating to promote health.

Evaluation is the systematic assessment of the merit, worth or significance of a public-health programme, used to improve it and plan better. CDC's framework has six steps:

  1. Engage stakeholders — people involved in or affected by the programme.
  2. Describe the programme — need, expected effects, activities, resources, stage, context, logic model.
  3. Focus the evaluation design — purpose, users, questions, methods, agreements.
  4. Gather credible evidence — indicators, sources, quality and quantity of data.
  5. Justify conclusions — compare with agreed standards; interpret and judge.
  6. Ensure use and share lessons learned — feedback, follow-up, dissemination.

Four standards guide every step: Utility, Feasibility, Propriety, Accuracy.

Q. Explain on socio-cultural factors related to health and disease.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Dec 2023 · ~12 min

Introduction: Social and cultural factors are closely linked with every life process, including health and disease. Socio-cultural factors are the beliefs, customs, traditions, family patterns, education, income, occupation and habits of people that influence their health and health-seeking behaviour.

  1. Customs and traditions — discarding colostrum, prelacteal feeds (harmful); prolonged breastfeeding (helpful).
  2. Beliefs and superstitions — mental illness or epilepsy attributed to spirits → delay in treatment.
  3. Religion — dietary rules, fasting, prohibition of alcohol; vaccine hesitancy in some groups.
  4. Food habits and taboos — avoiding nutritious foods in pregnancy; polished rice → beriberi; maize → pellagra.
  5. Family and marriage — joint families support the sick; child marriage → teenage pregnancy; consanguinity → genetic disease.
  6. Status of women — son preference, women eating last → anaemia and neglect of girls.
  7. Education — literacy, especially female literacy, improves health (e.g. Kerala).
  8. Income, occupation and housing — poverty, occupational diseases, overcrowding.
  9. Personal habits — tobacco, alcohol, open defecation, hand-washing.
  10. Stigma — hiding HIV, TB, leprosy, mental illness.

Conclusion: health programmes succeed only when they respect culture, build on healthy customs and use health education to change harmful ones.

Q. Balanced diet.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Jul 2023 & Model paper · ~12 min

Definition: A balanced diet is one that contains a variety of foods in such quantities and proportions that the need for energy, proteins, fats, carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals and other nutrients is adequately met for maintaining health, vitality and well-being, with a small extra provision for short periods of leanness.

Seven components: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, fibre, vitamins, minerals and water.

Q. What are the impacts of urbanization on health? · What is the impact of urbanisation on health?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS May 2022 & Model · ~3 min

Urbanization is the increasing proportion of people living in towns and cities. Its effect on health is two-edged: better access to hospitals, water and sanitation, but also (1) infectious diseases from polluted water, vectors and crowding (diarrhoea, dengue, TB); (2) NCDs from junk food, inactivity and stress (diabetes, hypertension); (3) substance abuse; (4) road accidents, violence and crime; (5) slums and poor sanitation; (6) water and food crisis; (7) air pollution and urban heat; (8) social isolation and mental stress.

Q. Mention avoidable habits · What are avoidable habits?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS May 2022 & Nov 2022 · ~3 min

Avoidable habits are harmful behaviours that a person can choose to stop: smoking and tobacco chewing · alcohol · drug abuse · not drinking enough water · eating late at night or at the desk · too much salt · junk food · lack of exercise · inadequate sleep · neglecting mental health · not protecting hearing · self-medication/antibiotic misuse · nail-biting and spitting · open defecation. They are major risk factors for lifestyle diseases such as cancer, hypertension, diabetes and heart disease.

Q. Explain health · Define the term ‘Health’.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS May 2022 & Model · ~3 min

WHO (1948) defines health as “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.” Health is therefore multidimensional — physical, mental, social (and spiritual) — and lies on a spectrum from positive health to death. Positive health means perfect functioning of body and mind, an ideal to strive for; wellness is the active lifestyle that promotes it. (Draw the triad Venn if time permits.)

Q. Name the indicators of health · What are the indicators of health?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 & Model · ~3 min

Indicators are variables that measure changes in health status. They are: (1) mortality indicators — IMR, MMR, life expectancy, crude death rate; (2) morbidity indicators — incidence, prevalence; (3) disability rates — DALY; (4) nutritional status — LBW, stunting; (5) health-care delivery — doctor–population ratio; (6) utilization rates — immunization coverage; (7) social and mental health; (8) environmental; (9) socio-economic — literacy, per-capita income; (10) health policy; (11) quality of life — PQLI, HDI; (12) others (social, basic needs, Health-for-All indicators).

Q. Concept of mental health.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 · ~3 min

Mental health is a state of well-being in which a person realises his or her abilities, copes with normal stresses of life, works productively and contributes to the community — a state of balance between the individual and the surrounding world. A mentally healthy person is free from internal conflicts, well adjusted, has good self-esteem and self-control, knows his needs and goals, and faces problems intelligently. It is promoted under the National Mental Health Programme (Unit 3).

Q. Explain the concept of prevention and control of diseases · Concept of prevention of disease.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Dec 2023 & Model · ~3 min

Prevention covers measures to prevent the occurrence of disease and to arrest its progress and reduce its consequences once established (WHO). Levels: primordial (prevent risk factors — policies, education), primary (health promotion + specific protection, e.g. vaccines), secondary (early diagnosis and treatment, e.g. screening), tertiary (disability limitation and rehabilitation). Control means reducing incidence, duration, effects and cost of disease; its highest stages are elimination (regional) and eradication (global — smallpox).

Q. What are the social problems of the sick?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Jul 2023 · ~3 min

“Sick” means affected by physical or mental illness, and sickness creates problems in society: poverty and loss of income · unemployment · catastrophic medical expenses · stigma and discrimination (TB, leprosy, HIV, mental illness) · social isolation · family disruption and caregiver burden · malnutrition and compromised immunity · inadequate health system and lack of funding · misuse of antibiotics/antivirals and pathogen evolution · disrupted social system.

Q. What are the deficiency disorders of Vitamin C and Vitamin D?
Brief · 2 marks · Model paper · ~3 min

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) → scurvy: swollen, spongy, bleeding gums; perifollicular haemorrhages; poor wound healing; bone pain; infantile scurvy. Sources: amla, guava, citrus.
Vitamin D (calciferol) → rickets in children (bow legs, rickety rosary, pigeon chest, frontal bossing, delayed fontanelle closure) and osteomalacia in adults (bone pain, muscle weakness). Prevention: sunlight, fortified milk, fish-liver oil, supplements.

🎁 Bonus 2-markers from the question banks (Beriberi, Night blindness, Marasmus, Rickets, Goitre, Anaemia, IMR…)

Beriberi — vitamin B1 (thiamine) deficiency; dry beriberi = peripheral neuritis and muscle wasting; wet beriberi = oedema and cardiac failure; infantile beriberi in breast-fed infants of deficient mothers. Common with polished-rice diets and alcoholism.

Night blindness — earliest sign of vitamin A deficiency (XN): inability to see in dim light because rhodopsin cannot be regenerated. Prevented by vitamin A prophylaxis and GLV, carrots, liver.

Marasmus — severe calorie + protein deficiency in infants: extreme wasting, “old-man face”, no oedema, alert and hungry, weight <60% of expected.

Rickets — vitamin D deficiency in children → soft, deformed bones: bow legs, knock knees, rickety rosary, pigeon chest, frontal bossing, delayed teething.

Goitre — enlargement of the thyroid gland due to iodine deficiency; part of iodine-deficiency disorders; prevented by iodised salt (NIDDCP).

Nutritional anaemia — Hb below normal for age and sex due to lack of iron, folate or B12 (WHO: <13 g/dL men, <12 women, <11 pregnant women and children 6–59 months). Prevented by IFA supplementation and deworming (Anaemia Mukt Bharat).

Infant mortality rate — number of deaths of infants under one year of age in a year per 1000 live births in the same year; a sensitive indicator of a community's health and socio-economic status.

Social causes of disease — poverty, low education, unemployment, poor housing, discrimination, polluted environment and lack of healthy food, acting through physiological, behavioural and psychosocial risk factors.

Factors of poverty affecting health — poor diet, high child mortality, bad housing and sanitation, no access to care and insurance, chronic stress.

Define balanced diet — a diet with a variety of foods in right quantities and proportions to meet all nutrient needs, with a small margin for leanness.
