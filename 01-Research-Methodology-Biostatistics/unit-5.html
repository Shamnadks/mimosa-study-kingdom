2 Hypothesis: Definition, Types & Characteristics

A hypothesis is the detective's suspect: a guess precise enough to be proved wrong.

🎬 Story mode

Judy's hunch: "Night-howler flowers make predators go savage." That's a research hypothesis. In court, Chief Bogo insists on starting from "no connection between night-howlers and savagery" (null hypothesis, H₀) until the evidence is overwhelming. Judy's claim becomes the alternative hypothesis, H₁.

📘 Exam definition

A hypothesis is a tentative, testable statement or proposition about the relationship between two or more variables, formulated as a provisional answer to the research question and verified by collecting and analysing data. (Kothari describes it as a proposition set forth to explain a group of facts, accepted provisionally as a basis for investigation.)

Points to consider while formulating a hypothesis (Nov 2022)

  1. Start from a clearly defined research problem and a thorough literature review.
  2. Identify the variables (independent, dependent, confounders) and define them operationally.
  3. State the expected relationship in simple, precise and testable terms; decide directional or non-directional.
  4. Make sure suitable methods, data and statistical tests exist to verify it within available time and resources.
  5. Convert into statistical form: H₀ (no difference) and H₁ (difference) before collecting data.
  6. Keep it consistent with known facts, limited in scope and ethically testable.

Functions of a hypothesis: gives direction to research, identifies relevant facts/variables, suggests the design and tests, links theory to observation, and provides a framework for conclusions.

3 Testing of Hypothesis: Basic Concepts

The statistical courtroom. These six concepts are exactly the "5 marks" of the Nov 2022 essay.

🎬 Story mode: the Zootopia courtroom

The accused (H₀ "no effect") is presumed innocent. Judy brings evidence (sample data). The judge sets a rule in advance: "Convict only if evidence this strong would happen less than 5 times in 100 by pure chance" (α = 0.05). Convicting an innocent animal = Type I error; letting a guilty one walk free = Type II error.

📘 Definition

Hypothesis testing (test of significance) is a statistical procedure that uses sample data to decide whether there is enough evidence to reject a null hypothesis about a population parameter, at a pre-chosen level of significance, i.e., whether an observed difference is real or merely due to chance (sampling variation).

1. Null & alternative hypothesis

H₀: no difference (μ₁ = μ₂). H₁: difference exists (μ₁ ≠ μ₂, or μ₁ > μ₂). H₀ is the one tested; H₁ is accepted only when H₀ is rejected.

2. Level of significance (α)

The maximum probability of a Type I error the researcher accepts, fixed before the test: usually 5% (0.05) or 1% (0.01). Confidence level = 1 − α (95%).

3. Test statistic & decision rule

A value calculated from sample data (z, t, χ², F) and compared with the critical value from tables. Rule: if calculated |value| ≥ critical value (or p ≤ α) → reject H₀.

4. Critical (rejection) region vs acceptance region

The part of the sampling distribution where H₀ is rejected. For z at α = 0.05: beyond ±1.96 (two-tailed) or beyond +1.645 (one-tailed).

5. One-tailed vs two-tailed test

Two-tailed when H₁ is non-directional (≠): α split equally into both tails (2.5% each). One-tailed when H₁ is directional (> or <): all of α in one tail.

6. Type I & Type II errors, power, df, SE

Type I (α): rejecting a true H₀. Type II (β): failing to reject a false H₀. Power = 1 − β. Degrees of freedom (usually n − 1). Standard error measures sampling variability of the statistic.

📘 Degrees of freedom

The number of independent values that are free to vary after certain restrictions (like a fixed mean) are imposed. One-sample/paired t: df = n − 1; unpaired t: n₁ + n₂ − 2; χ²: (r − 1)(c − 1); ANOVA: k − 1 (between) and N − k (within).

6 The P Value

"How surprised should we be if the suspect were actually innocent?"

🎬 Story mode

Nick claims a coin is fair (H₀). Judy flips it 10 times and gets 10 heads. If the coin really were fair, 10 heads in a row would happen only about once in 1000 tries (p ≈ 0.001). Such a small p makes Judy doubt Nick's claim: she rejects H₀. Sly fox!

📘 Exam definition

The P value is the probability of obtaining a result as extreme as, or more extreme than, the one observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. A small p value means the observed data are unlikely under H₀, giving evidence against H₀.

P valueUsual interpretationDecision at α = 0.05
p > 0.05Not statistically significant (NS)Fail to reject H₀
p ≤ 0.05Significant (*)Reject H₀
p ≤ 0.01Highly significant (**)Reject H₀
p ≤ 0.001Very highly significant (***)Reject H₀

✅ P value IS

  • A measure of evidence against H₀
  • Compared with α to decide
  • Reported exactly (p = 0.032) by software

❌ P value is NOT

  • The probability that H₀ is true
  • The size or importance of the effect
  • Proof of clinical usefulness
  • A guarantee that the study had no bias
⚠️ Statistical vs clinical significance

A huge trial may find that drug X lowers SBP by 1 mmHg with p = 0.001: statistically significant, clinically useless. Always report the effect size and the 95% confidence interval along with p. If the 95% CI of a difference does not include 0 (or of a ratio does not include 1), p < 0.05.

7 Type I & Type II Errors

Asked in 4 of 5 KUHS papers. Chicken Little will make sure you never forget it.

🎬 Story mode: Chicken Little

An acorn hits Chicken Little and he rings the school bell: "The sky is falling!" The town panics; there was nothing (H₀ "nothing is happening" was true, but he rejected it) → Type I error, false alarm. Months later a real alien piece falls and the town refuses to listen (H₀ was false, but they failed to reject it) → Type II error, a missed alarm.

Decision ↓ / Truth →H₀ true (no real effect)H₀ false (real effect exists)
Reject H₀❌ Type I error (α), false positive 🐥 acorn panic✅ Correct decision: Power (1 − β)
Fail to reject H₀✅ Correct decision (1 − α)❌ Type II error (β), false negative 👽 aliens ignored

Type I error (α)

  • Rejecting a true null hypothesis
  • "False positive", seeing a difference that isn't there
  • Probability = level of significance α (0.05)
  • e.g., concluding an ineffective drug works → useless drug reaches patients
  • Reduced by choosing smaller α (0.01)
VS

Type II error (β)

  • Failing to reject a false null hypothesis
  • "False negative", missing a real difference
  • Probability = β (often accepted 0.1–0.2)
  • e.g., concluding a truly effective drug is useless → patients lose a good drug
  • Reduced by increasing sample size / power
🧠 Mnemonic

Type I = "I" see something that isn't there (acorn panic). Type II = "II" (too) blind to see what is there (aliens ignored). Alternatively: α = Alarm false; β = Blind.

💊 Pharmacy examples

Type I: a BE study wrongly concludes a generic is bioequivalent when it is not → consumer's risk. Type II: a small trial fails to show that a genuinely effective antihypertensive works, and development is dropped → loss to patients and sponsor.

⭐ Exam tip

For a 2-mark brief: definition of both + the 2×2 table + "α is the level of significance; power = 1 − β". That's full marks in 4 lines.

8 Parametric vs Non-parametric Tests

THE most repeated KUHS essay (May 2022, Dec 2023, model paper). Learn the table, the family tree and the decision chart.

🎬 Story mode

Judy has two kinds of evidence. Sometimes she has exact measurements (paw-print length in cm, nicely bell-shaped across suspects): she uses precise "parametric" tools that rely on the mean and SD. Sometimes she only has rankings ("Duke Weaselton looked more nervous than Flash"), or very few, lopsided observations: she switches to "non-parametric" tools that work with ranks and don't care about the bell shape.

📘 Exam definitions

Parametric tests are tests of significance that assume the data come from a population with a specified distribution (usually normal) and test hypotheses about population parameters (mean, variance), using interval/ratio data. e.g., z-test, t-test, ANOVA, Pearson's r.

Non-parametric (distribution-free) tests make no (or minimal) assumption about the population distribution; they are based on ranks, signs or frequencies and are used for ordinal/nominal data, skewed data or small samples. e.g., χ², Mann–Whitney U, Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis.

9 Parametric Tests & Their Applications

z, t, ANOVA, F and Pearson: the precision tools.

A. z-test (standard normal deviate test)

  • When: large samples (n ≥ 30) or population SD known; data approximately normal.
  • Uses: sample mean vs population mean; difference between two means; difference between two proportions.
  • z = (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n); for two proportions z = (p₁ − p₂)/√[pq(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)].
  • Reject H₀ if |z| ≥ 1.96 (5%) or 2.58 (1%).
  • Example: comparing mean haemoglobin of 100 pregnant women with a standard value; comparing cure rates of two antibiotics in 200 patients each.

B. Student's t-test

📘 Definition

The t-test is a parametric test used to compare means when the sample is small (usually n < 30) and the population SD is unknown. It was described in 1908 by W. S. Gosset, who published under the pen-name "Student". The test statistic follows the t-distribution with specific degrees of freedom.

Assumptions: random sampling; data from a normally distributed population; interval/ratio scale; independent observations; equal variances in the two groups (for unpaired t; otherwise Welch's correction).

1. One-sample t

t = (x̄ − μ)/(s/√n), df = n − 1
Mean content of 10 tablets vs label claim 500 mg.

2. Unpaired (independent) t

t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂)/[Sp√(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)], df = n₁ + n₂ − 2
Drug group vs placebo group; batch A vs batch B dissolution.

3. Paired t

t = d̄/(sd/√n), df = n − 1
BP before vs after treatment in the same patients; crossover BA studies.
🧮 Mini worked example: unpaired t-test (tablet hardness, kg/cm²)

Formulation A: 5, 6, 7 (mean 6, s₁² = 1). Formulation B: 8, 9, 10 (mean 9, s₂² = 1).

Sp = √{[(2 × 1) + (2 × 1)]/4} = 1. t = (9 − 6)/[1 × √(1/3 + 1/3)] = 3/0.816 = 3.67, df = 4.

Critical t (df 4, 0.05 two-tailed) = 2.776 → 3.67 > 2.776 → reject H₀: hardness differs significantly. (Practice numbers.)

The paired t example (digoxin, t = 2.83) is solved step by step in Section 4.

Applications in pharmacy: comparing efficacy of drug vs placebo; before–after studies; comparing two formulations, two analytical methods or two batches; bioavailability comparisons; animal pharmacology (treated vs control).

C. Analysis of variance (ANOVA)

📘 Definition (KUHS May 2022 brief)

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) is a parametric statistical technique, developed by R. A. Fisher, that tests whether the means of three or more groups differ significantly by partitioning the total variation in the data into variation between groups and variation within groups, and comparing them by the F ratio: F = MSbetween / MSwithin.

Types

  • One-way ANOVA: one factor (e.g., 3 binders → disintegration time)
  • Two-way ANOVA: two factors and their interaction (binder × compression force)
  • Repeated-measures ANOVA: same subjects measured several times

Assumptions

  • Normal distribution within each group
  • Homogeneity of variances (homoscedasticity)
  • Independent, random observations
  • Interval/ratio dependent variable

Why not several t-tests? With 3 groups you'd need 3 t-tests; the chance of at least one false positive rises to 1 − 0.95³ ≈ 14% instead of 5%. ANOVA keeps the overall α at 5%. If F is significant, a post-hoc test (Tukey's HSD, Bonferroni, Dunnett's vs control, Scheffé) shows which pairs differ.

D. Pearson's correlation coefficient & regression

Tests linear association between two normally distributed continuous variables (r from −1 to +1). Significance of r: t = r√(n − 2)/√(1 − r²), df = n − 2. Regression Y = a + bX predicts Y from X (see Unit 4, Section 7). Applications: calibration curves, PK parameter estimation, shelf-life prediction, IVIVC.

E. F-test for equality of variances

F = larger variance / smaller variance (s₁²/s₂²), df (n₁ − 1, n₂ − 1). Used to check the equal-variance assumption before an unpaired t-test, and to compare the precision of two analytical methods.

10 Non-parametric Tests & Their Applications

Rank-and-count tools for when the bell curve fails.

A. Chi-square (χ²) test

📘 Definition

The chi-square test (Karl Pearson, 1900) is a non-parametric test that compares observed frequencies (O) with expected frequencies (E) (expected if H₀ were true) to test association between categorical variables or the goodness of fit of data to a distribution. χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E; df = (r − 1)(c − 1).

Uses

  • Test of independence/association (smoking vs cancer)
  • Goodness of fit (do observed counts fit an expected ratio?)
  • Test of homogeneity (same proportion in several groups)

Conditions

  • Random sample, independent observations
  • Data as frequencies (not %)
  • Expected frequency ≥ 5 in each cell (else Fisher's exact; Yates' correction for small 2×2)
  • Total N reasonably large

2×2 short-cut

χ² = N(ad − bc)² / [(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)]

Critical χ² (df 1) = 3.84 at 0.05 (6.63 at 0.01). 7.5 > 6.63 → reject H₀ (p < 0.01): recovery is significantly associated with the antidote. (Check with the short-cut: 80 × (660 − 180)² / (40 × 40 × 48 × 32) = 7.5 ✔)

Male: 1173 with / 10410 without (11583); Female: 547 / 7640 (8187); total 1720 / 18050 (19770). Expected for males with scabies = 11583 × 1720/19770 = 1007.7 (others: 10575.3, 712.3, 7474.7). χ² ≈ 71.7 > 10.83 (df 1, p 0.001) → prevalence differs very highly significantly between genders.

B. Rank-based tests

Mann–Whitney U test (= Wilcoxon rank-sum)

Alternative to the unpaired t-test: compares two independent groups on ordinal or non-normal data.

Steps: pool both samples → rank from lowest to highest (average ranks for ties) → sum ranks of each group (R₁, R₂) → U₁ = n₁n₂ + n₁(n₁ + 1)/2 − R₁, U₂ = n₁n₂ − U₁ → U = smaller value → reject H₀ if U ≤ table value.

e.g., pain scores (0–10) with two analgesics; ADR severity grades in two groups.

Wilcoxon signed-rank test

Alternative to the paired t-test: two related measurements on the same subjects.

Steps: find differences (after − before) → drop zero differences → rank absolute differences → give each rank the sign of its difference → T = smaller of the sums of + and − ranks → reject H₀ if T ≤ table value.

e.g., symptom scores before and after a herbal cough syrup.

Kruskal–Wallis H test

Alternative to one-way ANOVA: three or more independent groups.

H = [12/(N(N + 1))] Σ(Ri²/ni) − 3(N + 1), compared with χ² (df k − 1).

e.g., patient-satisfaction ranks across three pharmacies.

Friedman test

Alternative to repeated-measures (two-way) ANOVA: three or more related conditions in the same subjects (randomised block).

e.g., the same 10 volunteers ranking the taste of three paediatric syrup flavours.

Spearman's rank correlation (ρ)

Alternative to Pearson's r: association between two ranked/ordinal variables.

ρ = 1 − 6Σd²/[n(n² − 1)], d = difference between ranks.

e.g., ranking of students in pharmacology vs pharmaceutics.

Sign test, McNemar & Fisher's exact

Sign test: uses only + / − signs of paired differences (simplest).

McNemar: paired nominal data (yes/no before and after counselling).

Fisher's exact: 2×2 tables with small expected counts (< 5).

✅ Advantages of non-parametric tests

  • No normality assumption
  • Work with ordinal/nominal data and ranks
  • Suitable for very small samples
  • Robust to outliers
  • Simple and quick
VS

❌ Disadvantages

  • Less powerful than parametric tests when assumptions hold
  • Waste information (values reduced to ranks)
  • Test medians, not means; effect size harder to express
  • Tables needed for many tests; ties complicate

B. Impact factor (model paper brief)

📘 Exam definition

The (Journal) Impact Factor is a journal-level metric that indicates the average number of citations received in a given year by the citable items (articles and reviews) the journal published in the previous two years. It is calculated annually by Clarivate and published in the Journal Citation Reports (JCR); the idea was introduced by Eugene Garfield.

IF (2025) = Citations in 2025 to items published in 2023 + 2024 ÷ Number of citable items published in 2023 + 2024

Example (fictional journal): Zootopia J Pharm Sci published 90 citable items in 2023 and 110 in 2024 (total 200). In 2025 these were cited 600 times. IF 2025 = 600/200 = 3.0, i.e., an average article is cited about three times a year in its first two years.

Uses

  • Compare journals within the same field
  • Help authors choose where to submit
  • Library subscription decisions
  • One of many indicators in academic evaluation

Limitations

  • Journal-level, not a measure of an individual article or author
  • Varies widely between fields
  • Skewed by a few highly cited papers; reviews attract more citations
  • Can be manipulated (excess self-citation, citation stacking)
  • Two-year window is short for slow-citing fields

Other metrics: 5-year impact factor; CiteScore (Scopus/Elsevier, 4-year window); SJR and SNIP; h-index (an author has index h if h of their papers have at least h citations each; proposed by J. E. Hirsch in 2005); i10-index (Google Scholar: papers with ≥ 10 citations); altmetrics (social-media and news attention).

Q. Differentiate between parametric and nonparametric tests. Briefly explain any three parametric and non-parametric tests each used in hypothesis testing.

1. Introduction

Tests of significance decide whether an observed difference is real or due to chance. They are of two types depending on whether assumptions about the population distribution are made.

Parametric tests assume that the data are drawn from a normally distributed population and test hypotheses about parameters (mean, variance) using interval/ratio data. Non-parametric tests are distribution-free tests based on ranks, signs or frequencies, used for ordinal/nominal or non-normal data.

3. Parametric tests

(a) Student's t-test (Gosset, 1908): compares means of small samples (n < 30) with unknown σ. Types: one-sample t = (x̄ − μ)/(s/√n); unpaired t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂)/[Sp√(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)] with df n₁ + n₂ − 2; paired t = d̄/(sd/√n) with df n − 1. Assumptions: normality, random samples, equal variances. Use: drug vs placebo, BP before vs after treatment.

(b) ANOVA (R. A. Fisher): compares means of three or more groups by partitioning total variance into between- and within-group variance; F = MSbetween/MSwithin, df (k − 1, N − k). If significant, post-hoc tests (Tukey, Dunnett) locate the difference. Use: comparing three formulations or doses.

(c) Pearson's correlation coefficient (r): measures linear association between two normally distributed continuous variables; r = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ)/√[Σ(x − x̄)²Σ(y − ȳ)²], range −1 to +1; significance by t = r√(n − 2)/√(1 − r²). Use: calibration curves, dose vs response. (z-test for large samples may be written as an alternative.)

4. Non-parametric tests

(a) Chi-square test (Karl Pearson): compares observed with expected frequencies: χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E, df = (r − 1)(c − 1). Uses: association between categorical variables, goodness of fit, homogeneity. Condition: expected frequency ≥ 5 per cell. Example: cure rate (cured/not cured) with drug vs placebo.

(b) Mann–Whitney U test: alternative to unpaired t-test for two independent groups. Pool and rank the data, sum ranks R₁, R₂; U₁ = n₁n₂ + n₁(n₁ + 1)/2 − R₁; the smaller U is compared with table value. Example: pain scores with two analgesics.

(c) Wilcoxon signed-rank test: alternative to paired t-test. Find differences, ignore zeros, rank absolute differences, attach signs, T = smaller sum of ranks; compare with table. Example: symptom scores before and after treatment in the same patients.

(Draw Fig 5.3: parametric tests on the left with their non-parametric twins on the right.)

5. Conclusion

Parametric tests are preferred when their assumptions are met because they are more powerful; non-parametric tests are valuable for ordinal or nominal data, small samples and skewed distributions. Choosing the right test according to data type, distribution, number of groups and pairing is essential for valid conclusions.

  • States the relationship between variables (independent and dependent).
  • Specific and limited in scope; consistent with known facts.
  • Stated in advance as H₀ and H₁ (directional or non-directional).

c) Types of statistical hypothesis (2 marks)

Null hypothesis (H₀): no difference or no relationship; e.g., H₀: μdrug = μplacebo. Alternative hypothesis (H₁): a difference exists. It may be two-tailed/non-directional (H₁: μ₁ ≠ μ₂) or one-tailed/directional (H₁: μ₁ > μ₂ or μ₁ < μ₂). Hypotheses may also be simple (specify the parameter exactly, e.g., μ = 500 mg) or composite (a range, e.g., μ > 500 mg).

d) Basic concepts concerning testing of hypothesis (5 marks)

  1. Null and alternative hypothesis: H₀ is tested; H₁ is accepted only if H₀ is rejected. We "reject" or "fail to reject" H₀; we never prove it.
  2. Level of significance (α): maximum risk of rejecting a true H₀ that we accept, fixed in advance: 5% (0.05) or 1%. Confidence level = 1 − α.
  3. Test statistic and decision rule: a value computed from the sample (z, t, χ², F) and compared with the critical value from tables; if |calculated| ≥ critical (or p ≤ α), reject H₀.
  4. Critical region and acceptance region: the area of the sampling distribution where H₀ is rejected; for z at 5%, beyond ±1.96.
  5. One-tailed and two-tailed tests: two-tailed when H₁ is ≠ (α split into both tails, 2.5% each, z = ±1.96); one-tailed when H₁ is > or < (5% in one tail, z = 1.645). (Draw Fig 5.1.)
  6. Type I and Type II errors: Type I (α) = rejecting a true H₀ (false positive); Type II (β) = accepting a false H₀ (false negative); power = 1 − β. Reduced by proper α and adequate sample size.
  7. Degrees of freedom and standard error: df = number of independent values free to vary (n − 1 etc.); SE = SD/√n measures sampling variability and is the denominator of most test statistics.

Conclusion: these concepts form the logical framework that allows researchers to draw objective, probability-based conclusions from samples.

Q. Describe TWO parametric tests used in context of testing hypothesis.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Jul 2023 · ~12 min
One line on parametric tests (½) → Student's t-test: definition, assumptions, 3 types with formulae, application (2) → ANOVA: definition, assumptions, F, table, post-hoc, application (2) → line on when to use each (½)

Parametric tests assume normally distributed populations and test hypotheses about means/variances using interval or ratio data.

1. Student's t-test

Used to compare means when n is small (< 30) and σ is unknown (W. S. Gosset, "Student", 1908). Assumptions: normality, random independent samples, equal variances (unpaired). Types: one-sample t = (x̄ − μ)/(s/√n), df n − 1; unpaired t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂)/[Sp√(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)], df n₁ + n₂ − 2; paired t = d̄/(sd/√n), df n − 1. If calculated t ≥ table t, reject H₀. Applications: drug vs placebo, before–after BP, two batches or two analytical methods.

2. Analysis of variance (ANOVA)

Compares the means of three or more groups (R. A. Fisher) by splitting total variation into between-group and within-group variation. F = MSbetween/MSwithin with df (k − 1, N − k). Assumptions: normality, homogeneity of variances, independence. Types: one-way, two-way, repeated measures. A significant F is followed by post-hoc tests (Tukey, Bonferroni, Dunnett). Applications: comparing three formulations, doses or treatment groups; inter-day precision in method validation.

t-test for two groups; ANOVA for three or more (multiple t-tests would inflate Type I error).

Q. Explain the procedure involved in testing of hypothesis.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Dec 2023 · ~12 min
Definition (½) → 7 steps as a vertical flow with one line each (3) → short worked illustration (1) → Type I/II line (½)

Testing of hypothesis is a systematic procedure to decide, using sample data, whether to reject a null hypothesis at a chosen level of significance.

  1. State the hypotheses: H₀ (no difference, μ₁ = μ₂) and H₁ (μ₁ ≠ μ₂, or directional).
  2. Set the level of significance (α = 0.05 or 0.01) and decide one- or two-tailed.
  3. Select the appropriate test based on data type, distribution, sample size, number of groups and pairing (z, t, F, χ² or non-parametric).
  4. Compute the test statistic from sample data and determine df.
  5. Find the critical value from tables (or the p value from software).
  6. Decision: if calculated value ≥ critical value (p ≤ α), reject H₀; otherwise fail to reject H₀.
  7. Conclusion and interpretation in terms of the research problem.

Example: digoxin levels at 4 h vs 8 h in 9 patients: paired t = 2.83 > 2.306 (df 8) → reject H₀; the level fell significantly.

Errors possible: Type I (α) and Type II (β); power = 1 − β.

A Type II error occurs when a researcher fails to reject a false null hypothesis, i.e., concludes there is no difference when a real difference exists (false negative).
