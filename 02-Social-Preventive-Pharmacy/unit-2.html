1 General Principles of Prevention & Control — Moana's Darkness

Every disease answer in this unit stands on these principles. Learn them once, reuse them fifteen times.

🎬 Story mode

In Moana, a darkness starts on one island and creeps outward — coconuts rot, fish vanish — until it threatens every island in the ocean. That is an epidemic turning into a pandemic. Moana's fix has three parts: return the stolen heart (remove the source), sail past the reef where the darkness travels (cut the route), and help each island recover (protect the host). Baymax uses the exact same plan for cholera, malaria or influenza.

The chain of infection — break any link

Modes of transmission

➡️ Direct

  • Direct contact (skin, sexual) — STIs, leprosy
  • Droplet infection — influenza, TB, ARI
  • Contact with soil — tetanus, hookworm
  • Inoculation into skin — rabies (bite), hepatitis B
  • Transplacental — syphilis, HIV, rubella

↪️ Indirect

  • Vehicle-borne — water, food, blood (cholera, hepatitis A)
  • Vector-borne — mosquito, flea, sandfly (malaria, dengue)
  • Air-borne — droplet nuclei, dust (TB, measles)
  • Fomite-borne — towels, utensils, toys
  • Unclean hands & fingers

General principles of control of communicable diseases

ApproachMeasures
1. Control the reservoir / sourceEarly diagnosis · notification · epidemiological investigation · isolation · treatment of cases and carriers · quarantine of contacts · disinfection (concurrent and terminal)
2. Interrupt transmissionSafe water (chlorination), sanitation, food hygiene, fly control · vector control (anti-larval, anti-adult) · hand hygiene · respiratory etiquette, masks, ventilation · safe blood and injections · condoms
3. Protect the susceptible hostActive immunization (vaccines) · passive immunization (immunoglobulins) · chemoprophylaxis · nutrition · personal protection (nets, repellents) · health education
4. Surveillance & evaluationContinuous surveillance (IDSP/IHIP), outbreak detection, monitoring and evaluation of control measures
🔎 Investigating an epidemic — the 10 steps (open)
  1. Verify the diagnosis
  2. Confirm the existence of an epidemic (compare with usual numbers)
  3. Define the population at risk
  4. Rapid search for all cases (using a case definition)
  5. Analyse data by time, place and person (epidemic curve, spot map)
  6. Formulate a hypothesis
  7. Test the hypothesis
  8. Evaluate ecological factors (water, food, vectors)
  9. Further investigation of the population at risk
  10. Write the report — and start control measures without waiting

Principles for non-communicable (lifestyle) diseases

👥 Population (mass) strategy

  • Shift the whole population's risk downward — salt reduction, tobacco taxes, healthy school food, parks for exercise
  • Includes primordial prevention
+

🎯 High-risk strategy

  • Find individuals at high risk by screening (BP, sugar, BMI, tobacco) and give them targeted care
  • Example: screening everyone ≥30 years at Ayushman Arogya Mandirs

NCDs share four behavioural risk factors — tobacco use, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity and harmful use of alcohol — so one integrated programme can fight them together.

🆕 Update — NPCDCS is now NP-NCD

India's National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS, 2010) was renamed the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD) in May 2023 to cover all NCDs. 2 Cholera — The Darkness in the Water

🎬 Story mode

When Te Kā's darkness poisoned Motunui's lagoon, everyone who drank from it fell sick. Cholera travels the same way — through dirty water and food — and can kill a healthy adult within hours. The good news: a simple sugar-salt drink (ORS) saves nearly everyone.

🦠

Agent

Vibrio cholerae O1 (classical & El Tor biotypes; Ogawa, Inaba serotypes) and O139 (Bengal). Gram-negative, comma-shaped, highly motile.
🚰

Spread

Faeco-oral — contaminated water and food (raw seafood, vegetables), flies, dirty hands. Reservoir: cases, carriers, aquatic environment.
⏳

Incubation

A few hours to 5 days (usually 1–2 days).
🩺

Key signs

Sudden, painless, profuse “rice-water” stools, vomiting, leg cramps, sunken eyes, poor skin turgor, rapid dehydration → shock.

Diagnosis: stool culture on TCBS agar, dark-field microscopy (darting motility), rapid dipstick tests. Treatment: rehydration — WHO low-osmolarity ORS for mild/moderate, IV Ringer's lactate for severe; antibiotics shorten illness (doxycycline 300 mg single dose in adults; azithromycin for children and pregnant women); zinc for children.

LevelPrevention of cholera
PrimarySafe drinking water (boiling, chlorination — residual chlorine 0.5 mg/L), sanitary latrines (no open defecation), food hygiene (eat hot, cooked food; avoid raw seafood), fly control, hand-washing with soap, health education, oral cholera vaccine (killed whole-cell, 2 doses) in high-risk areas and outbreaks
SecondaryEarly detection of every case of acute watery diarrhoea (IDSP), prompt ORS + antibiotics, treatment centres
TertiaryManagement of severe dehydration, shock and renal failure; nutritional rehabilitation

Control measures during an outbreak

🔬Verify diagnosisstool culture
📢Notifyhealth authorities, IDSP
🏥Early case finding & treatmentORS depots, treatment centres
🚪Isolation & disinfectionenteric precautions; bleach stools/vomit, clothes
🚰Water & sanitationchlorinate wells, repair pipes
💊Contactswatch 5 days; chemoprophylaxis of household contacts only
📣Health educationboil water, wash hands
⚠️ Classic confusion

Mass chemoprophylaxis of a whole community and the old injectable cholera vaccine are NOT recommended — they waste resources and give false security. Only household contacts get prophylaxis; oral vaccine is the modern option.

💊 Pharmacist's role

Keep ORS and zinc in stock; teach ORS preparation (one packet in 1 litre of clean water, use within 24 hours); dispense chlorine tablets/bleaching powder; discourage anti-motility drugs (loperamide) in cholera; report clusters of diarrhoea.

3 SARS — Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome

🎬 Story mode

In 2003 a new darkness jumped from animal markets in southern China to hotels, hospitals and airports in about 29 countries within months. There was no vaccine and no cure — yet it was stopped by old-fashioned public health: find, isolate, trace, quarantine. Baymax calls it “the outbreak that proved isolation works”.

🦠

Agent

SARS coronavirus (SARS-CoV); bats are the natural reservoir, civet cats the likely intermediate host.
💨

Spread

Respiratory droplets and close contact; contaminated surfaces; “super-spreaders”; mostly after symptoms start (peak in week 2).
⏳

Incubation

2–7 days (up to 10 days).
🩺

Key signs

High fever (>38 °C), chills, headache, myalgia → dry cough, breathlessness, atypical pneumonia, ARDS; worst in the elderly.

Outbreak facts: November 2002 – July 2003; about 8,096 cases and 774 deaths in 29 countries. Diagnosis by RT-PCR and serology; treatment is supportive (oxygen, ventilation) — no proven specific antiviral, no vaccine.

PrincipleMeasures
Early detection & isolationCase definition, fever screening, isolate suspected and probable cases in hospital with airborne + contact precautions
Contact tracing & quarantineTrace all close contacts; home quarantine and daily temperature checks for 10 days
Infection control in hospitalsN95 masks, gowns, gloves, eye protection, negative-pressure rooms, hand hygiene — health workers were a big share of cases
Community measuresRespiratory etiquette, masks for the symptomatic, hand-washing, avoid crowding, public information
Travel measuresEntry/exit screening at airports, WHO global alert and travel advisories
Surveillance & researchInternational reporting (WHO), laboratory network that identified the virus within weeks

Psst, Mimosa… everything you saw during COVID-19 — masks, quarantine, contact tracing — is the SARS playbook. Write it with confidence; you lived it! 😷

💊 Pharmacist's role

Supply masks, sanitizers and PPE; counsel on cough etiquette; avoid unnecessary antibiotics; report clusters of severe respiratory illness; keep the pharmacy area ventilated.

4 Ebola Virus Disease

🎬 Story mode

Ebola is the darkness that travels by touch, not by air — through blood, vomit and other body fluids, and even from the bodies of the dead at funerals. So the shield is gloves, isolation, safe burials and a ring of vaccinated people around every case.

🦠

Agent

Ebola virus (family Filoviridae); Zaire species most deadly. First recognised in 1976 near the Ebola river (DR Congo). Fruit bats are believed to be the natural host.
🖐️

Spread

Animal-to-human (handling sick bats, monkeys, bush-meat) then human-to-human by direct contact with blood/body fluids of sick or dead persons, contaminated bedding, needles; sexual transmission from survivors. Not airborne; not infectious before symptoms.
⏳

Incubation

2–21 days (hence 21-day follow-up).
🩺

Key signs

Sudden fever, fatigue, muscle pain, headache, sore throat → vomiting, diarrhoea, rash, kidney/liver failure, bleeding. Case fatality around 50% on average.

Diagnosis: RT-PCR, antigen tests (high-containment labs). Treatment: early supportive care (rehydration, electrolytes, oxygen); monoclonal antibodies are approved for Zaire Ebola virus. Vaccine: rVSV-ZEBOV (Ervebo), WHO-prequalified in November 2019, used for ring vaccination of contacts and front-line workers.

LevelPrevention & control of Ebola
PrimaryAvoid contact with bats, monkeys and bush-meat; cook animal products well; avoid contact with body fluids; hand hygiene; PPE and infection control for health workers; condom use by male survivors; ring vaccination
SecondaryEarly detection with a case definition, isolation in Ebola treatment units, rapid supportive care, contact tracing with 21-day follow-up
TertiaryCare for survivors (joint pain, eye problems, mental health), stigma reduction
Community & travelSafe and dignified burials, community engagement, disinfection of homes, exit/entry screening of travellers, laboratory surveillance
💊 Pharmacist's role

Manage PPE and disinfectant supplies (1:10 / 0.5% chlorine solutions), safe disposal of sharps, accurate information to counter rumours, and cold-chain support for vaccine campaigns.

5 Influenza — The Shape-Shifting Darkness

🎬 Story mode

Remember how Maui could change his shape whenever he wanted? Influenza virus does the same: small changes every year (drift) and, once in a while, a total transformation (shift) that nobody's immunity recognises — a pandemic. That is why the flu vaccine must be updated every year.

🦠

Agent

Influenza virus (Orthomyxoviridae), types A, B, C. Type A has subtypes by surface haemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N) — e.g. H1N1, H3N2; avian H5N1.
💨

Spread

Droplets and aerosols from coughs/sneezes, contaminated hands and surfaces; crowded places; children spread it widely.
⏳

Incubation

Short — 18–72 hours (1–4 days).
🩺

Key signs

Sudden fever, chills, headache, body ache, sore throat, dry cough, running nose, exhaustion. Complications: viral or secondary bacterial pneumonia.

🌊 Antigenic drift

  • Small point mutations in H/N
  • Types A and B
  • Causes yearly epidemics
  • Reason for annual vaccine update
VS

🌋 Antigenic shift

  • Major change — new H and/or N (gene reassortment)
  • Type A only
  • Causes pandemics — 1918 (H1N1), 1957 (H2N2), 1968 (H3N2), 2009 (H1N1 “swine flu”)

High-risk groups: elderly (≥65), pregnant women, children under 5, people with chronic heart/lung/kidney disease, diabetes, immunosuppression, health-care workers. Diagnosis: RT-PCR on nasal/throat swab. Treatment: rest, fluids, paracetamol (no aspirin in children — Reye's syndrome); antiviral oseltamivir (neuraminidase inhibitor), best within 48 h, for high-risk or severe cases.

Principles of influenza prevention & control

PrincipleMeasures
1. SurveillanceWHO Global Influenza Surveillance and Response System; national labs (ICMR-NIV network) and IDSP watch circulating strains and outbreaks — the basis for choosing vaccine strains
2. VaccinationInactivated (trivalent/quadrivalent) vaccine every year, strains chosen by WHO twice a year; priority to high-risk groups and health workers; live attenuated nasal vaccine in some countries
3. Personal & respiratory hygieneCover coughs/sneezes, hand-washing, masks for the sick, avoid touching face, ventilation
4. Isolation & social distancingSick people stay home until fever-free; school closures and avoiding mass gatherings during pandemics
5. AntiviralsEarly oseltamivir for high-risk/severe cases; chemoprophylaxis for selected high-risk contacts
6. Pandemic preparednessStockpiling antivirals & PPE, hospital surge plans, risk communication; for avian flu — culling infected poultry, protecting poultry workers
📋 India's H1N1 triage (MoHFW categories)

Category A — mild fever, cough, sore throat: home isolation, no test, no oseltamivir. Category B — A + high fever/severe sore throat, or A in a high-risk person: oseltamivir at home, no test needed. Category C — breathlessness, chest pain, drowsiness, low BP, blood in sputum, bluish nails: test, admit, treat immediately.

SurveillanceVaccineHygieneIsolationAntiviralsPreparedness

“Shape-shifting Viruses Hate Island Armour Plans.”

💊 Pharmacist's role

Run flu-vaccination reminders for elderly and diabetic customers, maintain the vaccine cold chain (2–8 °C), dispense oseltamivir only on prescription, discourage antibiotics for plain flu, counsel on aspirin avoidance in children.

6 Acute Respiratory Infections (ARI)

🎬 Story mode

Baymax's favourite tool here is the simplest: a watch. By counting a child's breaths for one full minute, a health worker in any village can tell a simple cold from pneumonia — and save a life with a few days of amoxicillin.

📘 Definition

ARI is an acute infection of any part of the respiratory tract (nose to alveoli) lasting less than 30 days (less than 14 days for acute ear infection). Upper ARI: common cold, pharyngitis, tonsillitis, sinusitis, otitis media. Lower ARI: epiglottitis, laryngitis/croup, bronchitis, bronchiolitis, pneumonia (the killer).

🦠

Agents

Viruses — RSV, rhinovirus, influenza, parainfluenza, adenovirus, measles. Bacteria — S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae type b, S. aureus, B. pertussis, C. diphtheriae.
⚠️

Risk factors

Low birth weight, malnutrition, no breastfeeding, incomplete immunization, vitamin A deficiency, indoor smoke (wood/dung fuel), passive smoking, overcrowding, cold weather.
🩺

Signs

Cough, cold, fever, sore throat, ear pain; danger — fast breathing, chest indrawing, stridor, inability to drink, convulsions, lethargy.

Prevention & control — Protect · Prevent · Treat

🛡️ Protect

Exclusive breastfeeding for 6 months, adequate complementary feeding, vitamin A, good nutrition, hand-washing.

💉 Prevent

Vaccines — measles–rubella, DPT/pentavalent (pertussis, diphtheria, Hib), PCV, BCG, influenza; reduce indoor air pollution (clean cooking fuel such as LPG), no smoking at home, better housing and ventilation, HIV care.

💊 Treat

Early case detection and standard case management by ASHA/ANM, pre-referral amoxicillin, pulse oximetry and oxygen, referral of severe cases.
🏛️ Programmes

ARI control began as a national programme in 1990, merged into Child Survival & Safe Motherhood (1992), RCH and today's IMNCI. SAANS (Social Awareness and Action to Neutralise Pneumonia Successfully) was launched in November 2019; its goal was to bring childhood pneumonia deaths below 3 per 1,000 live births by 2025. World Pneumonia Day — 12 November.

7 Pneumonia

📘 Definition

Pneumonia is an acute inflammation of the lung parenchyma (alveoli), usually infectious, in which the air sacs fill with pus and fluid, making breathing painful and limiting oxygen intake.

🦠

Agents

Streptococcus pneumoniae (commonest), Hib, S. aureus, Klebsiella, atypicals (Mycoplasma, Legionella), viruses (RSV, influenza, SARS-CoV-2), Pneumocystis in HIV.
💨

Spread

Droplets, aspiration of throat organisms, blood-borne; hospital (ventilator-associated) infection.
📂

Types

Community-acquired, hospital-acquired (≥48 h after admission), ventilator-associated, aspiration; lobar vs bronchopneumonia.
🩺

Symptoms

Fever with chills, cough with purulent/rusty sputum, pleuritic chest pain, breathlessness, fast breathing, chest indrawing (children), confusion (elderly), crackles.

Diagnosis: chest X-ray, sputum Gram stain/culture, blood count, pulse oximetry. Severity in adults by CURB-65 — Confusion, Urea raised, Respiratory rate ≥30, low BP, age ≥65. Treatment: amoxicillin first-line (children and mild adult CAP); macrolide for atypicals; β-lactam + macrolide when severe; oxygen.

LevelPrevention of pneumonia
PrimaryPCV (UIP: 6 weeks, 14 weeks, booster at 9 months), Hib (pentavalent), measles–rubella, influenza vaccine; pneumococcal vaccine for elderly; breastfeeding and nutrition; stop smoking; clean cooking fuel; hand hygiene
SecondaryEarly recognition (fast breathing, chest indrawing), prompt antibiotics, pulse oximetry, referral
TertiaryOxygen and intensive care, management of empyema/sepsis, pulmonary rehabilitation
Hospital controlVentilator bundle (head-end elevation, oral care), hand hygiene, early mobilisation
💊 Pharmacist's role

Ensure complete antibiotic courses, correct paediatric dosing, vaccine reminders for elderly and diabetics, smoking-cessation support, and stewardship to avoid needless broad-spectrum use.

8 Into the Bayou — Mosquitoes & Vector-Borne Diseases

Four syllabus diseases (malaria, dengue, chikungunya, filariasis) share one villain: the mosquito. Master the mosquito once.

🎬 Story mode

In The Princess and the Frog, Tiana and Naveen hop through a bayou humming with mosquitoes — while Ray, the kind firefly, lights the way. In real life, the bayou's still water is a mosquito nursery. Remove the water, and the nursery closes.

📘 Vector-borne disease

A disease transmitted to humans by blood-feeding arthropods (mosquitoes, sandflies, ticks, fleas). India's national programme — the National Vector Borne Disease Control Programme (NVBDCP), now the National Center for Vector Borne Diseases Control (NCVBDC) — covers six: malaria, dengue, chikungunya, Japanese encephalitis, lymphatic filariasis and kala-azar.

Integrated vector management (IVM)

🐛 Anti-larval

  • Source reduction — drain/fill pools, empty containers weekly (“dry day”), cover tanks
  • Larvicides — temephos (Abate), larvicidal oil, Bti (bacterial larvicide)
  • Biological — larvivorous fish Gambusia, guppy
  • Environmental management — engineering, drainage

🦟 Anti-adult

  • Indoor residual spraying (IRS) — synthetic pyrethroids, malathion, DDT
  • Long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs)
  • Space spraying / fogging (pyrethrum, malathion) during outbreaks

🧍 Personal protection

  • Bed nets, window screens
  • Repellents (DEET), coils, vaporisers
  • Full-sleeved clothing
  • Community participation & health education

Biological transmission types: propagative (agent multiplies, no change — plague bacilli in flea), cyclo-propagative (changes form AND multiplies — malaria parasite in mosquito), cyclo-developmental (develops but does not multiply — filarial larvae in mosquito).

9 Malaria — the Night Visitor of the Bayou

Asked in every single KUHS paper in some form. This is your highest-yield disease in the entire subject.

🎬 Story mode

When night falls on the bayou, Ray the firefly glows — and so does the hungry female Anopheles. She bites, leaving tiny sporozoites that hide in the liver, then burst out to attack red blood cells in waves: chills, fever, sweat… and again two days later. Break her night-time visits, and the waves stop.

🦠

Agent

Plasmodium vivax and P. falciparum (most dangerous — cerebral malaria) in India; also P. malariae, P. ovale, P. knowlesi.
🦟

Spread

Bite of infected female Anopheles (night). Main Indian vectors: An. culicifacies (rural), An. stephensi (urban), An. fluviatilis (hills), An. minimus & An. dirus (North-East forests), An. sundaicus (coast). Also blood transfusion, shared needles, mother-to-foetus.
⏳

Incubation

P. falciparum ~12 days, P. vivax ~14 days (relapses months later from hypnozoites), P. malariae ~28 days.
🩺

Key signs

Paroxysms: cold stage (chills, rigors) → hot stage (high fever, headache, vomiting) → sweating stage; anaemia, splenomegaly. Severe falciparum: cerebral malaria, severe anaemia, renal failure, jaundice, hypoglycaemia, shock.

Diagnosis: peripheral blood smear — thick (detects parasites) and thin (identifies species) — the gold standard; rapid diagnostic tests (bivalent RDT for Pf and Pv antigens) used by ASHAs in villages. Every fever case in an endemic area should be tested.

Treatment (national drug policy)

InfectionTreatment
P. vivaxChloroquine for 3 days (total 25 mg/kg) + primaquine 0.25 mg/kg daily for 14 days (radical cure — kills hypnozoites; avoid in G6PD deficiency, pregnancy, infants)
P. falciparum (uncomplicated)ACT — artesunate 3 days + sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine on day 1 (AS+SP); artemether-lumefantrine in the North-East; + single-dose primaquine (gametocytocidal) on day 2

10 Dengue — the Daytime Bayou Biter

🎬 Story mode

Not every bayou mosquito waits for night. The striped Aedes bites in broad daylight and breeds in the water left in Tiana's flower pots, old tyres and air-coolers. Dengue's trick is cruel: the danger comes just when the fever falls and everyone relaxes.

🦠

Agent

Dengue virus (Flavivirus), 4 serotypes DENV-1 to 4. Recovery gives lifelong immunity only to that serotype; a second infection with another serotype raises the risk of severe dengue.
🦟

Spread

Bite of infected Aedes aegypti (main) and Ae. albopictus — day-biters, breeding in clean water in containers.
⏳

Incubation

4–10 days (usually 5–7).
🩺

Key signs

High fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle & joint pain (“break-bone fever”), rash, nausea, positive tourniquet test, low platelets.

🙂 Dengue without warning signs

Fever + any two of: nausea/vomiting, rash, aches, positive tourniquet test, low WBC.

⚠️ Dengue with warning signs

Abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, fluid accumulation, mucosal bleeding, lethargy/restlessness, liver enlargement, rising haematocrit with rapid platelet fall.

🚨 Severe dengue

Severe plasma leakage → shock (old “dengue shock syndrome”) or fluid with breathing distress; severe bleeding; severe organ damage (old “DHF/DSS”).

Diagnosis: NS1 antigen (first 5 days), IgM ELISA (after day 5), RT-PCR; platelet count and haematocrit for monitoring. Treatment: no specific antiviral — rest, oral fluids, paracetamol only; IV fluids by protocol; platelet transfusion only when indicated.

⚠️ Never in dengue

No aspirin, ibuprofen, diclofenac or other NSAIDs — they worsen bleeding. No steroids or antibiotics without reason. This is a classic pharmacist exam point.

PrincipleMeasures
1. Vector control (main method)Source reduction — empty, scrub and dry coolers, tanks and containers once a week (“dry day”), discard tyres/coconut shells, cover water storage; temephos in large containers; larvivorous fish in tanks/fountains; fogging/space spray only in outbreaks
2. Personal protectionFull-sleeved clothes, repellents, screens, bed nets even during daytime naps (infants, patients)
3. Case management & isolationEarly diagnosis (NS1), keep febrile patients under nets so mosquitoes cannot pick up the virus, hospital referral for warning signs
4. SurveillanceSentinel surveillance hospitals with lab support, IDSP reporting, entomological surveys (house index, container index, Breteau index)
5. Community participation & IECSchool and RWA campaigns, house-to-house checks, National Dengue Day (16 May)
6. Inter-sectoralMunicipal bodies (drainage, solid waste), construction sites, building bye-laws

In July 2026 India's drug regulator (CDSCO) approved Takeda's Qdenga (TAK-003) for people aged 4–60 years — two doses about three months apart; market launch is expected in 2027. It is not part of the Universal Immunization Programme, and an Indian candidate vaccine is still in phase-3 trials. Vector control remains the backbone of prevention.

💊 Pharmacist's role

Recommend paracetamol, ORS and fluids; refuse NSAIDs for fever during dengue season; recognise warning signs and refer; stock repellents; preach the weekly dry-day message to every customer.

12 Lymphatic Filariasis — the Culex of the Cesspool

🎬 Story mode

Deep in the dirtiest part of the bayou, the brown Culex breeds in drains and cesspools. She carries tiny thread-like worms that live for years in the lymph vessels, slowly swelling legs into “elephant legs”. The cure for a whole town is surprisingly simple: everyone swallows a few tablets once a year — together.

🪱

Agent

Filarial nematodes: Wuchereria bancrofti (about 99% of Indian cases) and Brugia malayi (pockets incl. Kerala). Adult worms live in lymphatics; microfilariae circulate in blood at night (nocturnal periodicity).
🦟

Spread

Culex quinquefasciatus (bancroftian; dirty urban water); Mansonia (brugian; ponds with water plants such as Pistia).
⏳

Incubation

Long — clinical signs usually appear 8–16 months after infection.
🩺

Key signs

Often silent; acute — filarial fever, lymphangitis, lymphadenitis (ADL attacks); chronic — lymphoedema → elephantiasis of legs/arms, hydrocele, chyluria; tropical pulmonary eosinophilia.

Diagnosis: night blood smear (collected roughly 8.30 pm–12.30 am), filarial antigen card test (can be done by day), ultrasound (“filarial dance sign”). Drugs: diethylcarbamazine (DEC, 6 mg/kg), albendazole 400 mg, ivermectin; doxycycline (kills Wolbachia bacteria in worms).

General principles of prevention & control — two pillars

💊 Pillar 1 — Stop transmission: Mass Drug Administration (MDA)

  • Annual single dose to the entire at-risk population in endemic districts — door to door, swallowed in front of the drug distributor
  • DA = DEC + albendazole, or IDA = ivermectin + DEC + albendazole (triple-drug therapy, introduced in India from 2018)
  • Excluded: children under 2, pregnant women, the seriously ill
  • Very high coverage needed (>90% of the eligible population should actually consume the drugs)
  • Transmission Assessment Surveys (TAS) decide when MDA can stop

🦵 Pillar 2 — MMDP: Morbidity Management & Disability Prevention

  • Daily washing of the affected limb with soap and water, careful drying
  • Elevation, exercise, proper footwear
  • Treat entry lesions (antifungal/antibiotic creams); manage acute attacks
  • Hydrocele surgery
  • Reduce stigma and disability

Supporting measures: vector control (anti-larval measures in drains and cesspools — larvicidal oil, Bti, removing water plants for Mansonia), personal protection (nets, repellents), health education, surveillance. National Filaria Control Programme started in 1955; now under NCVBDC.

🆕 Update — target 2027

India aims to eliminate lymphatic filariasis by 2027, three years ahead of the global 2030 target (earlier deadlines of 2015 and 2017 were missed). In February 2025 the Health Minister launched the nationwide MDA campaign in 111 endemic districts of 13 states.

13 Hypertension — the Silent Slow Villain

🎬 Story mode

Lifestyle diseases are the slow villains — like Mother Gothel quietly draining Rapunzel's years, they give no alarm until real damage is done. High blood pressure has no symptoms for years and then strikes as a stroke or heart attack — the “silent killer”. Baymax's answer: measure, measure, measure.

📘 Definitions

Hypertension — persistently raised arterial blood pressure: systolic ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥90 mmHg, on at least two separate occasions.

Essential (primary) hypertension (asked in the model paper) — high BP without any identifiable cause; it forms about 90–95% of all cases and results from genetic factors plus lifestyle (salt, obesity, inactivity, stress, alcohol). Secondary hypertension (5–10%) has a known cause — kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, endocrine disorders (Cushing's, Conn's, phaeochromocytoma), coarctation of aorta, pregnancy, drugs (oral contraceptives, NSAIDs, steroids).

⚠️ Risk factors

Non-modifiable: age, family history, genetics, sex, ethnicity.
Modifiable: high salt intake, obesity, physical inactivity, alcohol, smoking, stress, low fruit & vegetable intake, diabetes, high cholesterol.

💥 Complications (target organs)

Stroke · heart attack, heart failure, LVH · kidney failure · retinopathy · aortic dissection · peripheral arterial disease.

Prevention & control of hypertension

LevelMeasures
PrimordialPrevent risk factors from appearing in children: low-salt school meals, physical-education periods, anti-tobacco laws, front-of-pack labelling, salt reduction in processed food
Primary (population + high-risk)Salt <5 g/day (WHO); DASH-type diet (fruits, vegetables, low-fat dairy, whole grains); weight control; 30 min of exercise most days; limit alcohol; quit tobacco; stress management (yoga, meditation)
SecondaryScreening of all adults ≥30 years (opportunistic + population-based at Ayushman Arogya Mandirs under NP-NCD); early diagnosis; regular treatment — thiazides, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, calcium-channel blockers (amlodipine), β-blockers; adherence and follow-up; target usually <140/90
TertiaryManage complications — stroke and cardiac rehabilitation, dialysis; secondary prevention with antiplatelets and statins as advised

On World Hypertension Day (17 May) 2023 India launched the “75/25” initiative — to put 75 million people with hypertension or diabetes on standard care by 2025. By March 2025 about 42 million hypertensives and 25 million diabetics were on treatment (≈90% of the target). The India Hypertension Control Initiative supports simple, standard drug protocols.

14 Diabetes Mellitus — the Sweet Slow Villain

📘 Definition

Diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic disorders characterised by chronic hyperglycaemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both, with disturbances of carbohydrate, fat and protein metabolism.

Type 1

Autoimmune β-cell destruction; usually young; insulin-dependent; ~5–10%.

Type 2

Insulin resistance + relative deficiency; adults (now younger too); obesity, inactivity; ~90%.

Gestational

First detected in pregnancy; risk to baby and later T2DM in mother.

Other types

Genetic (MODY), pancreatic disease, drug-induced (steroids).
Diagnosis (WHO/ADA): fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL · 2-h glucose ≥200 mg/dL after 75 g OGTT · HbA1c ≥6.5% · random ≥200 mg/dL with symptoms Prediabetes: IFG / IGT (2-h 140–199) / HbA1c 5.7–6.4% (ADA)

Symptoms: polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia, weight loss, tiredness, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds, recurrent infections. Complications: acute — hypoglycaemia, ketoacidosis; chronic — microvascular (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and macrovascular (heart attack, stroke, peripheral vascular disease), diabetic foot. Burden: the ICMR-INDIAB study (2023) estimated about 101 million Indians with diabetes and 136 million with prediabetes.

A. MALARIA

Introduction: Malaria is a protozoal disease caused by Plasmodium (P. vivax, P. falciparum, P. malariae, P. ovale) and transmitted by the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. It presents with paroxysms of chills, fever and sweating, anaemia and splenomegaly; P. falciparum can cause cerebral malaria. Incubation is about 12 days (Pf) to 14 days (Pv).

[Draw Fig 2.4 — life cycle: sporogony in mosquito, schizogony in liver and RBC.]

General principles of prevention and control:

  1. Early diagnosis and complete treatment (EDCT): test every fever case by microscopy or RDT; treat P. vivax with chloroquine + primaquine (14 days) and P. falciparum with ACT (artesunate + SP) + single-dose primaquine. This removes the human reservoir.
  2. Anti-larval measures: source reduction and environmental management (filling, draining, clearing water collections, covering overhead tanks), larvicides (temephos, Bti), larvivorous fish (Gambusia, guppy).
  3. Anti-adult measures: indoor residual spraying with synthetic pyrethroids/malathion/DDT; long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs); space spraying in epidemics.
  4. Personal protection: bed nets, repellents, screens, protective clothing.
  5. Chemoprophylaxis for travellers and special groups: doxycycline daily or mefloquine weekly.
  6. Surveillance: active and passive case detection; indicators — API, ABER, slide positivity rate; epidemic forecasting and rapid response.
  7. Health education and community participation; inter-sectoral action with irrigation and urban departments.

Programme: NMCP 1953 → NMEP 1958 → MPO 1977 → NVBDCP 2003–04 (now NCVBDC). The National Framework for Malaria Elimination (2016–2030) targets zero indigenous cases by 2030, with interruption of transmission by 2027.

B. CHOLERA

Introduction: Cholera is an acute diarrhoeal disease caused by Vibrio cholerae O1 (classical/El Tor) and O139, spread by the faeco-oral route through contaminated water and food. Incubation is a few hours to 5 days. It causes sudden profuse painless “rice-water” stools, vomiting and rapid dehydration, which can kill within hours.

Principle of treatment: rapid rehydration with ORS (IV Ringer's lactate if severe) — this alone prevents most deaths; antibiotics (single-dose doxycycline in adults, azithromycin for children/pregnant women) reduce duration and spread; zinc for children.

Prevention and control:

  1. Verification and notification of cases; epidemiological investigation; surveillance through IDSP.
  2. Early case finding and treatment — ORS depots and treatment centres.
  3. Isolation with enteric precautions; disinfection of stools, vomit, linen (bleaching powder, cresol).
  4. Safe water supply — chlorination (residual 0.5 mg/L), boiling; repair of leaking pipes.
  5. Sanitation — sanitary latrines, no open defecation, refuse disposal, fly control.
  6. Food hygiene — eat freshly cooked hot food, avoid raw seafood and street food in outbreaks.
  7. Contacts — surveillance for 5 days; chemoprophylaxis only for household contacts (mass chemoprophylaxis not advised).
  8. Vaccination — killed oral cholera vaccine (2 doses) in endemic hot-spots and outbreaks; injectable vaccine is not recommended.
  9. Health education — hand-washing with soap, ORS preparation, early reporting.

Conclusion

Malaria is a vector-borne and cholera a water-borne disease, but both are controlled by the same logic — treat the source early, cut the route (mosquito or water) and protect the host. With these principles India is moving towards malaria elimination by 2030, and cholera deaths can be almost eliminated with ORS.

Cholera — acute diarrhoeal disease by Vibrio cholerae O1/O139; faeco-oral spread through water and food; incubation hours to 5 days; profuse rice-water stools, vomiting, dehydration and shock.

Treatment principle: immediate rehydration (ORS; IV Ringer's lactate in severe cases), antibiotics (doxycycline 300 mg single dose in adults), zinc in children.

Prevention & control: (1) verification, notification and IDSP surveillance; (2) early case detection and treatment centres; (3) isolation and disinfection of excreta and linen; (4) chlorination/boiling of water; (5) sanitary latrines, no open defecation, fly control; (6) food hygiene; (7) contacts watched for 5 days, prophylaxis only for household contacts; (8) oral cholera vaccine in high-risk areas; (9) health education on hand-washing and ORS.

Conclusion: safe water, sanitation and ORS are the three pillars that prevent cholera deaths.

Q. Explain the general principles of prevention and control of SARS.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS May 2022 · ~12 min

SARS (severe acute respiratory syndrome) is a viral respiratory illness caused by the SARS coronavirus. It emerged in southern China in November 2002 and spread to 29 countries by July 2003 (about 8,096 cases, 774 deaths). Bats are the reservoir; civet cats the likely intermediate host.

Transmission: respiratory droplets, close contact, contaminated surfaces; incubation 2–7 days (up to 10). Features: high fever, myalgia, dry cough, breathlessness, atypical pneumonia, ARDS.

Principles of prevention and control:

  1. Early detection using a case definition; fever screening.
  2. Isolation of suspected/probable cases with airborne and contact precautions.
  3. Contact tracing and quarantine of close contacts for 10 days with temperature monitoring.
  4. Hospital infection control — N95 masks, gowns, gloves, eye protection, hand hygiene, negative-pressure rooms.
  5. Community measures — respiratory etiquette, masks for symptomatic persons, hand-washing, avoiding crowds.
  6. Travel measures — airport screening, WHO alerts and travel advisories.
  7. Surveillance, international reporting and laboratory diagnosis (RT-PCR).
  8. Supportive treatment — oxygen, ventilation (no specific antiviral or vaccine).

Conclusion: SARS was contained within months without a vaccine — proof that isolation, quarantine and infection control work.

Q. Explain the general principles of prevention and control of influenza · What are the principles of influenza control?
Short 5m · KUHS May 2022 · Brief 2m · Jul 2023 · ~12 min

Influenza is an acute viral respiratory infection caused by influenza viruses A, B and C (Orthomyxoviridae). Type A shows antigenic drift (yearly epidemics) and shift (pandemics — 1918, 1957, 1968, 2009). Spread by droplets and fomites; incubation 18–72 hours; sudden fever, body ache, sore throat, dry cough; complication — pneumonia in high-risk groups.

Principles of control:

  1. Surveillance — WHO Global Influenza Surveillance and Response System and national labs identify circulating strains and guide vaccine composition.
  2. Vaccination — annual inactivated vaccine for elderly, pregnant women, young children, chronic patients and health workers.
  3. Respiratory and hand hygiene — cover coughs, masks, hand-washing.
  4. Isolation of the sick and social distancing — stay home when ill; avoid mass gatherings in pandemics.
  5. Antiviral drugs — oseltamivir early for high-risk/severe cases and selected contacts (MoHFW categories A, B, C).
  6. Pandemic preparedness — stockpiles, hospital readiness, risk communication; culling of infected poultry for avian flu.

✂️ 2-mark version: surveillance, vaccination, hygiene, isolation, antivirals, preparedness — one line each.

Q. Explain on drug addiction and drug abuse · Drug addiction · Define drug abuse.
Short 5m · KUHS May 2022 · Brief 2m · Nov 2022 & Model · ~12 min

Drug abuse: self-administration of a drug for non-medical reasons, in quantities and frequencies that impair functioning and may cause physical, social or emotional harm.

Drug addiction (WHO): a state of periodic or chronic intoxication produced by repeated consumption of a drug, characterised by (i) compulsion to continue taking it and obtain it by any means, (ii) tendency to increase the dose, (iii) psychic and physical dependence, and (iv) harmful effects on the individual and society.

Addiction vs habituation: compulsion vs desire; tolerance vs little dose increase; physical + psychic vs psychic dependence; withdrawal present vs absent; harms society vs mainly the individual.

Common drugs: alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, opioids (heroin, codeine syrups, tramadol), sedatives, stimulants (cocaine, amphetamines), hallucinogens, inhalants.

Causes: curiosity, peer pressure, stress, family problems, easy availability, unemployment, psychiatric illness.

Effects: overdose, organ damage, HIV/hepatitis C, psychosis, suicide, crime, family breakdown.

Prevention & control: Primary — health education, life skills, parental involvement, NDPS Act 1985, prescription-drug control (Schedule H1/X); Secondary — early identification and counselling; Tertiary — detoxification, opioid substitution, relapse-prevention drugs, psychotherapy, self-help groups, rehabilitation. Nasha Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan (2020), helpline 14446, MANAS 1933.

✂️ 2-mark “Drug addiction”: WHO definition with its four features. 2-mark “Define drug abuse”: definition + 2 examples (alcohol, codeine syrup misuse).

Q. Explain prevention and control of acute respiratory infections.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Jul 2023 · ~12 min

ARI — acute infection of any part of the respiratory tract lasting less than 30 days; upper (cold, pharyngitis, otitis media) and lower (bronchiolitis, pneumonia). Leading cause of death in under-5 children. Agents: RSV, influenza, measles virus, S. pneumoniae, Hib.

Risk factors: low birth weight, malnutrition, no breastfeeding, incomplete immunization, indoor smoke, passive smoking, overcrowding.

Case management (IMNCI): count breaths for 1 minute — fast breathing ≥60 (<2 months), ≥50 (2–12 months), ≥40 (1–5 years). No pneumonia → home care; pneumonia (fast breathing/chest indrawing) → oral amoxicillin; severe pneumonia (danger signs) → first dose antibiotic and urgent referral.

Prevention — Protect, Prevent, Treat: exclusive breastfeeding, nutrition, vitamin A, hand-washing · vaccines (MR, pentavalent/Hib, PCV, DPT, BCG, influenza), clean cooking fuel, no smoking indoors, good ventilation · early detection by ASHAs, standard treatment, pulse oximetry, oxygen and referral.

Programmes: ARI control programme (1990) → CSSM → RCH → IMNCI; SAANS campaign (2019); World Pneumonia Day 12 November.

Q. Discuss the control of hypertension.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS May 2022 · ~3 min

Hypertension (BP ≥140/90 mmHg) is controlled by: lifestyle — salt <5 g/day, DASH diet rich in fruits and vegetables, weight reduction, 30 min exercise daily, limiting alcohol, stopping tobacco, stress management; screening of all adults ≥30 years (NP-NCD) and early diagnosis; regular drug treatment — thiazides, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, calcium-channel blockers, β-blockers — with adherence and follow-up; and management of complications. The “rule of halves” shows why screening and adherence are vital.

Q. What is essential hypertension?
Brief · 2 marks · Model paper · ~3 min

Essential (primary) hypertension is a persistent rise of blood pressure (≥140/90 mmHg) for which no specific underlying cause can be identified. It accounts for about 90–95% of all hypertension and results from an interaction of genetic predisposition with risk factors such as high salt intake, obesity, physical inactivity, stress, alcohol and smoking. It differs from secondary hypertension, which is due to a known cause such as kidney or endocrine disease.

Q. Discuss the control of diabetes mellitus.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Jul 2023 · ~3 min

Diabetes mellitus (FPG ≥126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥6.5%) is controlled by: primary — healthy diet, normal weight, 150 min/week exercise, no tobacco; secondary — screening of adults ≥30 years and high-risk persons (Indian Diabetes Risk Score), early diagnosis, glycaemic control (metformin, other oral drugs, insulin) and control of BP and cholesterol (“ABC”); tertiary — yearly eye, kidney and foot checks to prevent complications. Patient education and self-monitoring are essential.

(1) Malaria — Plasmodium vivax/falciparum — Anopheles. (2) Dengue — dengue virus — Aedes aegypti. (3) Chikungunya — chikungunya virus — Aedes. (4) Lymphatic filariasis — Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi — Culex, Mansonia. (5) Japanese encephalitis — JE virus — Culex. (6) Kala-azar — Leishmania donovani — sandfly. Q. Discuss the prevention methods of chikungunya.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS May 2022 · ~3 min

Chikungunya (alphavirus, spread by day-biting Aedes) is prevented by: eliminating breeding sites — weekly emptying of coolers and containers, discarding tyres and coconut shells, covering tanks; larvicides (temephos) and larvivorous fish; fogging during outbreaks; personal protection — full-sleeved clothes, repellents, screens and bed nets during the day; keeping patients under nets in the first week of fever; early reporting and community health education.

Q. Mention the general principles of prevention of dengue · Give measures for effective control of dengue.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Dec 2023 & Model · ~3 min

Dengue (Flavivirus, 4 serotypes, Aedes aegypti) is controlled by: vector control — source reduction (weekly dry day, covering water containers, removing junk), temephos, larvivorous fish, fogging in outbreaks; personal protection — repellents, full-sleeved clothing, daytime bed nets; early diagnosis (NS1/IgM) and case management with paracetamol and fluids (no NSAIDs), patients under nets; surveillance (sentinel hospitals, IDSP, Breteau index); community participation and IEC (National Dengue Day 16 May).

Q. Mention the prevention of Ebola virus.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Dec 2023 · ~3 min

Ebola (filovirus; fruit bats reservoir; spread by contact with blood/body fluids; incubation 2–21 days) is prevented by: avoiding contact with bats, monkeys and bush-meat; early isolation of cases in treatment units; strict infection control and PPE for health workers; contact tracing with 21-day follow-up; safe and dignified burials; hand hygiene; condom use by male survivors; ring vaccination with rVSV-ZEBOV (Ervebo); traveller screening and community education.

Q. Mention the prevention of lymphatic filariasis · General principle involved in the prevention of lymphatic filariasis.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Jul 2023 & Model · ~3 min

Lymphatic filariasis (W. bancrofti, B. malayi; vector Culex/Mansonia) is prevented by two pillars: (1) interruption of transmission by annual Mass Drug Administration to the whole at-risk population — DEC + albendazole or the triple-drug IDA (ivermectin + DEC + albendazole), with high coverage; (2) Morbidity Management and Disability Prevention — limb hygiene, care of lymphoedema, hydrocele surgery. Supported by vector control (anti-larval measures in drains), personal protection and health education. India targets elimination by 2027.

Q. Write the general principles of prevention and control of diabetes mellitus. Predicted
Essay · 10 marks · Predicted (Brainkart long essay; KUHS brief Jul 2023) · ~25 min
Definition & types (1.5m) → diagnostic criteria (1m) → risk factors & burden (1.5m) → complications (1m) → prevention by levels (4m) → programme & conclusion (1m)

1. Definition and types

Diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic disorders with chronic hyperglycaemia due to defects in insulin secretion, action or both. Types: type 1 (autoimmune, insulin-dependent), type 2 (insulin resistance; ~90%), gestational and other specific types.

2. Diagnosis

FPG ≥126 mg/dL; 2-h OGTT ≥200 mg/dL; HbA1c ≥6.5%; random ≥200 mg/dL with symptoms. Prediabetes — IFG, IGT, HbA1c 5.7–6.4%.

3. Risk factors and burden

Non-modifiable: age, family history, ethnicity (Asian Indians develop diabetes at lower BMI), history of GDM. Modifiable: obesity (especially central), physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, smoking, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, stress. ICMR-INDIAB (2023): about 101 million diabetics and 136 million prediabetics in India.

4. Complications

Acute: hypoglycaemia, DKA, hyperosmolar state. Chronic: retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, CAD, stroke, peripheral vascular disease, diabetic foot.

5. Prevention and control

Primordial: healthy food environment, school physical activity, anti-obesity and anti-tobacco policies.

Primary: (a) population strategy — balanced diet low in refined carbohydrates and sugar, high in fibre; maintain BMI <23; 150 min/week exercise; avoid tobacco and alcohol; (b) high-risk strategy — intensive lifestyle change for people with prediabetes, obesity or GDM history.

Secondary: screening of all adults ≥30 years and high-risk groups (random capillary glucose, Indian Diabetes Risk Score); early diagnosis; treatment — diet, exercise, metformin, other oral agents, insulin; target HbA1c around <7%, BP and lipid control (ABC); self-monitoring and patient education.

Tertiary: annual fundus, renal (urine albumin, creatinine) and foot examination; foot care and footwear; laser therapy, dialysis, amputation care and rehabilitation.

6. Programme

NPCDCS (2010), renamed NP-NCD (2023): population-based screening at Ayushman Arogya Mandirs, NCD clinics at district hospitals and CHCs; the 75/25 initiative (2023) to bring 75 million hypertensives and diabetics under standard care.

Conclusion

Type 2 diabetes is largely preventable. Lifestyle change for the population, screening of high-risk people and good long-term control — with the pharmacist as educator — can stop India's diabetes epidemic.

Q. Describe the general principles, prevention and control of dengue. Predicted
Essay · 10 marks · Predicted (Brainkart long essay; KUHS brief Dec 2023 & Model) · ~25 min
Introduction & agent (1m) → vector & transmission (1.5m) → clinical features with phases diagram (2m) → diagnosis & treatment (1.5m) → prevention & control principles (3m) → conclusion (1m)

1. Introduction

Dengue is an acute viral febrile illness caused by the dengue virus (Flavivirus; serotypes DENV-1 to 4) and transmitted by Aedes aegypti and Ae. albopictus. It is the fastest-spreading mosquito-borne viral disease and occurs in urban and peri-urban India, especially after the monsoon.

2. Vector and transmission

Aedes is a day-biting, black-and-white striped mosquito that breeds in clean water in artificial containers — coolers, tyres, pots, flower vases. Eggs survive drying for months. Incubation in man is 4–10 days.

3. Clinical features

Febrile phase — high fever, headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, rash. Critical phase around defervescence — plasma leakage, falling platelets, rising haematocrit; warning signs include abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy, liver enlargement. Severe dengue — shock, severe bleeding, organ impairment. Recovery phase follows. [Draw Fig 2.5.]

4. Diagnosis and treatment

NS1 antigen (first 5 days), IgM ELISA, RT-PCR; platelet count and haematocrit. Treatment is supportive — rest, oral fluids, paracetamol; IV fluids by protocol; no aspirin/NSAIDs; platelets only when indicated.

5. Prevention and control

  1. Vector control — source reduction (weekly dry day, covering and cleaning containers), temephos, larvivorous fish, environmental management, fogging during outbreaks.
  2. Personal protection — repellents, full-sleeved clothing, screens, daytime bed nets.
  3. Early diagnosis and case management — keep patients under nets; refer when warning signs appear.
  4. Surveillance — sentinel surveillance hospitals, IDSP, entomological indices (house, container, Breteau).
  5. Community participation and IEC — school campaigns, house-to-house surveys, National Dengue Day 16 May.
  6. Inter-sectoral coordination — municipal solid-waste management, construction-site rules.
  7. Vaccine — Qdenga approved by CDSCO in July 2026 (ages 4–60, two doses), not yet in the national programme.

Conclusion

As there is no specific drug, dengue control depends on denying Aedes its breeding places — a community responsibility — and on early recognition of warning signs.

Q. Write a note on general principles of prevention and control of cancer. Predicted
Essay · 10 marks · Predicted (Brainkart long essay) · ~25 min
Definition & burden (1m) → risk factors (2.5m) → warning signs CAUTION (1m) → primary prevention (2m) → screening/secondary (2m) → tertiary & programmes (1m) → conclusion (0.5m)

1. Definition

Cancer is uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells that invade tissues and metastasise. In India oral and lung cancers lead in men, breast and cervical cancers in women.

2. Risk factors

Tobacco (smoked and smokeless), alcohol, infections (HPV, hepatitis B/C, H. pylori), unhealthy diet and obesity, physical inactivity, radiation (UV, ionising), occupational carcinogens (asbestos, benzene, aniline dyes), air pollution, genetic factors and age.

3. Warning signals — CAUTION

Change in bowel/bladder habits; a sore that does not heal; unusual bleeding or discharge; thickening or lump; indigestion or difficulty swallowing; obvious change in a wart or mole; nagging cough or hoarseness.

4. Primary prevention

Tobacco control (COTPA 2003, taxes, cessation), avoiding alcohol, healthy diet and weight, exercise, hepatitis B vaccination (UIP) and HPV vaccination (nationwide single-dose campaign for 14-year-old girls launched 28 February 2026), safe sex, sun protection, occupational safety.

5. Secondary prevention — screening

Population-based screening of people aged 30+ under NP-NCD for oral (oral visual examination), breast (clinical breast examination) and cervical (VIA) cancers; awareness of warning signs for early presentation.

6. Tertiary prevention and programmes

Surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy; palliative care with pain relief; rehabilitation. National Cancer Control Programme (1975–76) → NPCDCS (2010) → NP-NCD (2023); Regional Cancer Centres; National Cancer Registry Programme.

Conclusion

A large share of cancers can be prevented by avoiding tobacco and alcohol, vaccinating against HPV and hepatitis B, and screening early — cheaper and kinder than late treatment.

What is SARS? A severe viral pneumonia caused by SARS coronavirus, which spread from southern China to 29 countries in 2002–03 (≈8,096 cases, 774 deaths); spread by droplets; controlled by isolation, quarantine and infection control.
