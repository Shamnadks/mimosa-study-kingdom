1 Regulatory Requirements for Herbal Medicines

Every kingdom has its own rules for potions — but all of them want the same three things: quality, safety and efficacy.

🎬 Story mode — the kingdoms' potion laws

Imagine Rapunzel's healing flower being sold across Disney kingdoms. In Corona (India) the King's ministry for traditional medicine checks that it follows the old books. In Agrabah (USA) it may be sold as a "supplement" without prior approval, but it must not claim to cure a disease. In Arendelle (EU) it needs proof of long traditional use before getting a simple registration. And the Fairy Godmother (WHO) writes guidebooks that help every kingdom make good rules. That is exactly how herbal medicines are regulated around the world.

📘 Key definitions (WHO)

Herbal medicines include herbs (crude plant material — leaves, flowers, fruit, seeds, stems, wood, bark, roots, rhizomes; whole, fragmented or powdered), herbal materials (also fresh juices, gums, fixed and essential oils, resins, powders), herbal preparations (powdered materials, extracts, tinctures, fatty oils made by extraction, fractionation, purification, concentration etc.) and finished herbal products (herbal preparations from one or more herbs, with excipients). Products to which chemically defined active substances (synthetic or isolated constituents) have been added are not considered herbal.

Regulation of herbal medicines = the legal framework (laws, rules, guidelines, licensing, inspection) that controls their manufacture, quality, claims, labelling, sale, advertising and safety monitoring.

Specific objectives of herbal drug regulation (KUHS Model brief)

🛡️

Safety

Protect the public from toxic, adulterated, contaminated (heavy metals, pesticides, microbes) and spurious products.
🏅

Quality

Ensure GACP/GMP, pharmacopoeial standards, standardization and stability.
💪

Efficacy

Ensure claims are supported by traditional use or clinical evidence.
🏷️

Truthful information

Control labelling, claims and misleading advertisements.
🦜

Post-marketing safety

Pharmacovigilance and recall of unsafe products.
🌍

Trade & heritage

Harmonise standards for export/import; protect biodiversity and traditional knowledge; promote rational use.

Why herbal regulation is difficult

  • The same herb is a medicine in one country, a food or dietary supplement in another (different legal status).
  • Complex, variable composition; actives often unknown; adulteration and contamination common.
  • Efficacy based on traditional use rather than modern clinical trials.
  • Sold without prescription, by many non-physician providers, often online.
  • Few countries had full systems — WHO surveys found that fewer than half of Member States regulated herbal medicines in the early 2000s.

🏛️ Authorities

  • Ministry of Ayush (formed 9 Nov 2014; earlier Dept. of ISM&H 1995, renamed Dept. of AYUSH 2003) — policy for Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, Homoeopathy.

1.2 United States 🇺🇸

Dietary supplements — DSHEA 1994

  • Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act, 1994: herbs/botanicals sold as dietary supplements (regulated like foods), no pre-market approval.
  • Manufacturer is responsible for safety; FDA must prove a product unsafe to remove it (e.g. ephedra alkaloid supplements banned in 2004).
  • Only structure/function claims ("supports joint health"), never disease claims; label carries the disclaimer that FDA has not evaluated the statement and the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease; FDA notified within 30 days of marketing.
  • New Dietary Ingredient notification 75 days before marketing (ingredients not sold before 15 Oct 1994).
  • cGMP: 21 CFR Part 111 (2007); serious adverse event reports to FDA within 15 business days (2006 Act).
VS

Botanical drugs — NDA route

  • If a disease claim is made, the herb is a drug: IND → clinical trials → NDA (or OTC monograph for some, e.g. senna, psyllium laxatives).
  • FDA Botanical Drug Development Guidance for Industry (2004; revised 2016): allows mixtures, focuses on raw-material control, batch consistency, multiple markers, clinical data.
  • Approved botanical drugs: Veregen (sinecatechins, green-tea extract, 2006) and crofelemer (from Croton lechleri latex, 2012).
  • Standards: USP–NF botanical monographs.

1.3 European Union 🇪🇺

📘 EU definition of herbal medicinal product (KUHS Model brief)

Herbal medicinal product (Directive 2001/83/EC as amended by Directive 2004/24/EC): any medicinal product exclusively containing as active ingredients one or more herbal substances, or one or more herbal preparations, or one or more such herbal substances in combination with one or more such herbal preparations.

Herbal substances = whole, fragmented or cut plants, plant parts, algae, fungi, lichens in unprocessed (usually dried, sometimes fresh) form, plus certain untreated exudates; defined by plant part and full botanical name. Herbal preparations = obtained by treating herbal substances (extraction, distillation, expression, fractionation, purification, concentration, fermentation) — e.g. comminuted substances, tinctures, extracts, essential oils, expressed juices, processed exudates.

Herbal medicines in the EU are regulated as medicinal products under Directive 2001/83/EC. The Traditional Herbal Medicinal Products Directive (THMPD) 2004/24/EC added a simplified procedure. The Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products (HMPC) at the EMA (set up in 2004) prepares EU herbal monographs and the EU list of herbal substances. The transition period for products already on the market ended on 30 April 2011.

Conditions for traditional use registration (THMPD)

  • Indications suitable for use without medical supervision (self-care, minor ailments).
  • Administered only at a specified strength and posology; oral, external or inhalation routes only.
  • Proof of traditional medicinal use for at least 30 years before application, including at least 15 years within the EU.
  • Sufficient safety data (bibliographic review + expert report); efficacy plausible from long-standing use — no clinical trials required.
  • Full quality dossier (GMP, specifications, stability) is still required.
  • Label states that it is a traditional herbal medicinal product for the indication, based exclusively on long-standing use.

2 WHO Guidelines on Safety Monitoring of Herbal Medicines in Pharmacovigilance Systems (2004)

Zazu's morning report: every strange event in the kingdom must reach the king quickly.

🎬 Story mode — Zazu's morning report

Every morning Zazu flies up to Pride Rock and reports to King Mufasa: "The giraffes are feeling a bit ill, the elephants are acting strangely…". Mufasa listens, checks, and acts to protect the kingdom. In pharmacovigilance, doctors, pharmacists, traditional practitioners and even patients are the "Zazus" who report suspected reactions; the national PV centre is Mufasa, who assesses each report; and the Uppsala Monitoring Centre in Sweden is the great Circle of Life where reports from all kingdoms come together. When young Simba ignores Zazu, trouble follows — that's under-reporting, the biggest weakness of herbal PV.

📘 Definitions (WHO glossary)

Pharmacovigilance — the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other possible drug-related problems.

Adverse drug reaction (ADR) — a response to a drug that is noxious and unintended and occurs at doses normally used in humans for prophylaxis, diagnosis or therapy, or for modification of physiological function.

Adverse event — any untoward medical occurrence during treatment that does not necessarily have a causal relationship with the treatment. Serious adverse event — results in death, is life-threatening, requires/prolongs hospitalisation, or results in persistent/significant disability.

Side effect — any unintended effect at normal doses related to the pharmacological properties of the drug. Unexpected ADR — nature or severity not consistent with the label/authorisation. Signal — reported information on a possible causal relationship between an adverse event and a drug, previously unknown or incompletely documented (usually needs more than one report). Spontaneous reporting — voluntary submission of case reports by health professionals and manufacturers to the national authority.

Background of the guideline

  • Published by WHO, Geneva, 2004, after a Working Group meeting (Amsterdam, 2002) and a WHO Consultation hosted by Health Canada (Vancouver, February 2004), with the Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC).
  • Responded to requests from national PV centres (2000, 2001), ICDRA, and resolution WHA56.31 (2003), which urged Member States to monitor the safety of herbal and traditional medicines.
  • Key idea: include herbal medicines in existing national pharmacovigilance systems (not a separate system); where no system exists, set up a comprehensive one.
  • Part II reproduces the UMC guideline "Safety monitoring of medicinal products: guidelines for setting up and running a pharmacovigilance centre" (2000).
⚠️ Why it was needed — real problems cited by WHO
  • "Natural = safe" misconception, although some plants are inherently toxic.
  • Herbal capsules found to contain hidden betamethasone (corticosteroid side-effects).
  • Misidentification of species → plants containing aristolochic acid used by mistake → severe kidney failure in several countries.
  • Ginkgo biloba associated with bleeding (prolonged clotting, haematomas, intracranial haemorrhage).
  • A well-known traditional herbal formula (the Kampo medicine Sho-saiko-to) caused interstitial pneumonia when combined with interferon.

Objectives of the guidelines

  1. Support Member States, within the WHO International Drug Monitoring Programme, to strengthen national PV capacity for herbal medicines.
  2. Give technical guidance on good pharmacovigilance and on including herbal medicines in existing systems (or building inclusive systems).
  3. Provide standard definitions of terms.
  4. Promote internationally coordinated information exchange.
  5. Promote the safe and proper use of herbal medicines.

Challenges in monitoring the safety of herbal medicines (Chapter 3)

📜 Regulation

  • Legal status varies — prescription, OTC, food, supplement — even for the same herb in different countries.
  • Many herbs enter the market straight as non-prescription products, skipping the usual prescription-phase safety monitoring.
  • Mail-order and internet sales with weak control.
  • Regulatory information not shared between authorities and PV centres.

🧪 Quality assurance & control

  • Adulteration with undeclared drugs; contamination with heavy metals, microbes, pesticides.
  • QC of mixture products is far more complex than for single chemicals; depends on raw-material quality (GACP).
  • The same pharmacopoeial name may refer to a different plant or processing in another country.

👩‍⚕️ Appropriate use — providers

  • Many providers are not physicians (traditional practitioners, pharmacists, nurses) and are excluded from reporting schemes.
  • Health professionals often lack knowledge of herbal medicines.
  • Traditional medicines used outside their traditional context — different doses, extraction methods, new indications, combined with modern drugs.

🙋 Appropriate use — patients

  • "Natural means safe" belief.
  • Patients do not tell doctors they take herbs (and vice-versa).
  • Self-medication without supervision.
  • Professionals should ask patients directly, respectfully and persistently about all products used.

Additional practical difficulties (useful for "challenges in monitoring ADRs"): identification of the product (vernacular names, multi-herb products, unknown ingredients); no standard coding; difficult causality because of quality problems and concomitant drugs; delayed or chronic toxicity missed; under-reporting and fear of liability; lack of analytical facilities to test suspected products.

Safety monitoring of herbal medicines (Chapter 4)

4.1 Sources of reports

🩺

Health professionals

Physicians, pharmacists, nurses — main spontaneous reporters; community pharmacists vital for OTC herbs.
🙋‍♀️

Consumers

Accepted as a serious source; for OTC herbs may be the only source of signals.
🏭

Manufacturers

Producers, importers, distributors, marketing-authorisation holders.
☎️

Other sources

National poisons centres, drug information centres, consumer organisations, clinical trials & studies; traditional medicine practitioners.

CIOMS principle quoted by WHO: the value of a report depends on its quality, not on who sent it.

4.2 Herbal products targeted

By regulatory status (prescription herbal medicines, non-prescription herbal medicines, other herbal products for health care) and by registration/marketing status (in clinical trials, under post-marketing surveillance, under re-evaluation, on the market, dietary supplements).

4.3 Reporting of suspected adverse reactions

Who reports to whom?

  • Health professionals → national PV centre
  • Patients/consumers → their physician or herbal provider (or directly to the PV centre, consumer organisations, manufacturers)
  • Manufacturers → national PV centre or regulatory authority

How: a single reporting form for all medicines (herbal included); also accept telephone, letter, e-mail; send a sample of the product and its packaging if possible; simple forms and education for traditional providers.

What information should a report contain?

  • Patient identification (confidential), age, sex, brief medical history
  • Details of the herbal product: Latin binomial + vernacular name, brand/ingredient names, plant part, preparation method, manufacturer, country of origin, batch number, expiry date, provider
  • Administration: dose, quantity, dosage form, route, start/stop dates
  • Indication / reason for use
  • Reaction: onset, description, severity, seriousness, investigations, course, outcome, dechallenge/rechallenge
  • All other medicines used (including self-medication)
  • Risk factors (age, renal impairment, previous exposure, allergies, misuse)
  • Reporter's name and address (confidential)

Recording & coding: UMC proposes the WHO Drug Dictionary for herbal product names and the Herbal ATC (HATC) classification for therapeutic grouping; the exact Latin binomial (genus, species, author, family), plant part and preparation should be recorded; PV centres should work with pharmacognosists and botanists. Information must never be used commercially; patient and reporter identities stay confidential.

4.4 Assessment of case reports — causality

Assessment is based on: time relationship; outcome of dechallenge and rechallenge; known pharmacology; medical/pharmacological plausibility; likelihood or exclusion of other causes; testing for adulterants or contaminants; and inappropriate use (wrong preparation, wrong plant, overdose, outside the tradition).

WHO-UMC causality categoryMeaning (own words)
1. CertainPlausible time relation; not explained by disease or other drugs; plausible response to withdrawal; definitive (rechallenge if needed)
2. Probable / likelyReasonable time sequence; unlikely due to other causes; improves on withdrawal; rechallenge not required
3. PossibleReasonable time sequence but could also be explained by disease or other drugs; withdrawal data unclear
4. UnlikelyTime relation makes a causal link improbable; other explanations plausible
5. Conditional / unclassifiedMore data are essential or being examined
6. Unassessable / unclassifiableInformation insufficient or contradictory and cannot be supplemented

Further steps: feedback to reporters (acknowledge every report); signal detection nationally and internationally (data mining in the global WHO database, VigiBase, managed by UMC); an advisory committee of experts (including pharmacognosy, phytochemistry, traditional medicine); investigation of serious reactions — medical, pharmaceutical, pharmacognostical/phytochemical (authentication), physicochemical analysis and pharmacoepidemiology; access to analytical laboratories.

4.5 Data management & 5. Communication

  • Data quality (complete, accurate, no duplicates), secure storage, regular analysis, transmission to UMC.
  • Communication at all levels — PV centre ↔ health professionals, herbal providers, consumers, regulators, manufacturers, other countries, UMC and mass media.
  • Risk-communication tools: ADR bulletins and journal articles, public advisories/warnings, "Dear Health Professional" letters; spread via internet, mailings, media briefings, patient associations, professional meetings — in local languages.

India — pharmacovigilance for ASU&H drugs

  • Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI) (2010; national coordination centre at the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, Ghaziabad) — covers modern medicines.
  • National Pharmacovigilance Programme for Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani & Homoeopathy drugs — Ministry of Ayush central sector scheme in implementation since December 2017; three-tier network: National PV Coordination Centre at the All India Institute of Ayurveda (AIIA), New Delhi → Intermediary PV Centres (national Ayush institutes) → Peripheral PV Centres (Ayush colleges/hospitals).

3 Comparison of Various Herbal Pharmacopoeias

Every kitchen has its recipe book — and every country has its book of standards.

🎬 Story mode — Tiana's and Remy's recipe books

Tiana cooks gumbo from her father's well-loved notebook in New Orleans; Remy follows Chef Gusteau's French recipes in Paris. Both dishes are delicious, but the books are written differently — different ingredients, measures and tests of "doneness". Herbal pharmacopoeias are the same: the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia describes an herb with rasa, guna, virya and vipaka; the European Pharmacopoeia gives HPTLC fingerprints and assay limits; the Chinese Pharmacopoeia covers decoction pieces and patent medicines. Comparing them is like comparing Tiana's and Remy's cookbooks.

📘 Exam definitions

Pharmacopoeia — an official (usually legally binding) compendium of standards, published by a government-recognised authority, containing monographs that define the identity, purity, strength and quality of drugs, with the analytical methods to test them.

Herbal monograph — the standard for one herbal drug or preparation: title (Latin binomial and family), definition (plant part, collection), synonyms/vernacular names, macroscopic and microscopic description, identity tests (chemical, TLC/HPTLC), purity tests (foreign matter, total & acid-insoluble ash, extractive values, loss on drying, heavy metals, pesticide residues, microbial limits, aflatoxins), assay of markers, storage — and in traditional pharmacopoeias also properties, actions, therapeutic uses, important formulations and dose.

4 Role of Chemical and Biological Markers in Standardization

📘 Exam definitions

Standardization of herbal drugs — the process of prescribing a set of standards or inherent characteristics, constant parameters and definite qualitative and quantitative values that carry an assurance of quality, efficacy, safety and reproducibility of a herbal drug or product.

Marker (chemical marker) — a chemically defined constituent or group of constituents of a herbal substance, preparation or product that is used for quality-control purposes, whether or not it has therapeutic activity (EMA concept).

Biomarker (biological marker) — a characteristic that is objectively measured and evaluated as an indicator of normal biological processes, pathogenic processes or pharmacological responses to a therapeutic intervention (NIH definition, 2001). In herbal standardization, biological markers measure the herb's biological activity (bioassay, enzyme inhibition, DNA identity or clinical response) rather than a chemical's amount. Example: inhibition of platelet aggregation by Ginkgo extract; fall in blood glucose / HbA1c with bitter gourd; serum cortisol for Ashwagandha.

Classification of chemical markers

⭐

Active markers

Constituents (or groups) generally accepted to contribute to the therapeutic activity. e.g. sennosides (senna, laxative), hyperforin (St John's wort), ginkgo flavone glycosides & terpene lactones, andrographolide (Kalmegh), curcuminoids (turmeric).
🔎

Analytical markers

Constituents used only for control (identity, content, consistency), with no proven link to activity. e.g. hypericin (St John's wort), valerenic acid (valerian), gallic acid (Triphala), withaferin A as an identity marker.
⛔

Negative markers

Toxic or allergenic constituents that must be limited or absent. e.g. ginkgolic acids in Ginkgo extracts, aristolochic acids (Aristolochia), pyrrolizidine alkaloids (comfrey, Senecio), thujone (wormwood), safrole.

A wider textbook classification lists: therapeutic components, bioactive components, synergistic components, characteristic (unique) components, main (major) components, correlative components, toxic components and general components (common to many plants, e.g. sugars, chlorophyll — poor markers).

Qualities of an ideal marker

  • Specific (characteristic) to the plant or plant part; ideally linked to activity or toxicity.
  • Present in measurable amount; chemically stable (or a known degradation indicator).
  • Available as a pure reference standard; measurable by a validated method (HPTLC/HPLC/GC).
  • Multiple markers + whole fingerprint are better than a single marker (India's phytopharmaceutical rules ask for a defined minimum of four).

Biological markers / biomarkers in herbal standardization

🧫 Bioassay-based (biological standardization)

  • Potency by biological response when actives are unknown or chemical assay is inadequate — classic example: digitalis cardiac glycosides assayed biologically.
  • Bitterness value (gentian, chirata), haemolytic index (saponin drugs), antimicrobial potency.
  • Enzyme/receptor assays: α-glucosidase / α-amylase inhibition (antidiabetic herbs), acetylcholinesterase inhibition (Brahmi), COX inhibition, antioxidant capacity (DPPH).
  • HPTLC-bioautography — activity located on the fingerprint band.

🧬 Genetic (DNA) markers — identity

  • RAPD, AFLP, ISSR, SCAR markers and DNA barcoding (ITS2, rbcL, matK regions).
  • Authenticate species when morphology or chemistry is confusing (e.g. different Phyllanthus, Ocimum or Curcuma species, powdered drugs).
  • Unaffected by season, age or storage — the DNA "fingerprint" stays the same.

🩸 Clinical / pharmacodynamic biomarkers

  • Ginkgo — platelet aggregation; garlic/guggul — serum lipids; bitter gourd — blood glucose/HbA1c; Ashwagandha — serum cortisol; curcumin — CRP, TNF-α.
  • Used in clinical trials to link a standardised extract to a measurable effect.

⚠️ Safety biomarkers

  • Liver enzymes (ALT, AST, bilirubin) for hepatotoxic herbs (kava, black cohosh).
  • Serum creatinine / urine markers for nephrotoxicity (aristolochic acids).
  • Serum potassium and BP for liquorice excess.

Q. Describe the regulatory requirements of herbal drugs. (Also: Enumerate the regulatory requirement of herbal drugs.)
Essay · 10 marks · KUHS Model ★★★ · MGR 2022 · ~25 min · ~3 pages

1. Introduction

Herbal medicines (herbs, herbal materials, herbal preparations and finished herbal products — WHO) are used worldwide, often without prescription. Their regulation is the legal framework of laws, rules, licensing, standards and monitoring that controls their quality, safety, efficacy, claims and sale. Legal status differs between countries — medicine, traditional medicine, dietary supplement or food.

2. Objectives of regulation

Ensure safety (no toxic, adulterated, contaminated or spurious products), quality (GACP, GMP, pharmacopoeial standards), efficacy (traditional or clinical evidence), truthful labelling and advertising, pharmacovigilance, rational use, and harmonised trade while protecting biodiversity and traditional knowledge.

3. WHO

WHO guidelines guide national regulators: Assessment of herbal medicines (1991), QC methods for medicinal plant materials (1998), GACP (2003), PV safety monitoring (2004), GMP for herbal medicines (2007), monographs on selected medicinal plants (4 vols), Traditional Medicine Strategy 2025–2034 (WHA78, 2025). Principle: assess quality, safety (long use without harm or toxicology) and efficacy (traditional evidence or clinical trials), and monitor post-marketing.

4. India

  • Authorities: Ministry of Ayush; State Licensing Authorities (ASU); ASUDTAB & ASUDCC; PCIM&H (API, UPI, SPI); CDSCO for phytopharmaceuticals and COPP; FSSAI for nutraceuticals.
  • Law: D&C Act 1940 Chapter IVA (Sec 33B–33O, added 1964) and Drugs Rules 1945 Parts XVI–XVIII.
  • Licence: Form 24-D → Form 25-D; Schedule T GMP; Rule 158-B (classical vs patent/proprietary: safety and effectiveness evidence); approved testing labs (160-A–J).
  • Quality & labelling: pharmacopoeial standards; Rule 161 labelling; Schedule E(1) caution; Rule 161-B shelf life.
  • Phytopharmaceutical drugs: new drugs under NDCT Rules 2019 — standardised fraction with ≥ 4 defined markers + safety and clinical data.
  • Others: DMR Act 1954 (advertisements); National PV Programme for ASU&H drugs (AIIA, since 2017); Biological Diversity Act 2002.

5. USA

DSHEA 1994: herbs sold as dietary supplements without pre-market approval; manufacturer ensures safety; only structure/function claims with FDA disclaimer; NDI notification 75 days before marketing; cGMP 21 CFR 111 (2007); serious AE reporting. Botanical drugs making disease claims need IND/NDA under the FDA Botanical Drug Development Guidance (2004, revised 2016) — e.g. Veregen (2006), crofelemer (2012). Standards: USP–NF.

6. European Union

Herbal medicinal product = medicinal product exclusively containing herbal substances and/or herbal preparations as actives (Directive 2001/83/EC as amended by 2004/24/EC). Three routes (draw Fig 5.1): (i) full marketing authorisation with own data; (ii) well-established use — bibliographic, ≥ 10 years in EU; (iii) traditional use registration (THMPD) — ≥ 30 years' use incl. ≥ 15 in EU, safe, efficacy plausible, oral/external/inhalation, no medical supervision, full quality data, label "based exclusively on long-standing use". HMPC (EMA) issues EU herbal monographs and the EU list; transition ended 30 April 2011.

7. Other countries

Canada — Natural Health Products Regulations 2004 (NPN licence); Australia — TGA listed (AUST L) vs registered (AUST R) medicines; China — TCM regulated as drugs by NMPA with Chinese Pharmacopoeia; Japan — Kampo products under the JP.

Conclusion

Despite different approaches, all regulators aim at quality, safety and efficacy. Harmonisation through WHO guidelines, pharmacopoeias and pharmacovigilance is essential for the safe global use and trade of herbal medicines.

Q. Explain the WHO guidelines on safety monitoring of herbal medicines in pharmacovigilance systems. Predicted essay
Essay · 10 marks · bank Qs 26, 63, 88 · ~25 min

1. Introduction

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problems. In 2004 WHO published guidelines on safety monitoring of herbal medicines, after consultations in Amsterdam (2002) and Vancouver (2004) and resolution WHA56.31 (2003). Core principle: include herbal medicines in existing national PV systems, or create comprehensive systems where none exist. Problems that prompted them: hidden betamethasone in herbal capsules, aristolochic-acid nephropathy from misidentified plants, bleeding with Ginkgo, interstitial pneumonia with a Kampo formula plus interferon.

2. Objectives

Strengthen national PV capacity; give technical guidance for including herbal medicines; provide standard definitions; promote international information exchange; promote safe and proper use.

3. Challenges

  • Regulation: different legal status in different countries; many herbs enter directly as OTC; internet sales; information not shared.
  • Quality: adulteration, contamination, complex mixtures, same name for different plants.
  • Providers: non-physician providers excluded from reporting; poor knowledge; traditional medicines used outside their context and with modern drugs.
  • Patients: "natural = safe", non-disclosure, self-medication.

4. Safety monitoring

Sources of reports: health professionals, consumers, manufacturers, poisons and drug information centres, consumer organisations, clinical trials. Products targeted: by regulatory status and marketing status. Reporting: health professionals and manufacturers → national PV centre; consumers → physician/provider or directly; one common form; send product sample and packaging. Report content: patient details; herbal product (Latin binomial, vernacular name, plant part, preparation, manufacturer, batch, expiry, provider); dose and dates; indication; reaction with dechallenge/rechallenge; concomitant medicines; risk factors; reporter. Coding: WHO Drug Dictionary and Herbal ATC classification.

5. Assessment of case reports

Based on time relation, dechallenge/rechallenge, known pharmacology, plausibility, exclusion of other causes, testing for adulterants/contaminants and inappropriate use. WHO-UMC categories: certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified, unassessable/unclassifiable. Then feedback to reporters, signal detection (national and global via UMC's database), advisory committee, scientific investigation (including pharmacognostical authentication), data management. (Draw Fig 5.2.)

6. Communication

Between PV centre, professionals, herbal providers, consumers, regulators, manufacturers, other countries, UMC and media; tools — ADR bulletins, public warnings, "Dear Health Professional" letters, internet, media briefings, local languages. In India, the National PV Programme for ASU&H drugs (since Dec 2017; AIIA New Delhi as national centre; intermediary and peripheral centres) applies these principles.

Conclusion

The WHO guidelines make herbal medicines part of mainstream drug-safety monitoring, ensuring that reactions are reported, correctly attributed (including quality causes) and communicated, so that herbal medicines can be used safely.

Q. Challenges in monitoring adverse drug reactions in herbal medicines.
Short note · 5 marks · MGR Mar 2023 · bank Qs 35, 88 · ~12 min
Definition of ADR/PV (0.5m) → 8–10 challenges grouped (3.5m) → WHO solutions (1m)

An ADR is a noxious, unintended response at normal doses. WHO (2004) lists the following challenges for herbal medicines:

  1. Regulatory diversity — same herb is a drug, supplement or food in different countries; many enter directly as OTC.
  2. Quality problems — adulteration (hidden steroids), contamination, misidentification (aristolochic acid) → hard to tell true ADR from quality defect.
  3. Product identification — vernacular names, multi-herb products, unknown ingredients, no standard coding.
  4. Providers outside the system — traditional practitioners, shopkeepers, online sellers not reporting.
  5. Poor knowledge of herbs among health professionals.
  6. Patient attitudes — "natural = safe"; not disclosing herb use; self-medication.
  7. Concomitant use with modern drugs → interactions, confusing causality.
  8. Use outside tradition — new doses, extraction methods, indications.
  9. Under-reporting, fear of liability, lack of analytical labs to test suspected products.

WHO remedies: include herbal medicines in national PV, accept consumer and traditional-provider reports, one reporting form, product samples, WHO-DD/HATC coding, training, labs and communication.

Q. What is the EU definition of herbal medicinal product?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Model · 3 min

According to Directive 2001/83/EC (as amended by 2004/24/EC), a herbal medicinal product is any medicinal product exclusively containing as active ingredients one or more herbal substances or one or more herbal preparations, or one or more such herbal substances in combination with one or more such herbal preparations. (Herbal substances = mainly whole/fragmented plants or parts, unprocessed; herbal preparations = extracts, tinctures, oils, juices etc.)

Q. What are the specific objectives of herbal drug regulation?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Model · 3 min
  • Ensure quality, safety and efficacy of herbal medicines
  • Prevent adulterated, contaminated, misbranded and spurious products
  • Control labelling, claims and advertisements; promote rational use
  • Post-marketing safety monitoring (pharmacovigilance); facilitate trade while protecting traditional knowledge and biodiversity
Q. Define the term herbal drug standardization. (Also: Discuss herbal drug standardization.)
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Model · AKTU 2023 · 3 min

Herbal drug standardization is the process of prescribing a set of standards or inherent characteristics, constant parameters and definite qualitative and quantitative values that carry an assurance of quality, efficacy, safety and reproducibility. It includes authentication (macroscopy, microscopy, DNA), physicochemical parameters (ash, extractives, LOD), chromatographic fingerprint, marker assay, contaminant limits and biological activity.

Q. Define biomarker giving example.
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Model · 3 min

A biomarker is a characteristic that is objectively measured and evaluated as an indicator of normal biological processes, pathogenic processes or pharmacological responses to a therapeutic intervention. In herbal standardization it measures biological activity rather than chemical amount. Examples: inhibition of platelet aggregation by Ginkgo extract; reduction of blood glucose/HbA1c by bitter gourd; serum cortisol with Ashwagandha; liver enzymes (ALT/AST) as safety biomarkers for hepatotoxic herbs.

Q. Why standardization is essential for a herbal formulation?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Model · 3 min
  • Natural variation in raw materials (species, part, season, soil, processing)
  • Ensures batch-to-batch consistency and correct dose of actives → reproducible efficacy
  • Detects adulteration, substitution and contaminants → safety
  • Required for regulatory approval, export and credible clinical research
Q. Why safety evaluation is more complex in herbal medicines?
Brief · 2 marks · KUHS Model (also Unit 3) · 3 min

Because herbal medicines are complex, variable mixtures with often unknown actives; they may be adulterated, contaminated or misidentified; traditional use does not reveal rare, delayed or chronic toxicity; they are taken with modern drugs (interactions) and by self-medication without disclosure; multi-herb products and processing (e.g. shodhana) change toxicity; and toxicological data and ADR reports are scarce.

Q. Examples of herbal drug interaction.
Brief · 2 marks · MGR 2022 · MGR Mar 2023 · MGR Aug 2023 ★★★ · 3 min
  • St John's wort + cyclosporine / oral contraceptives / warfarin → reduced levels (CYP3A4 & P-gp induction)
  • Ginkgo or garlic + warfarin / aspirin → bleeding
  • Liquorice + digoxin / diuretics → hypokalaemia, digoxin toxicity
  • Bitter gourd + sulfonylureas → hypoglycaemia; piperine + phenytoin → raised drug levels
Q. Write a note on marker compounds.
Brief · 2 marks · AKTU 2023 · 3 min

Marker compounds are chemically defined constituents of a herbal drug used to control its identity, quality, content and stability, whether or not they are active. Types: active (sennosides, curcumin), analytical (hypericin, gallic acid) and negative (ginkgolic acids, aristolochic acids). They are measured by HPTLC/HPLC and used in specifications and stability studies.

Q. Define the term herbal medicine (and crude drug).
Brief · 2 marks · MGR 2022 (also Unit 1) · 3 min

Herbal medicines (WHO) include herbs, herbal materials, herbal preparations and finished herbal products that contain as active ingredients parts of plants or other plant materials, or combinations thereof. A crude drug is a natural substance of plant, animal or mineral origin that has undergone only collection and drying (no other processing), used as a drug or for making drugs — e.g. senna leaf, ginger rhizome.

Q. Define pharmacovigilance and give its importance. / Enlist sources of safety reports.
Brief · 2 marks · bank Qs 27, 32 · 3 min

Pharmacovigilance: the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem. Importance: early detection of unknown ADRs and interactions, improved patient safety, benefit–risk assessment, regulatory action. Sources of safety reports (WHO 2004): health professionals, consumers, manufacturers, poisons centres, drug information centres, consumer organisations, clinical trials and literature.

Q. How does FDA regulate natural products in the US?
Brief · 2 marks · bank Q54 · 3 min

Most herbal products are dietary supplements under DSHEA 1994 — no pre-market approval, manufacturer responsible for safety, only structure/function claims with a disclaimer, cGMP (21 CFR 111), NDI notification and serious AE reporting. Herbal products claiming to treat disease are botanical drugs requiring IND/NDA under FDA's Botanical Drug Development Guidance (e.g. Veregen, crofelemer).
