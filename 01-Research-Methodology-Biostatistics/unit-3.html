1 Introduction to Epidemiology

Epidemiology = "epi" (upon) + "demos" (people) + "logos" (study): the study of what falls upon people.

🎬 Story mode — the CDA at work

When a 2319 happens, the Child Detection Agency doesn't treat one monster and go home. They ask: How many monsters are affected? Where — which scare floor? When — which shift? Who — scarers or assistants? Why — what exposure do the sick ones share? And what will stop it? Those questions — distribution, determinants and control — are the definition of epidemiology. (Their big mistake? Believing "children are toxic" without ever testing it. Good epidemiology tests beliefs with data!)

📘 Exam definition (J. M. Last — quoted in Park)

Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems.

Three key words: Distribution (frequency and pattern by time, place, person) · Determinants (causes and risk factors) · Application (prevention and control). Unlike clinical medicine, the unit of study is the population, not the individual patient.

🎯 Aims (International Epidemiological Association)

  1. Describe the distribution and magnitude of health and disease problems in human populations.
  2. Identify aetiological (risk) factors in the pathogenesis of disease.
  3. Provide data essential for planning, implementing and evaluating services for prevention, control and treatment, and for setting priorities.

🧰 Uses of epidemiology (Morris)

  1. To study historically the rise and fall of disease
  2. Community diagnosis
  3. Planning and evaluation of health services
  4. Evaluation of individual risks and chances
  5. Syndrome identification
  6. Completing the natural history of disease
  7. Searching for causes and risk factors

💊 Pharmacy links

  • Pharmacoepidemiology — use and effects of drugs in large populations
  • Pharmacovigilance signal detection
  • Drug-utilisation studies
  • Post-marketing (Phase IV) studies
  • Antimicrobial-resistance surveillance
💊 Pharmacy example

The thalidomide tragedy: epidemiologists noticed a sudden rise of a rare limb defect (phocomelia) in babies (distribution), linked it to thalidomide taken by mothers in early pregnancy (determinant), and the drug was withdrawn (control). Pharmacovigilance was born from epidemiology.

2 Types of Epidemiological Studies

Two big families: watch (observational) or intervene (experimental).

🎬 Story mode — three CDA squads

Squad 1 just describes the outbreak: how many cases, which floor, which day (descriptive). Squad 2 compares sick and healthy monsters to find what exposure they differ in (analytical). Squad 3 tries a new decontamination spray on randomly chosen monsters and compares results (experimental). Describe → explain → test.

A. Descriptive studies

Descriptive studies describe the occurrence and distribution of disease in a population by time, place and person, without testing any hypothesis. They are usually the first step and they generate hypotheses.

⏰ Time

Short-term fluctuations (epidemics — common-source single exposure, continuous, or propagated), periodic fluctuations (seasonal, cyclic), long-term (secular) trends.

📍 Place

International, national, rural–urban and local variations (e.g. spot maps).

🧑 Person

Age, sex, occupation, marital status, socio-economic status, habits, blood group, behaviour.

Types of descriptive studies (Nov 2022 2-marker): (1) case report — detailed description of one patient; (2) case series — a group of similar cases; (3) cross-sectional (prevalence) surveys; (4) ecological (correlational) descriptive studies; also routine data/surveillance and time-trend studies.

Procedure of a descriptive study (Park): define the population → define the disease → describe the disease by time, place, person → measure the disease (incidence, prevalence) → compare with known indices → formulate an aetiological hypothesis.

B. Analytical studies

Test hypotheses about associations by comparing groups. Unit of study is the individual (except ecological studies, where it is the group). Types: ecological, cross-sectional, case-control (case-reference) and cohort (follow-up) — explained in topic 6.

C. Experimental (intervention) studies

The investigator deliberately alters a factor and measures its effect: RCTs (clinical trials), field trials (healthy people, e.g. vaccine trials), community trials (whole communities, e.g. fluoridation of water), and non-randomised trials — explained in topic 7.

🧠 Mnemonic — the analytical ladder
EcologicalCross-sectionalCase-controlCohort

“Every CDA Case Counts.” — weakest to strongest observational evidence.

3 Standard Measures in Epidemiological Studies

Epidemiology never says "a lot of people are sick" — it says "20 per 1000 per year".

🎬 Story mode — why raw counts lie

Scare Floor A reports 10 sick monsters; Scare Floor F reports 30. Floor F is worse, right? Not if Floor A has 50 workers (20%) and Floor F has 600 (5%)! Roz insists on rates — numbers divided by the population at risk — before anyone panics.

Tools of measurement: rate, ratio, proportion

📈 Rate

Measures the occurrence of an event in a population during a given time.

Rate = (No. of events in a period ÷ population at risk) × 10ⁿ

Has time, numerator included in denominator. E.g. death rate 7 per 1000 per year.

⚖️ Ratio

Relation in size between two independent quantities: x : y.

Ratio = a / b

Numerator NOT part of denominator. E.g. sex ratio (females per 1000 males), doctor–population ratio.

🥧 Proportion

A ratio in which the numerator is included in the denominator; usually a %.

Proportion = a / (a + b) × 100

No time element. E.g. % of patients who are diabetic.

Rates may be crude (whole population), specific (by age, sex, cause) or standardised (adjusted for age so populations can be compared — direct and indirect standardisation).

What epidemiologists measure (Park)

Mortality · morbidity · disability · natality · presence or absence of attributes (e.g. blood group) · distribution of determinants (e.g. smoking) · medical needs · health-care facilities, utilisation and cost.

Common mortality indicators

MeasureFormulaWorked example
Crude death rate (CDR)Deaths in the year ÷ mid-year population × 1000350 deaths, population 50,000 → 7 per 1000
Specific death rateDeaths in a specific group (age/sex/cause) ÷ mid-year population of that group × 1000Deaths from TB per 100,000 population

⚠️ Common mistake

Calling the sex ratio a "rate", or forgetting the time period and the multiplier (per 1000, per 100,000). A rate without "per … per year" loses marks.

4 Measures of Disease Frequency

Incidence = NEW cases (the tap). Prevalence = ALL existing cases (the water in the tank).

🎬 Story mode — the scream-canister tank

Think of Monsters, Inc.'s giant scream tank. New screams pour in from the top — that's incidence (new cases). Some energy drains out at the bottom — monsters recover or, sadly, die. The level of liquid in the tank at any moment is prevalence (all current cases). Pour faster or drain slower and the level rises. That's why a long-lasting disease (slow drain) has high prevalence even with low incidence.

📘 Definitions

Incidence: the number of new cases of a disease occurring in a defined population at risk during a specified period of time.

Prevalence: the number of all current cases (old + new) of a disease existing in a defined population at a given point of time (point prevalence) or over a period (period prevalence).

Measures with formulae and worked examples

🍛 Food-specific attack rates find the culprit

Of the 200 wedding guests, 120 ate chicken biryani and 54 fell ill (attack rate 45%); of 80 who didn't, only 6 fell ill (7.5%). The big difference points to the biryani — and the ratio 45/7.5 = 6 is already a relative risk (next topic).

Measures of disease frequency (Nov 2022 2-marker): incidence (cumulative incidence, incidence density), attack rate, secondary attack rate, prevalence (point and period); plus morbidity and mortality rates (CFR, specific death rates).

5 Measures of Association

How strongly is the exposure (the sock) linked to the disease? Everything comes from one 2×2 table.

🎬 Story mode — does the sock really matter?

The CDA suspects that touching a child's item makes monsters sick. They compare the risk among monsters who touched a sock with the risk among those who didn't. If the touched group gets sick 10 times as often, the association is strong. (Spoiler: in the film the "toxic children" idea was false — which is exactly why you measure association instead of assuming it!)

📘 Definition

Measures of association are statistical measures that quantify the strength and direction of the relationship between an exposure (risk factor) and an outcome (disease). Main measures: relative risk (RR), odds ratio (OR), attributable risk (AR) and its percentage, population attributable risk (PAR), and in trials ARR, RRR, NNT; for continuous variables, correlation and regression coefficients.

The 2×2 table

Disease present (cases)Disease absent (controls / healthy)Total
Exposedaba + b
Not exposedcdc + d
Totala + cb + dN
Relative Risk = [a/(a+b)] ÷ [c/(c+d)] = Iₑ ÷ Iᵤ cohort studies · incidence in exposed ÷ incidence in unexposed
Odds Ratio = (a/c) ÷ (b/d) = ad / bc case-control studies · "cross-product ratio"
Attributable Risk = Iₑ − Iᵤ AR% = (Iₑ − Iᵤ)/Iₑ × 100 · risk difference
Population AR = I(total) − Iᵤ PAR% = PAR ÷ I(total) × 100

Worked example 1 — cohort study (relative risk)

Cohort followed 10 yearsLung cancerNo lung cancerTotalIncidence
Smokersa = 30b = 9701,00030 per 1000
Non-smokersc = 6d = 1,9942,0003 per 1000
  • RR = 30/1000 ÷ 6/2000 = 30 ÷ 3 = 10 → smokers have 10 times the risk.
  • AR = 30 − 3 = 27 per 1000 → 27 cases per 1000 smokers are due to smoking.
  • AR% = 27/30 × 100 = 90% of lung cancer among smokers is attributable to smoking.
  • PAR: total incidence = 36/3000 = 12 per 1000 → PAR = 12 − 3 = 9 per 1000; PAR% = 9/12 = 75% of cases in the whole population could be prevented by removing smoking.

Worked example 2 — case-control study (odds ratio)

NSAID useCases (GI bleed) = 100Controls (no bleed) = 200
Used NSAIDs (exposed)a = 60b = 40
Did not use (unexposed)c = 40d = 160
OR = ad / bc = (60 × 160) / (40 × 40) = 9,600 / 1,600 = 6the odds of NSAID exposure are 6 times higher among GI-bleed cases than among controls

Because the disease is rare in the population, the OR is a good estimate of the RR (the "rare disease assumption"). Incidence and RR cannot be calculated directly from a case-control study.

Worked example 3 — clinical trial (ARR, RRR, NNT)

Stroke in 5 years: placebo group 20% (control event rate, CER); new drug 15% (experimental event rate, EER).

  • RR = 15/20 = 0.75 (protective, < 1)
  • Absolute risk reduction ARR = 20% − 15% = 5%
  • Relative risk reduction RRR = 5/20 = 25%
  • Number needed to treat NNT = 1/ARR = 1/0.05 = 20 patients treated for 5 years to prevent one stroke. (Number needed to harm, NNH = 1/absolute risk increase.)

Interpretation

RR or OR = 1

No association.

RR or OR > 1

Positive association — exposure is a risk factor.

RR or OR < 1

Negative association — exposure is protective.

The 95% confidence interval tells precision: if it includes 1 (e.g. 0.8–2.4) the association is not statistically significant. Strength, consistency, temporality, dose–response and biological plausibility support causation (Hill's criteria).

🧠 Mnemonic

RR = Risk, needs incidence → cohort (follows people forward). OR = Odds, cross-product ad/bc → case-control (looks back). “All Detectives Beat Criminals” = ad over bc.

6 Study Designs in Epidemiology

The direction of the arrow decides the design: snapshot, look back, or follow forward.

🎬 Story mode — Roz's three filing methods

Snapshot: Roz surveys every monster on the floor today — who is sick and who touched a sock right now (cross-sectional). Look back: she pulls the files of monsters already sick and of healthy ones, and checks backwards who handled a sock last week (case-control). Follow forward: she tags monsters who touched a sock and others who didn't, and watches all of them for a month to see who falls ill (cohort). "Always watching" — Roz was born to run a cohort study!

1. Cross-sectional (prevalence) study

Exposure and disease are measured at the same point in time in a defined population — a "snapshot". Gives prevalence. E.g. National Family Health Survey; a survey of hypertension and salt intake among adults in a ward. Merits: quick, cheap, measures burden, generates hypotheses, useful for planning. Demerits: can't tell which came first (temporality); not useful for rare or short diseases; prevalence–incidence bias.

2. Ecological (correlational) study

The unit of observation is a group or population, not individuals — e.g. average salt intake vs stroke mortality across countries. Merits: cheap, uses existing data. Demerit: ecological fallacy — an association seen in groups may not hold for individuals.

3. Case-control (case-reference, retrospective) study

📘 Definition (Jul 2023 2-marker)

A case-control study is an analytical observational study that starts with people who have the disease (cases) and a comparable group without it (controls), and looks back to compare their past exposure to a suspected risk factor. It proceeds from effect to cause; the measure of association is the odds ratio.

🧑‍⚕️Select casesclear diagnostic criteria; hospital or population
🧑‍🤝‍🧑Select controlsfree of disease, from same source population
⚖️Matchingfor age, sex, etc. (confounders)
🔎Measure exposuresame way in both — interview, records
🧮Analyseexposure rates, odds ratio, CI

✅ Advantages

  • Relatively easy, quick and inexpensive
  • Needs comparatively few subjects
  • Ideal for rare diseases and diseases with long latency
  • No attrition (no follow-up); no risk to subjects
  • Several risk factors can be studied for one disease
  • Good for generating and testing hypotheses about unknown causes

❌ Disadvantages

  • Recall bias and selection bias (e.g. Berkson's bias)
  • Incidence and true RR cannot be measured — only OR
  • Temporal sequence may be unclear
  • Selecting an appropriate control group is difficult
  • Not efficient for rare exposures
  • Only one outcome studied at a time
💊 Classic examples

Doll & Hill (1950): lung-cancer patients vs controls — far more smokers among cases. Thalidomide and phocomelia. Diethylstilboestrol (DES) taken by mothers in pregnancy and vaginal adenocarcinoma in their daughters (Herbst, 1971). Pharmacy-style: NSAID use among GI-bleed cases vs controls (OR = 6 above).

4. Cohort (prospective, follow-up, incidence) study

📘 Definition

A cohort study is an analytical observational study in which a group of people free of the disease is classified by exposure (exposed vs unexposed) and followed over time to compare the incidence of disease. It proceeds from cause to effect; measures are incidence, RR and AR. A cohort is a group sharing a common characteristic or experience within a defined time.

Prospective (concurrent)

Exposure measured now; outcome awaited in the future. E.g. Framingham Heart Study (from 1948).

Retrospective (historical)

Exposure and outcome both already in records; investigator goes back to records and follows forward to now.

Combined (ambi-directional)

Retrospective records + further prospective follow-up.

Steps (Park): selection of study subjects → obtaining data on exposure → selection of comparison groups (internal, external, general-population comparison) → follow-up → analysis (incidence in exposed and unexposed, RR, AR).

✅ Advantages (Nov 2022 / Jul 2023)

  • Incidence can be calculated directly
  • Gives direct estimate of risk — RR and AR
  • Several outcomes of one exposure can be studied
  • Clear temporal sequence (exposure precedes disease)
  • Dose–response relationship can be studied
  • Less bias — exposure recorded before disease, so no recall bias
  • Good for rare exposures

❌ Disadvantages

  • Needs a large population and long follow-up
  • Expensive and time-consuming
  • Loss to follow-up (attrition) → bias
  • Not suitable for rare diseases
  • Changes in diagnostic criteria/methods over time
  • Participants may change behaviour (Hawthorne effect); administrative problems

7 Intervention Studies & Controlled Clinical Trials

The gold standard: assign the treatment by chance, hide who got what, and compare.

🎬 Story mode — testing the decontamination spray

The CDA invents a new decontamination spray. If they spray only the monsters they like, any good result could be favouritism. So Roz tosses a coin for each exposed monster: heads = new spray, tails = the old soap (control). Nobody — not the monster, not the doctor checking them — knows who got which (double-blind). A few monsters vanish halfway through, like Mike and Sulley getting banished to the Himalayas (dropouts!) — Roz still counts them in the group they were assigned to (intention-to-treat). That is a randomised controlled trial.

📘 Exam definitions

Intervention (experimental) studies (Dec 2023 2-marker): studies in which the investigator deliberately introduces or withholds an intervention (drug, vaccine, procedure, health education) in a group of people under controlled conditions and measures the effect, comparing with a control group.

Randomised controlled trial (RCT): an intervention study in which eligible subjects are allocated by chance (randomisation) to a study group receiving the intervention and a control group receiving placebo or standard treatment, and both are followed up and compared for outcomes.

Types of intervention studies (Park)

1. Randomised controlled trials

Clinical trials — on patients (therapeutic or preventive). Field trials — on healthy people at risk, in the field (e.g. vaccine trials). Community trials — whole communities are the unit (e.g. water fluoridation, health education).

2. Non-randomised (non-experimental) trials

Uncontrolled trials (no comparison group) · natural experiments (nature creates the groups, e.g. smokers vs non-smokers) · before-and-after studies without or with control.

3. By design

Parallel (each group gets one treatment) · Cross-over (each subject gets both, in random order, with a washout) · Factorial (2+ interventions tested together) · Cluster (groups randomised).

Steps of an RCT

Key features

  • Randomisation — simple (random-number table/computer), block, stratified; with allocation concealment. Removes selection bias and balances known and unknown confounders.
  • Blinding (masking) — single, double, triple; removes observer and subject bias.
  • Control group — placebo, active (standard) treatment, or no treatment; concurrent controls preferred over historical controls.
  • Pre-defined end points — primary and secondary; clinical or surrogate end points.

Dropouts (Dec 2023 · Model 2-marker)

Dropouts are participants who were enrolled and randomised but discontinue the study before its completion — due to adverse effects, lack of efficacy, withdrawal of consent, protocol violation, relocation, loss to follow-up or death.

They reduce sample size and power and can cause attrition bias. Handled by intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis (analyse everyone in the group they were randomised to) plus per-protocol and sensitivity analyses; minimised by run-in periods, reminders and close follow-up. Reasons for every dropout must be reported. (See also Unit 4.)

Phases of clinical trials

🧠 Mnemonic — phases

“Safe → Works → Better → Still safe.”

Writing that Phase III is done on healthy volunteers, or that Phase IV is "before approval". Phase III = large patient trials before approval; Phase IV = after marketing.

8 Errors in Epidemiological Studies

Every measured result = truth + random error + systematic error.

🎬 Story mode — the scream-canister gauge

A canister gauge that flickers a little up and down on every reading has random error — take many readings and average them, and it settles near the truth. A gauge that was wrongly calibrated and always reads 10% low has systematic error (bias) — taking more readings won't help; it is wrong in the same direction every time. Waternoose's rigged scream reports? That's deliberate bias!

🎲 Random error (chance)

  • Due to sampling variation and imprecise measurement
  • No fixed direction; affects precision / reliability
  • Reduced by increasing sample size and repeated measurements
  • Measured by standard error, confidence interval, p-value
  • Leads to Type I (α) and Type II (β) errors
VS

🎯 Systematic error (bias)

  • Due to flaws in design, selection or measurement
  • Consistent direction; affects validity / accuracy
  • Not reduced by increasing sample size
  • Prevented by good design: randomisation, blinding, standard methods
  • Types: selection bias, information bias, confounding

Measures of error

Standard error of mean = SD / √n smaller SE → more precise estimate
95% CI = estimate ± 1.96 × SE range likely to contain the true value; narrower = more precise
  • p-value — probability of getting the observed (or a more extreme) result by chance if H₀ were true; p < 0.05 is usually "significant".
  • Type I error (α) — rejecting a true null hypothesis (false positive), usually set at 5%. Type II error (β) — failing to reject a false null hypothesis (false negative), usually 10–20%. Power = 1 − β.
  • Sampling error (because only a sample is studied) vs non-sampling errors (non-response, measurement, recording, processing errors).
  • Measurement error — instrument, observer or subject variation; checked by validity and reliability (next topic).
⭐ Exam tip

Nov 2022 "measures of error and bias": first half = random vs systematic error + SE, CI, p-value, Type I/II; second half = bias types table from topic 10 + ways to reduce bias. Use the vs-block as your diagram.

9 Validity and Reliability

Valid = hits the bull's-eye. Reliable = hits the same spot every time.

🎬 Story mode — Mike's scare-o-meter darts

Mike Wazowski practises with a dartboard. If all his darts land in a tight bunch in the bull's-eye, his throwing is valid and reliable. Tight bunch in the corner? Reliable but not valid — consistently wrong. Darts scattered all around the bull's-eye? Valid on average but not reliable. Scattered and off-target? Neither — and Roz would not be impressed.

📘 Definitions (Jul 2023 2-marker)

Validity of an instrument/test is the extent to which it measures what it is intended to measure (accuracy).

Reliability is the extent to which it gives consistent, reproducible results when repeated under the same conditions (precision, consistency). A valid instrument must be reliable, but a reliable instrument need not be valid.

Types of validity

  • Internal validity — results of the study are true for the subjects studied (free of bias and confounding).
  • External validity (generalisability) — results apply to other populations and settings.
  • Face validity — on the face of it, the tool appears to measure the concept.
  • Content validity — covers all aspects of the concept (judged by experts).
  • Criterion validity — agrees with a gold standard: concurrent (same time) or predictive (future outcome).
  • Construct validity — behaves as theory predicts (convergent and discriminant).

Types of reliability

  • Test–retest (stability) — same tool, same subjects, two occasions.
  • Inter-rater (inter-observer) — agreement between different observers (Cohen's kappa).
  • Intra-rater — same observer on repeated occasions.
  • Parallel (alternate) forms — two equivalent versions agree.
  • Internal consistency — items of a questionnaire agree with each other (Cronbach's alpha; ≥ 0.7 usually acceptable; split-half).

10 Bias and Confounding

Bias bends the result; confounding hides a third character in the story.

🎬 Story mode — Randall, the hidden confounder

Everyone at Monsters, Inc. notices that "trouble happens whenever Sulley is near a door". It looks like Sulley causes the trouble. But Randall — the chameleon who can turn invisible against any background — is secretly behind the doors and behind the kidnapping plot. Randall is linked to both the "exposure" and the "outcome", and because he is camouflaged nobody sees him. That is exactly a confounder: a hidden third factor connected to both, making an association look real when it isn't (or hiding one that is).

📘 Definitions

Bias is any systematic error in the design, conduct or analysis of a study that results in a mistaken estimate of an exposure's effect on the risk of disease.

Confounding (Model 2-marker) is the distortion of the association between an exposure and an outcome by a third variable (confounder) that is (1) a risk factor for the outcome, (2) associated with the exposure, and (3) not an intermediate step in the causal pathway between them.

Types of bias

TypeMeaningExample / where seen
A. Selection bias — the groups compared differ systematically because of how subjects were selected or retained

Reducing bias: clear case definitions and selection from the same population; randomisation; blinding of subjects, investigators and analysts; standardised, validated instruments; training of interviewers; high follow-up rates; use of records rather than memory; ITT analysis; trial registration (against publication bias).

Confounding

Classic examples

  • Coffee and myocardial infarction — confounded by smoking.
  • Alcohol and lung cancer — confounded by smoking.
  • Grey hair and heart disease — confounded by age.
  • Confounding by indication (pharmacy): patients on a stronger drug seem to do worse because they were sicker to begin with.

Controlling confounding

At design stage: randomisation (best — balances known and unknown confounders), restriction (e.g. only non-smokers), matching (cases and controls matched for age, sex).

At analysis stage: stratification (analyse smokers and non-smokers separately; Mantel–Haenszel), standardisation (e.g. age-standardised rates), multivariable analysis (regression).

🧠 Mnemonic — control confounding with "R R M + S S M"

Design: Randomise, Restrict, Match. Analysis: Stratify, Standardise, Multivariable. “Randall Runs Mad; Sulley Stops Mischief.”

⚠️ Common mistake

Confusing a confounder with an intermediate (mediator) on the causal path — e.g. in "obesity → high BP → stroke", BP is a mediator, not a confounder. Also, effect modification (the effect truly differs between subgroups) is not confounding — it is a real finding to report, not a bias to remove.

1. Introduction

Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems (Last).

2. Classification of epidemiological studies

  • Observational studies — (a) Descriptive: case report, case series, cross-sectional survey; describe disease by time, place and person. (b) Analytical: ecological, cross-sectional, case-control and cohort studies.
  • Experimental (intervention) studies — randomised controlled trials (clinical, field and community trials) and non-randomised trials (uncontrolled trials, natural experiments, before-and-after studies).

[Draw Fig 3.2 — classification tree, and Fig 3.4 — direction of inquiry.]

3. Case-control study

An analytical observational study that starts with persons having the disease (cases) and comparable persons without it (controls), and looks back to compare their past exposure to a suspected cause. It proceeds from effect to cause.

Steps: (1) selection of cases with clear diagnostic criteria; (2) selection of controls from the same source population; (3) matching for confounders such as age and sex; (4) measurement of exposure in the same way in both groups; (5) analysis — exposure rates and odds ratio.

Odds ratio = ad/bc from the 2×2 table. E.g. GI bleed cases 100 (60 NSAID users), controls 200 (40 users): OR = (60 × 160)/(40 × 40) = 6.

Advantages: easy, quick, inexpensive; few subjects; suitable for rare diseases and long latency; no attrition; no risk to subjects; many risk factors can be studied. Disadvantages: recall and selection bias; incidence and true RR cannot be measured; temporal sequence unclear; choosing controls is difficult; unsuitable for rare exposures.

Example: Doll and Hill (1950) — smoking and lung cancer; thalidomide and phocomelia.

4. Cohort study

An analytical observational study in which disease-free persons are classified as exposed or unexposed and followed up over time to compare the incidence of disease. It proceeds from cause to effect.

Types: prospective (concurrent), retrospective (historical), combined (ambi-directional).

Steps: selection of study subjects → obtaining data on exposure → selection of comparison groups → follow-up → analysis (incidence in exposed and unexposed, relative risk, attributable risk).

Relative risk = incidence in exposed ÷ incidence in unexposed. E.g. lung cancer 30/1000 in smokers and 3/1000 in non-smokers → RR = 10; AR = 27 per 1000.

Advantages: incidence and RR calculated directly; several outcomes of one exposure studied; clear temporal sequence; dose–response studied; less bias in exposure measurement. Disadvantages: large population and long follow-up; expensive; loss to follow-up; unsuitable for rare diseases; changes in criteria over time; administrative problems.

Example: Framingham Heart Study; British doctors' study of smoking.

5. Comparison and conclusion

Case-control studies are usually done first to test a hypothesis cheaply; cohort studies then confirm it with stronger evidence.

Q. What are intervention studies? Describe the design of a randomised controlled trial and explain the phases of clinical trials. Predicted
Essay · 10 marks · scope line + repeated in question banks · ~25 min
Definition + types (1½ m) → RCT steps with flow diagram (3½ m) → randomisation, blinding, controls, dropouts/ITT (1½ m) → Phases 0–IV table (3 m) → Indian regulation + conclusion (½ m)

1. Intervention studies

Studies in which the investigator deliberately introduces or withholds an intervention (drug, vaccine, procedure) under controlled conditions and measures its effect against a control group. Types: RCTs — clinical trials, field trials, community trials; non-randomised trials — uncontrolled trials, natural experiments, before-and-after studies.

2. Steps of a randomised controlled trial

  1. Drawing up a protocol — aims, objectives, criteria for selection, sample size, procedures, outcome measures; approved by the ethics committee.
  2. Selecting reference and experimental populations — eligible subjects meeting inclusion/exclusion criteria who give informed consent.
  3. Randomisation — subjects allocated by chance (random-number tables, computer, block or stratified randomisation) to study and control groups, so the groups are comparable and selection bias is removed.
  4. Manipulation/intervention — study group receives the new drug; control group receives placebo or standard treatment.
  5. Follow-up — both groups followed for the same period under the same conditions; dropouts recorded.
  6. Assessment of outcome — blinded evaluation; analysis by intention-to-treat; results as RR, ARR, NNT with p-values and CIs.

[Draw Fig 3.6 — RCT flow chart.]

Blinding: single (subject unaware), double (subject and investigator), triple (plus analyst) — removes observer and subject bias. Designs: parallel, cross-over, factorial, cluster. Dropouts are subjects who leave before completion; they are handled by ITT analysis.

A well-designed RCT is the gold standard for proving that a treatment works. In India, trials follow the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules 2019, need ethics committee approval and CDSCO permission, and must be registered in CTRI.

Q. Explain various study designs in epidemiology. / Briefly discuss the different types of epidemiology studies.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS May 2022 · Model paper · ~12 min
Classification tree (1 m) → 3–4 lines each: descriptive, cross-sectional, ecological, case-control, cohort, RCT (3½ m) → direction diagram (½ m)

Epidemiological studies are classified into observational (descriptive and analytical) and experimental studies. [Draw Fig 3.2.]

  1. Descriptive studies — describe disease by time, place and person; include case reports, case series and cross-sectional surveys; generate hypotheses.
  2. Cross-sectional studies — exposure and disease measured at the same time; give prevalence; quick and cheap but temporality unclear.
  3. Ecological studies — groups rather than individuals are compared (e.g. salt intake vs stroke rates across countries); risk of ecological fallacy.
  4. Case-control studies — start from cases and controls and look back for exposure; odds ratio; good for rare diseases; prone to recall bias.
  5. Cohort studies — exposed and unexposed disease-free people followed forward; incidence, RR, AR; strong evidence but costly and long.
  6. Experimental studies — RCTs (clinical, field, community trials) and non-randomised trials; investigator assigns the intervention; strongest evidence of causation.

[Draw Fig 3.4 — direction of inquiry.]

Q. What are measures of association?
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS July 2023 · ~12 min
Definition (½ m) → 2×2 table (½ m) → RR with example (1 m) → OR with example (1 m) → AR, AR%, PAR (1 m) → NNT + interpretation (1 m)

Measures of association quantify the strength and direction of the relationship between an exposure and a disease.

Disease +Disease −
Exposedab
Unexposedcd
  1. Relative risk (RR) = [a/(a+b)] ÷ [c/(c+d)] — ratio of incidence in exposed to unexposed; from cohort studies. E.g. 30/1000 vs 3/1000 → RR = 10.
  2. Odds ratio (OR) = ad/bc — from case-control studies; approximates RR when the disease is rare. E.g. (60 × 160)/(40 × 40) = 6.
  3. Attributable risk (AR) = Iₑ − Iᵤ (risk difference); AR% = (Iₑ − Iᵤ)/Iₑ × 100 — proportion of disease in the exposed due to the exposure (27 per 1000; 90%).
  4. Population attributable risk = I(total) − Iᵤ — disease in the whole population preventable by removing the exposure.
  5. In trials: ARR, RRR and NNT = 1/ARR.
  6. Correlation and regression coefficients for continuous variables.

Interpretation: RR/OR = 1 no association; > 1 risk factor; < 1 protective. A 95% CI that includes 1 means the association is not significant.

Q. Explain the measures of error and bias.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 · ~12 min
Error definition: random vs systematic (1½ m) → measures of random error: SE, CI, p, Type I/II (1 m) → bias definition + types (2 m) → control measures (½ m)

Errors

Random error is due to chance (sampling variation); it has no fixed direction, reduces precision and decreases with a larger sample. Systematic error (bias) has a consistent direction, reduces validity and is not corrected by increasing the sample.

Measures of random error: standard error (SE = SD/√n); 95% confidence interval (estimate ± 1.96 SE); p-value; Type I error (α — false positive) and Type II error (β — false negative); power = 1 − β.

Bias

Bias is any systematic error in design, conduct or analysis that gives a mistaken estimate of an exposure's effect.

  • Selection bias — Berkson's bias, healthy-worker/volunteer bias, non-response bias, loss to follow-up, prevalence–incidence bias.
  • Information bias — recall bias, interviewer/observer bias, misclassification, Hawthorne effect, lead-time bias.
  • Confounding — distortion by a third variable linked to both exposure and outcome.
  • Publication and reporting bias.

Control: randomisation, blinding, matching, standardised validated tools, trained observers, good follow-up, stratified/multivariable analysis.

Q. Explain the measures of disease frequency with formulae and examples. Predicted
Short note · 5 marks · (2-mark version asked Nov 2022) · ~12 min
Incidence types (2 m) → prevalence types (1½ m) → P = I × D with tank diagram (1 m) → incidence vs prevalence (½ m)
  1. Incidence (cumulative) = new cases in a period ÷ population at risk × 1000. E.g. 480/48,000 = 10 per 1000 per year.
  2. Incidence density = new cases ÷ person-time at risk (e.g. 5 per 1000 person-years).
  3. Attack rate = new cases during an epidemic ÷ population at risk × 100 (e.g. 60/200 = 30%).
  4. Secondary attack rate = cases among contacts within the incubation period ÷ susceptible contacts × 100 (e.g. 12/40 = 30%).
  5. Point prevalence = all current cases at a point ÷ population × 1000; period prevalence = all cases during a period ÷ average population × 1000.
  6. P = I × D — prevalence ≈ incidence × mean duration. [Draw Fig 3.3.]

Incidence measures risk (best for aetiology, acute diseases); prevalence measures burden (best for chronic diseases and planning).

Q. Write the measures of disease frequency.
Brief · 2 marks · Nov 2022
  • Incidence — new cases ÷ population at risk in a period (cumulative incidence, incidence density, attack rate, secondary attack rate).
  • Prevalence — all existing cases ÷ population at a point (point prevalence) or over a period (period prevalence).
  • Related: P = I × D; mortality measures such as case fatality rate.
Q. Give the advantages of cohort study.
Brief · 2 marks · Nov 2022
  • Incidence can be calculated; direct estimate of relative risk and attributable risk.
  • Several outcomes of one exposure can be studied.
  • Clear temporal sequence; dose–response can be studied.
  • Less bias in exposure measurement (recorded before disease — no recall bias).
Q. Write the advantages and disadvantages of cohort study.
Brief · 2 marks · July 2023

Advantages: gives incidence, RR and AR; many outcomes studied; temporal sequence clear; less recall bias.

Disadvantages: needs a large sample and long follow-up; expensive; loss to follow-up; unsuitable for rare diseases; diagnostic criteria may change over time.

Q. Write the types of descriptive studies.
Brief · 2 marks · Nov 2022

Descriptive studies describe disease distribution by time, place and person. Types: (1) case report, (2) case series, (3) cross-sectional (prevalence) survey, (4) ecological / correlational study; also surveillance and time-trend studies. They generate hypotheses for analytical studies.

Q. Explain case-control study. Give an example of case-control study.
Brief · 2 marks · July 2023

A case-control study starts with people who have the disease (cases) and comparable people without it (controls), and looks back to compare their past exposure; it proceeds from effect to cause and gives the odds ratio (ad/bc). Quick, cheap, good for rare diseases, but prone to recall bias.

Example: Doll and Hill (1950) compared lung-cancer patients with controls and found far more smokers among the cases.

Q. Define intervention studies.
Brief · 2 marks · Dec 2023

Intervention (experimental) studies are studies in which the investigator deliberately introduces or withholds an intervention (drug, vaccine, procedure, health education) under controlled conditions and measures its effect by comparison with a control group. Types: randomised controlled trials (clinical, field, community trials) and non-randomised trials. E.g. RCT of a new antihypertensive vs placebo.

Q. Odds ratio.
Brief · 2 marks · Dec 2023

The odds ratio is the ratio of the odds of exposure among cases to the odds of exposure among controls; it is the measure of association in case-control studies. OR = ad/bc (a = exposed cases, b = exposed controls, c = unexposed cases, d = unexposed controls). E.g. (60 × 160)/(40 × 40) = 6. OR = 1 no association; > 1 risk factor; < 1 protective. It approximates relative risk when the disease is rare.

Q. Define dropouts. / What are drop outs in clinical research?
Brief · 2 marks · Dec 2023 · Model paper

Dropouts are subjects who were enrolled (randomised) in a clinical study but discontinue participation before its completion — because of adverse effects, lack of efficacy, withdrawal of consent, protocol violation, relocation, loss to follow-up or death. They reduce power and may cause attrition bias; they are handled by intention-to-treat analysis and must be reported with reasons.

Q. Validity and reliability of instrument.
Brief · 2 marks · July 2023

Validity — the extent to which an instrument measures what it is intended to measure (accuracy); types: face, content, criterion (concurrent, predictive), construct.

Reliability — the extent to which it gives consistent results on repeated use (precision); types: test–retest, inter-rater, parallel forms, internal consistency (Cronbach's α ≥ 0.7). A valid tool must be reliable, but a reliable tool may not be valid. [Sketch two dartboards.]

Q. With a suitable example describe the term "Confounding".
Brief · 2 marks · Model paper

Confounding is the distortion of the association between an exposure and an outcome by a third variable that is associated with the exposure, is an independent risk factor for the outcome, and is not on the causal pathway.

Example: coffee drinking appears to cause myocardial infarction, but smoking (more common in coffee drinkers, and a cause of MI) is the confounder. Controlled by randomisation, restriction, matching, stratification or multivariable analysis. [Draw the confounding triangle, Fig 3.9.]

Q. Observational study.
Brief · 2 marks · July 2023 · answered in Unit 2

A study in which the investigator only observes exposures and outcomes as they occur naturally, without assigning any intervention — descriptive (case report, case series, cross-sectional) or analytical (ecological, case-control, cohort). Full answer: Unit 2.

Epidemiology

Distribution + determinants + application to control (Last). Aims (IEA): describe, find causes, plan services. Triad: agent–host–environment. Morris's 7 uses.

Types of studies

Observational: descriptive (case report, series, cross-sectional) + analytical (ecological, cross-sectional, case-control, cohort). Experimental: RCT (clinical, field, community), non-randomised.

Standard measures

Rate (time, at-risk), ratio (a/b), proportion (a/(a+b)). CDR, specific DR, CFR (killing power), PMR, IMR (per 1000 LB), MMR (per 100,000 LB).

Disease frequency

Incidence = new/at risk; incidence density = per person-time; attack rate; SAR = cases among contacts/susceptible contacts. Point & period prevalence. P = I × D.

Association (2×2)

RR = Iₑ/Iᵤ (cohort) · OR = ad/bc (case-control) · AR = Iₑ − Iᵤ · AR% · PAR · NNT = 1/ARR. 1 = none, >1 risk, <1 protective; CI including 1 = not significant.

Case-control

Effect → cause; cases vs controls; matching; OR. ✔ quick, cheap, rare diseases. ✘ recall/selection bias, no incidence. Doll & Hill 1950.

Cohort

Cause → effect; exposed vs unexposed followed; incidence, RR, AR. Prospective/retrospective/ambi. ✔ temporality, many outcomes. ✘ costly, long, attrition. Framingham.

RCT & phases

Protocol → populations → randomise → intervene → follow-up → blinded outcome (ITT). Blinding single/double/triple. Phases 0 microdose · I 20–100 safety · II efficacy/dose · III 300–3,000 confirm · IV PMS.

Dropouts

Enrolled subjects who leave before completion (ADR, no effect, consent withdrawn, lost). Cause attrition bias → ITT analysis.

Errors

Random (chance, precision, ↓ with n; SE, CI, p, α, β) vs systematic = bias (validity; fixed only by design).

Validity & reliability

Validity = measures what it should (face, content, criterion, construct; internal/external). Reliability = consistency (test–retest, inter-rater κ, Cronbach α). Sens = TP/(TP+FN); Spec = TN/(TN+FP).

Bias & confounding

Selection (Berkson, healthy worker, non-response, attrition) · information (recall, observer, misclassification, Hawthorne). Confounder: linked to both, not on pathway (coffee–MI–smoking). Control: randomise, restrict, match; stratify, standardise, multivariable.
