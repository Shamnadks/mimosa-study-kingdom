1 Biostatistics: Definition & Applications

Why does a pharmacist need maths about crowds? Because every drug decision is made on groups of patients.

🎬 Story mode

Chief Bogo drops 500 files about missing mammals on Judy's desk. She can't read every file aloud at the briefing, so she says: "On average, they went missing 3 days ago, most of them near Tundratown." That's describing data. Then she checks 50 random burrows and announces something about all of Zootopia. That's inferring. Both together = statistics. When the data are about living things (patients, rats, microbes, tablets for patients), we call it biostatistics.

📘 Exam definitions (write exactly this)

Statistics (singular sense) is the science of collecting, organising, presenting, analysing and interpreting numerical data to draw valid conclusions and make decisions under uncertainty. Croxton & Cowden define it as “the collection, presentation, analysis and interpretation of numerical data” (four stages of every statistical study). In the plural sense, "statistics" means numerical facts themselves (e.g., vital statistics, sales statistics).

Biostatistics is the branch of statistics that applies statistical methods to biological, medical, pharmaceutical and public-health data: to design studies, collect and summarise data, and draw valid conclusions in spite of natural biological variation.

Two branches

📋 Descriptive statistics

  • Organises and summarises the data you actually have
  • Tables, graphs, mean, median, mode, SD, range
  • No conclusion beyond the data
  • e.g., "Mean age of our 60 patients was 52 years"
VS

🔮 Inferential statistics

  • Uses a sample to draw conclusions about the population
  • Estimation (CI), hypothesis tests, regression
  • Uses probability; always has some uncertainty
  • e.g., "Drug X lowers BP in hypertensives (p = 0.01)"

Characteristics of statistics (as data)

  • Aggregates of facts: a single value (one patient's BP) is not statistics; many values are.
  • Numerically expressed and reasonably accurate.
  • Affected by many causes (BP depends on age, salt, stress, genes…).
  • Collected systematically for a pre-determined purpose.
  • Comparable: placed in relation to each other (groups, time periods).

Applications of biostatistics in pharmacy

🧪

Drug discovery & preclinical

Dose–response curves, LD50/ED50 (probit analysis), QSAR regression, comparing treated vs control animals.
⚖️

Bioavailability / Bioequivalence

AUC, Cmax compared statistically; the 90% confidence interval of the test/reference ratio must lie within 80–125%.
🏥

Clinical trials

Sample size, randomisation, blinding plans, efficacy and safety comparisons (t-test, χ², survival analysis), interim analysis.
💉

Pharmacokinetics

Regression of log C vs time gives elimination rate constant (k) and half-life; population PK modelling.
🧴

Formulation & optimisation

Design of experiments, factorial designs, response-surface methods to find the best formula.
✅

Quality control

Sampling plans, control charts, process validation, weight/content uniformity, %RSD, dissolution profile comparison.
⏳

Stability studies

Regression of assay vs time to estimate shelf life (ICH Q1E evaluation of stability data).
🌍

Epidemiology & public health

Prevalence, incidence, relative risk, odds ratio, vital statistics, planning health programmes.
📣

Pharmacovigilance & economics

Signal detection from ADR reports, drug-utilisation studies, cost-effectiveness, market surveys.

Limitations of statistics

  • Deals with groups, not individuals: "average patient" may not exist.
  • Studies only quantitative facts (qualities must be coded first).
  • Results are true on average and with a probability, never 100% certain.
  • Can be misused (biased samples, wrong tests, selective reporting): "statistics can prove anything" only in untrained hands.

Types of variables (KUHS Nov 2022 short note)

🎭 By role in a study

  • Independent (explanatory / predictor): the presumed cause, manipulated or chosen by the researcher. e.g., dose of drug.
  • Dependent (outcome / response): the effect that is measured. e.g., fall in BP.
  • Confounding: linked to both exposure and outcome, distorting the true relation. e.g., age in a study of smoking and heart disease.
  • Extraneous: any other variable that could affect the outcome; must be controlled.
  • Control variable: kept constant deliberately. e.g., same diet for all rats.
  • Intervening / mediating: lies on the causal path (drug → lowers LDL → fewer heart attacks).

📏 By scale of measurement (NOIR)

  • Nominal: categories only, no order (blood group A/B/AB/O).
  • Ordinal: ordered, but gaps unequal (ADR severity mild < moderate < severe).
  • Interval: equal gaps, no true zero (temperature in °C; 0 °C is not "no heat").
  • Ratio: equal gaps and true zero (weight, height, drug concentration; 20 mg is twice 10 mg).

Nominal and ordinal = qualitative; interval and ratio = quantitative.

A number does not make a variable quantitative! Patient ID numbers or ward numbers are nominal. Pain score 0–10 is usually treated as ordinal. And "dichotomous/binary" (yes/no, dead/alive) is a special nominal variable with only two categories.

💊 Pharmacy example

In a trial of an antidiabetic: independent = drug vs placebo (nominal); dependent = HbA1c (continuous, ratio); confounder = baseline BMI; patient satisfaction on a 5-point Likert scale = ordinal; number of hypoglycaemic episodes = discrete.

3 Descriptive Statistics: Measures of Central Tendency

One number that represents the whole crowd: the "average".

🎬 Story mode: Snow White's hat problem

Snow White wants to knit hats and needs to know the "typical" dwarf height. She lines them up in the cottage. (These heights are made up for practice, not from any film!) She can add them all and share equally (mean), pick the dwarf standing in the exact middle (median), or choose the height that appears most often (mode). Three honest answers, three different ideas of "typical".

📘 Exam definition

A measure of central tendency (an "average") is a single value that represents the centre of a distribution, the value around which the observations tend to cluster. It summarises the whole data set and allows comparison between groups.

1) Arithmetic mean

x̄ = Σx / n Ungrouped data: sum of observations ÷ number of observations

Dwarfs: x̄ = 651 / 7 = 93 cm.

Grouped – direct

x̄ = Σfx / Σf
x = class mid-point, f = frequency.

Assumed-mean (short-cut)

x̄ = A + Σfd / N
d = x − A (A = an assumed mean).

Step-deviation

x̄ = A + (Σfu / N) × h
u = (x − A)/h, h = class width.

Weighted mean x̄w = Σwx / Σw, used when values have unequal importance (e.g., CGPA from credit-weighted grades; average price when different quantities were bought).

2) Geometric mean (GM) and 3) Harmonic mean (HM)

GM = ⁿ√(x₁·x₂·…·xₙ) = antilog(Σlog x / n)
Best for ratios, rates of growth, and values that multiply: antibody titres, bacterial counts, population growth. Example: titres 2, 8, 32 → GM = ∛(2×8×32) = ∛512 = 8.
HM = n / Σ(1/x)
Best for rates and speeds (equal distances, different speeds). Example: an ambulance goes 30 km/h out and 60 km/h back: HM = 2 / (1/30 + 1/60) = 40 km/h, not 45.

For any set of positive values: AM ≥ GM ≥ HM (all equal only when every value is the same).

4) Median

📘 Definition

The median is the middle value of the data arranged in ascending or descending order; it divides the distribution into two equal halves (50% below, 50% above).

  • Odd n: median = value at position (n + 1)/2.
  • Even n: median = average of values at positions n/2 and (n/2) + 1.

Dwarfs sorted: 86, 90, 92, 95, 95, 95, 98 → (7+1)/2 = 4th value = 95 cm.

Now Snow White (150 cm, also invented) joins the line: n = 8 → 86, 90, 92, 95, 95, 95, 98, 150 → median = (95 + 95)/2 = 95 cm (unchanged!) but the mean jumps to 801/8 = 100.1 cm. The median resists outliers; the mean does not.

Median (grouped) = L + [ (N/2 − cf) / f ] × h L = lower boundary of median class · N = Σf · cf = cumulative frequency of the class BEFORE the median class · f = frequency of median class · h = class width

5) Mode

📘 Definition (KUHS Dec 2023 brief)

The mode is the value that occurs most frequently in a data set, the most "fashionable" value. A distribution may be unimodal, bimodal, multimodal, or have no mode.

Dwarfs: 95 occurs three times (Grumpy, Sleepy, Sneezy) → mode = 95 cm.

Mode (grouped) = L + [ (f₁ − f₀) / (2f₁ − f₀ − f₂) ] × h f₁ = frequency of modal class · f₀ = class before · f₂ = class after · L = lower boundary of modal class

Empirical relation (moderately skewed data): Mode ≈ 3 Median − 2 Mean.

4 Measures of Dispersion

The average tells you the centre; dispersion tells you whether you can trust it.

🎬 Story mode

Two dwarf mining teams both have a mean height of 93 cm. Team A: everyone is 92–94 cm. Team B: one is 70 cm and one is 115 cm! Snow White needs one hat size for Team A, but a whole range for Team B. The mean alone hid this; dispersion reveals it.

📘 Exam definition

Dispersion (variability, scatter, spread) is the extent to which individual observations differ from each other or from a central value. A measure of dispersion is a single number describing this spread.

Why measure dispersion? (significance)

  • Tells how reliable/representative the average is (small spread = trustworthy mean).
  • Compares consistency of two groups/batches (which machine fills more uniformly?).
  • Basis for further statistics: SE, confidence intervals, t-test, ANOVA, correlation.
  • Quality control: content/weight uniformity limits, %RSD in analytical validation, control charts.
  • Sets normal reference ranges (mean ± 2 SD covers about 95% of healthy people).
  • Describes biological variation in drug response.

Absolute measures

Same unit as data (cm, mg, mmHg)

  • Range
  • Quartile deviation (IQR/2)
  • Mean deviation
  • Standard deviation, variance
VS

Relative measures

Unit-free ratios, to compare different units/means

  • Coefficient of range
  • Coefficient of QD
  • Coefficient of MD
  • Coefficient of variation (CV)

1. Range

Range = H − L
98 − 86 = 12 cm. Coefficient of range = (H − L)/(H + L) = 12/184 = 0.065.
Simplest, but uses only 2 values and is wrecked by outliers. Used in QC charts (R-chart) and weather reports.

2. Interquartile range & quartile deviation

IQR = Q₃ − Q₁ ; QD = (Q₃ − Q₁)/2
Q₁ = (n+1)/4 = 2nd value = 90; Q₃ = 3(n+1)/4 = 6th value = 95 → IQR = 5 cm, QD = 2.5 cm.
Covers the middle 50%; not affected by extremes; good for skewed data (pairs with median).

3. Mean deviation (MD)

MD = Σ|x − x̄| / n
22 / 7 = 3.14 cm.
Uses all values but ignores signs (mathematically awkward).

4. Variance & standard deviation

s² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1) ; s = √s²
s² = 96 / 6 = 16 cm²; s = √16 = 4 cm.
Population formula divides by N: σ² = 96/7 = 13.71. Short-cut: s² = [Σx² − (Σx)²/n]/(n − 1) = (60639 − 60543)/6 = 16 ✔

5. Coefficient of variation

CV = (SD / x̄) × 100
(4 / 93) × 100 = 4.3%.
Unit-free; compares variability of different variables (height vs weight) or batches. In analytical chemistry it is called %RSD.

6. Standard error of mean

SEM = SD / √n
4 / √7 = 4 / 2.646 = 1.51 cm.
Not spread of individuals but precision of the mean; used for confidence intervals and tests.
📘 Standard deviation (definition to write)

Standard deviation is the positive square root of the mean of squared deviations of observations from their arithmetic mean (Karl Pearson). It is the most widely used, most reliable absolute measure of dispersion because it uses every observation and can be treated algebraically. Variance is the square of the SD.

Grouped data (step-deviation): SD = h × √[ Σfu²/N − (Σfu/N)² ] u = (x − A)/h · see the solved BP example in Model Answers

SD + normal curve = the 68–95–99.7 rule

💊 Pharmacy example

Two tablet presses both give a mean weight of 500 mg. Press A: SD 2 mg (CV 0.4%); Press B: SD 15 mg (CV 3%). Press B will fail weight-variation tests more often, even though the average is perfect. In HPLC method validation, repeatability is reported as %RSD (= CV).

⚠️ Common mistake

Σ(x − x̄) is always zero, which is why we square deviations (variance/SD) or take absolute values (MD). Also remember: variance has squared units (cm²), SD has original units (cm).

5 Inferential Statistics: Areas & Models

From a handful of witnesses to a verdict about the whole city.

🎬 Story mode

Judy can't interview all of Zootopia's mammals about the night-howler attacks. She randomly picks 100, finds 18 saw something strange, and tells Chief Bogo: "About 18% of the city saw it, give or take 8%." She went from a sample statistic to a population estimate with a margin of error. That jump, made safe by probability, is inference.

📘 Exam definition

Inferential statistics is the branch of statistics that uses data from a sample to draw conclusions, make estimates, test hypotheses and make predictions about the population from which the sample was drawn, with the uncertainty expressed in terms of probability.

Different areas of inferential statistics

🎯

1. Estimation of parameters

  • Point estimate: a single best guess (x̄ estimates μ; sample proportion p estimates π).
  • Interval estimate (confidence interval): a range likely to contain the parameter. 95% CI = x̄ ± 1.96 × SEM (large n) or x̄ ± t × SEM (small n).
  • Dwarfs: 93 ± 2.447 × 1.51 = 93 ± 3.7 → 89.3 to 96.7 cm (t for df 6 = 2.447).
⚖️

2. Hypothesis testing (tests of significance)

  • Decides whether an observed difference/association is real or due to chance.
  • H₀ vs H₁, level of significance, p-value, Type I/II errors.
  • z, t, χ², F (ANOVA), non-parametric tests → Unit 5.
📈

3. Relationship & prediction

  • Correlation: strength/direction of association (r).
  • Regression: predicting Y from X (linear, multiple, logistic).
  • e.g., predicting shelf life, PK parameters.
🧩

4. Comparison of groups & variance analysis

  • Comparing 2 groups (t-test) or 3+ groups (ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis).
  • Underpinned by probability and sampling distributions (normal, t, χ², F) and the central limit theorem.
📘 Central limit theorem (the engine of inference)

If repeated random samples of size n are drawn from any population with mean μ and SD σ, the distribution of the sample means becomes approximately normal as n increases (usually n ≥ 30), with mean μ and standard error σ/√n. This is why we can use z and t tests even when individual values are not perfectly normal.

6 Probability (the language of chance)

KUHS asked "Define probability" (May 2022). The brainkart banks love "probability and its significance".

🎬 Story mode

Snow White writes the seven dwarf names on slips and draws one for dish-washing duty. Chance that Grumpy gets it? 1 out of 7. Chance it's a dwarf whose name ends in "y" (Grumpy, Happy, Sleepy, Sneezy, Dopey)? 5/7. That's probability!

📘 Exam definition (classical, Laplace)

Probability of an event is the ratio of the number of outcomes favourable to the event to the total number of equally likely, mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes. It is a number between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain).

P(A) = favourable outcomes / total outcomes ; 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1

Classical (a priori)

Outcomes known and equally likely in advance (coin, die, lottery).

Empirical (relative frequency)

P = f/n from long-run observation. e.g., if 20 of 1000 patients got a rash, P(rash) ≈ 0.02.

Subjective / axiomatic

Personal degree of belief (subjective); Kolmogorov's axioms give the mathematical rules.

Key terms & rules

  • Experiment / trial: an act with uncertain outcome. Sample space: set of all possible outcomes. Event: a subset of outcomes.
  • Mutually exclusive: cannot happen together. Independent: one does not affect the other. Complementary: P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • Addition rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B) (just add if mutually exclusive).
  • Multiplication rule: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) for independent events; otherwise P(A) × P(B|A).

Example: if the chance of an ADR is 0.02 per patient, P(no ADR) = 0.98; chance that two unrelated patients both get it = 0.02 × 0.02 = 0.0004.

7 Correlation & Regression (scope topics)

Do two variables move together? Can one predict the other?

🎬 Story mode

Finnick notices: the hotter the day in Sahara Square, the more jumbo-pops he sells. That's correlation (they move together). Nick then draws a line to predict "at 40 °C we'll sell 120 pops": that's regression. But careful: pops don't cause heat!

📘 Definitions

Correlation measures the degree and direction of the linear relationship between two quantitative variables; it is expressed by the correlation coefficient r (−1 to +1).

Regression is a statistical method of estimating (predicting) the value of a dependent variable (Y) from a known independent variable (X) using the line of best fit, Y = a + bX, found by the method of least squares.

Types of correlation

  • By direction: positive (dose ↑ effect ↑), negative (age ↑ creatinine clearance ↓), zero.
  • By form: linear vs non-linear (curvilinear).
  • By number of variables: simple (2), multiple (3+ together), partial (2, with others held constant).

Measuring correlation

  • Scatter diagram (visual).
  • Karl Pearson's r (parametric, interval/ratio, normal data):
    r = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / √[Σ(x − x̄)² · Σ(y − ȳ)²]
  • Spearman's rank ρ (non-parametric, ordinal/skewed): ρ = 1 − 6Σd² / [n(n² − 1)]
  • r² = coefficient of determination: proportion of variation in Y explained by X.

🎲 Probability (random) sampling

Every unit has a known, non-zero chance of selection; selection by chance, not by choice.

  • Representative, unbiased
  • Sampling error can be calculated
  • Results can be generalised
  • Needs a sampling frame; costlier
VS

🙋 Non-probability sampling

Chance of selection is unknown; units chosen by convenience or judgement.

  • Quick, cheap, no frame needed
  • Risk of selection bias
  • Sampling error cannot be estimated
  • Good for pilot, qualitative, rare-disease studies

A. Probability sampling methods

1. Simple random sampling (SRS)

Every unit has an equal and independent chance of selection.

Methods: lottery method; random number tables; computer-generated random numbers. With or without replacement.

Steps: define population → prepare frame and number units 1…N → decide n → draw n random numbers → select matching units.

✅ Simple, unbiased, basis of all theory. ❌ Needs complete frame; may miss small subgroups; costly if scattered.

2. Systematic random sampling

Select every k-th unit after a random start. k = N/n (sampling interval).

e.g., 1000 prescriptions, need 100 → k = 10; random start 7 → 7, 17, 27, 37 …

✅ Easy, quick, spreads evenly over the list. ❌ Periodicity bias if the list has a hidden cycle (e.g., every 10th bed is near a window).

3. Stratified random sampling

Divide a heterogeneous population into homogeneous strata (age, sex, ward, socio-economic class), then draw a random sample from each stratum.

Proportionate (n per stratum ∝ stratum size) or disproportionate.

✅ Every subgroup represented; more precise. ❌ Needs information to form strata; more complex.

4. Cluster sampling

Population divided into natural groups (clusters: villages, schools, wards); clusters selected randomly and all units in chosen clusters studied.

e.g., the classic WHO immunisation coverage survey uses 30 clusters × 7 children.

✅ Cheap and practical for scattered populations; no full frame of individuals needed. ❌ Higher sampling error (people within a cluster are alike).

5. Multistage sampling

Sampling in successive stages: state → districts → blocks → villages → households. Random selection at each stage.

✅ Used in large national health surveys; flexible. ❌ Error adds up at each stage.

6. Multiphase sampling

Part of the information is collected from the whole sample, and further information from a sub-sample.

e.g., all 1000 subjects screened by questionnaire for TB symptoms; only symptomatic ones get sputum test and X-ray.

B. Non-probability sampling methods

1. Convenience (accidental)

Whoever is easily available: patients attending today's OPD, classmates. Fast but least representative.

2. Purposive / judgemental

Researcher hand-picks subjects believed to be typical or information-rich (expert pharmacists for a Delphi survey).

3. Quota

Fixed quotas for subgroups (50 men, 50 women) filled non-randomly. The non-random cousin of stratified sampling.

4. Snowball (chain referral)

Initial subjects refer others: hidden or rare groups (drug users, people with rare diseases).

5. Consecutive

Every eligible patient presenting in a period is enrolled. Common in hospital studies; the best of the non-probability methods.

6. Volunteer (self-selection)

Subjects respond to an advertisement; volunteers may differ from the general population.

Non-probability: "Clawhauser Prefers Quick Snacks" → Convenience, Purposive, Quota, Snowball (+ consecutive, volunteer). 🍩

10 Sample Size

Too few witnesses and Judy misses the culprit. Too many and she wastes a month of carrots.

📘 Exam definition

Sample size (n) is the number of subjects/units included in a study. It is calculated before the study (stated in the protocol) so that the study has adequate power to detect a clinically important effect and adequate precision for its estimates.

Why sample size is important (KUHS Nov 2022 brief)

  • Power: an adequate n gives a good chance of detecting a real difference (avoids Type II error).
  • Precision: larger n → smaller SE → narrower confidence interval.
  • Validity & generalisability of conclusions.
  • Ethics: too small exposes subjects to risk without a useful answer; too large exposes more people than needed and wastes resources.
  • Planning of budget, time and staff; ethics committees and regulators require a justified n.

Estimating a proportion (prevalence survey)

n = Z² p q / d²
p = expected prevalence, q = 1 − p, d = allowable error, Z = 1.96 (95%).

Allowing 10% dropouts: 63 / 0.9 = 70 per group.

Sample size and power (KUHS Jul 2023 brief)

Power = 1 − β = probability of correctly rejecting a false H₀ (finding a difference that truly exists). Increasing n reduces the standard error, so the sampling distributions under H₀ and H₁ overlap less → power increases and the Type II error (β) decreases. Power also rises with a bigger effect size, smaller SD and larger α.

12 Dropouts

Cinderella was at the ball, but she ran away at midnight before the story ended.

🎬 Story mode

Cinderella was properly "enrolled" at the ball (she met every inclusion criterion: invited, dressed, dancing!). But at the stroke of midnight she fled before the final dance: she is a dropout. She even left a glass slipper behind, like the partial data a dropout leaves in a study. The Prince could not just pretend she never came; researchers can't ignore dropouts either.

📘 Exam definition (KUHS Dec 2023 & model paper)

Dropouts are subjects who have been enrolled (randomised) in a study but discontinue participation before its completion, so that the final outcome assessment is not available as per protocol, for any reason: adverse events, lack of efficacy, withdrawal of consent, loss to follow-up, etc.

Related terms

  • Withdrawal: subject withdraws consent, or investigator removes the subject (safety, protocol violation).
  • Lost to follow-up: cannot be contacted any more.
  • Non-compliance: remains in study but doesn't take drug as directed.
  • Screen failure: failed eligibility before enrolment, NOT a dropout.

Reasons for dropout

  • Adverse drug reactions
  • Lack of efficacy / patient feels cured
  • Withdrawal of consent
  • Relocation, travel cost, frequent visits
  • Intercurrent illness, pregnancy, death
  • Protocol violation

Why dropouts matter

  • Reduce effective sample size → loss of power.
  • Cause attrition bias if dropouts differ between groups (e.g., more ADR dropouts in the drug arm).
  • Create missing data and threaten internal validity.

Handling dropouts

At planning

Inflate sample size: nadjusted = n / (1 − dropout rate). (63 → 70 for 10%).

Intention-to-treat (ITT)

Analyse every randomised subject in the group originally assigned ("once randomised, always analysed"). Preserves randomisation; conservative.

Per-protocol (PP)

Analyse only those who completed as per protocol; shows effect under ideal use but can be biased.

Missing data methods

Last observation carried forward (LOCF), multiple imputation, sensitivity analysis.

Report transparently

Number and reasons of dropouts in each arm in a CONSORT flow diagram.

Prevent / minimise

Careful selection, run-in period, simple schedule, reminders, travel reimbursement, good rapport, prompt ADR management, multiple contact details.

1. Introduction & definition

In epidemiology we want to know the frequency and determinants of disease in a population, but examining every person (a census) is rarely possible. Sampling is the process of selecting a representative part (the sample) of a population so that, by studying it, valid conclusions about the whole population can be drawn. The population is the entire group of interest; the sample is the selected part; the sampling frame is the list of all units; the sampling unit is the individual element (person, household, village).

2. Relevance / need for sampling in epidemiology

  • Economy of money, manpower and materials.
  • Speed: quick estimates during outbreaks and for planning programmes.
  • Accuracy: smaller scale allows trained investigators and quality control → fewer non-sampling errors.
  • Feasibility: large, scattered or infinite populations (all diabetics in India) cannot be fully enumerated.
  • Destructive/invasive tests: blood tests, biopsies, QC tests can only be done on samples.
  • Ethics: fewer people exposed to procedures or experimental drugs.
  • Used for: prevalence surveys, immunisation coverage evaluation, case-control and cohort studies, drug-utilisation studies, KAP surveys.

A good sample is representative, adequate in size, unbiased (random), independent and practical. Sampling error (due to chance) reduces as n increases; non-sampling error (bias) does not.

3. Classification

4. Probability sampling methods

(a) Simple random sampling: every unit has an equal and independent chance. Units are numbered and selected by the lottery method, random number tables or computer. e.g., selecting 100 patients from a numbered register of 1000 diabetics. Merits: unbiased, simple theory. Demerits: needs a complete frame; may miss small subgroups.

(b) Systematic sampling: sampling interval k = N/n; first unit chosen randomly between 1 and k, then every k-th unit. e.g., N = 1000, n = 100, k = 10; random start 7 → 7, 17, 27… Merits: easy, evenly spread. Demerit: periodicity bias.

(c) Stratified random sampling: population divided into homogeneous strata (age group, sex, urban/rural), and simple random samples drawn from each stratum, proportionately or disproportionately. e.g., prevalence of anaemia sampled separately among urban and rural women. Merits: all subgroups represented; higher precision.

(d) Cluster sampling: natural groups (villages, schools, wards) are the sampling units; clusters are selected at random and all individuals within them studied. e.g., the WHO 30-cluster × 7-children immunisation coverage survey. Merits: cheap, practical, frame of individuals not needed. Demerit: larger sampling error (design effect).

(e) Multistage sampling: selection in stages: districts → blocks → villages → households. Used in large health surveys.

(f) Multiphase sampling: some information from the whole sample and further information from a sub-sample (e.g., questionnaire screening of all, laboratory test for symptomatic).

5. Non-probability sampling methods

  • Convenience: easily available subjects (today's OPD patients).
  • Purposive/judgement: researcher selects information-rich subjects.
  • Quota: fixed numbers from each category, chosen non-randomly.
  • Snowball: subjects refer others; hidden populations (IV drug users).
  • Consecutive: all eligible patients over a period, common in clinical studies.

These are quick and cheap, but selection bias is likely and the sampling error cannot be calculated, so results are hard to generalise.

Sampling makes epidemiological research economical, quick and often more accurate than a census. Q. What are measures of central tendency? Explain how it is calculated.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS May 2022 · ~12 min · 1.5 pages

Definition

A measure of central tendency is a single value that represents the centre of a set of data, around which the observations cluster. Types: arithmetic mean, median, mode (also geometric and harmonic mean). An ideal average is rigidly defined, easy to calculate, based on all observations, capable of algebraic treatment and not unduly affected by extreme values.

Calculation (example: heights 98, 95, 92, 95, 90, 95, 86 cm)

1. Arithmetic mean = Σx/n = 651/7 = 93 cm. Grouped data: x̄ = Σfx/Σf (x = mid-point), or A + (Σfu/N) × h.

2. Median: arrange in order (86, 90, 92, 95, 95, 95, 98); median = ((n+1)/2)th value = 4th = 95 cm. For even n, average of the two middle values. Grouped: Median = L + [(N/2 − cf)/f] × h.

3. Mode: most frequent value = 95 cm. Grouped: Mode = L + [(f₁ − f₀)/(2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)] × h. Empirical: Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean.

4. GM = ⁿ√(x₁x₂…xₙ) (for ratios, titres); HM = n/Σ(1/x) (for rates). AM ≥ GM ≥ HM.

Choice

Mean for symmetric data; median for skewed data or outliers (e.g., hospital stay); mode for nominal data (commonest blood group or dose). In a symmetric distribution mean = median = mode; in positive skew mean > median > mode (sketch Fig 4.2).

Q. With suitable examples, explain various types of variables.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 · ~12 min
Definition (½) → tree diagram by nature (1½) → by role (1½) → scales NOIR (1) → pharmacy example (½)

Variable: a characteristic that varies from one individual or object to another and can be measured, counted or categorised (e.g., age, BP, blood group).

A. According to nature (draw Fig 4.1)

  • Qualitative (categorical): describe a quality. Nominal: categories without order (sex, blood group, dosage form). Ordinal: ordered categories (pain mild/moderate/severe, NYHA class, cancer stage). Dichotomous: two categories (alive/dead).
  • Quantitative (numerical): measured or counted. Discrete: whole numbers (number of tablets, number of seizures per month). Continuous: any value within a range (height, weight, BP, serum drug level).

B. According to role in research

  • Independent (cause/predictor): dose of drug, type of treatment.
  • Dependent (effect/outcome): fall in blood sugar, cure rate.
  • Confounding: related to both exposure and outcome (age, smoking).
  • Extraneous / control: other factors kept constant (diet, temperature).
  • Intervening: on the causal path (statin → ↓LDL → ↓MI).

C. According to scale of measurement

Nominal, Ordinal, Interval (equal intervals, no true zero: °C), Ratio (true zero: weight, concentration).

Example

In a trial of a new antidiabetic drug: treatment group (independent, nominal), HbA1c (dependent, continuous, ratio), age (confounder), number of hypoglycaemic episodes (discrete).

Q. Explain inferential statistics. Write note on different models.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS May 2022 · ~12 min
Definition + diagram (1½) → areas: estimation, hypothesis testing, relationship/prediction (2) → models table (1½)

Inferential statistics is the branch of statistics that draws conclusions about a population from a random sample, using probability to express uncertainty. (Draw Fig 4.4: population → random sample → statistic → inference.) It rests on sampling distributions and the central limit theorem (sample means are approximately normal with SE = σ/√n).

Areas

  1. Estimation: point estimate (x̄ for μ) and interval estimate (95% CI = x̄ ± 1.96 SE).
  2. Hypothesis testing: H₀ vs H₁, level of significance, p-value; z, t, χ², F tests.
  3. Relationship & prediction: correlation (r), regression (Y = a + bX).
  4. Comparison of groups: t-test for two, ANOVA for three or more.

Different models

  • Probability distribution models: normal, binomial, Poisson; t, χ², F.
  • Parametric models: assume normality and use parameters μ, σ (t-test, ANOVA, Pearson r, linear regression).
  • Non-parametric models: distribution-free, based on ranks (Mann–Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis, χ², Spearman).
  • Regression models: simple and multiple linear, logistic (binary outcome; odds ratio), survival models.
  • Frequentist vs Bayesian approaches.

Application: generalising clinical-trial results to all patients, bioequivalence decisions, prevalence estimation from surveys.

Q. What are the different sampling techniques? Describe any ONE technique.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Nov 2022 · ~12 min

Sampling is selecting a representative part of a population to draw conclusions about the whole.

Stratified random sampling (described)

Used when the population is heterogeneous. The population is divided into mutually exclusive, internally homogeneous sub-groups called strata (e.g., age groups, sex, wards, urban/rural), and a simple random sample is drawn from each stratum.

Steps: (1) define population; (2) identify stratifying variable; (3) list units in each stratum; (4) decide total n and allocate to strata, proportionately (ni = n × Ni/N) or disproportionately; (5) draw random samples within each stratum; (6) combine.

Example: to study self-medication among 1000 pharmacy students (400 first-years, 300 second-years, 300 final-years), a 10% proportionate sample takes 40, 30 and 30 students randomly from each year.

Merits: ensures representation of every subgroup; more precise than SRS for the same n; allows separate subgroup analysis. Demerits: requires prior information to stratify; more complex and time-consuming; wrong stratification can introduce bias.

Q. Explain the probability sampling methods.
Short note · 5 marks · KUHS Jul 2023 · ~12 min
Definition + features (1) → 5–6 methods with one example each (3) → Fig 4.6 sketch (1)

Probability sampling is a method in which every unit of the population has a known, non-zero chance of being selected, and selection is by a random process. It gives representative samples and allows calculation of sampling error.

  1. Simple random: equal chance for each unit; lottery, random number table, computer.
  2. Systematic: every k-th unit (k = N/n) after a random start; e.g., every 10th prescription.
  3. Stratified: population divided into homogeneous strata; random sample from each (proportionate/disproportionate).
  4. Cluster: random selection of natural groups (villages, schools) and study of all members; WHO 30 × 7 immunisation survey.
  5. Multistage: random selection in stages (district → village → household).
  6. Multiphase: basic information from all, detailed information from a sub-sample.

Advantages: minimal selection bias, generalisable, error measurable. Disadvantages: need a sampling frame, more time and cost. (Sketch the four-panel dot diagram.)

Brief answers (2 marks each)

Q. Define sample and population.
Brief · 2 marks · May 2022

Population: the entire group of individuals or items possessing the characteristic of interest, about which conclusions are to be drawn (e.g., all hypertensive patients in Kerala). Its numerical characteristics are parameters (μ, σ). Sample: a representative subset of the population actually selected and studied (e.g., 200 hypertensives from 4 hospitals); its values are statistics (x̄, s), used to estimate parameters.

Q. Define probability.
Brief · 2 marks · May 2022

Probability is the numerical measure of the likelihood that an event will occur: the ratio of favourable outcomes to the total number of equally likely outcomes. P(A) = m/n; it ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), and P(not A) = 1 − P(A). e.g., probability of a head on tossing a coin = ½. Empirically, P = relative frequency f/n in a long series of trials. It is the basis of sampling, p-values and confidence intervals.

Q. Define statistics.
Brief · 2 marks · May 2022

Statistics is the science of collection, organisation, presentation, analysis and interpretation of numerical data, to draw valid conclusions and make decisions in the face of uncertainty (Croxton & Cowden: collection, presentation, analysis and interpretation of numerical data). It has two branches: descriptive and inferential. Its application to biological, medical and pharmaceutical data is biostatistics.

Q. Different types of sampling techniques.
Brief · 2 marks · May 2022

Probability sampling: simple random, systematic, stratified random, cluster, multistage, multiphase. Non-probability sampling: convenience, purposive (judgement), quota, snowball, consecutive. Probability methods give every unit a known chance of selection and permit generalisation; non-probability methods are quicker but prone to bias.

Q. Probability sampling.
Brief · 2 marks · Dec 2023

A sampling method in which every unit of the population has a known, non-zero chance of selection and units are chosen by a random process. Types: simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster, multistage. Advantages: representative, free from selection bias, sampling error can be estimated, results generalisable.

Q. Non-probability sampling methods. Enlist four methods.
Brief · 2 marks · Jul 2023

Non-probability sampling selects units by convenience or the researcher's judgement, so the probability of selection is unknown and sampling error cannot be estimated. Four methods: (1) Convenience sampling, (2) Purposive/judgement sampling, (3) Quota sampling, (4) Snowball sampling (also consecutive and volunteer sampling). Used in pilot, qualitative and rare-population studies.

Q. Why sample size is important for the analysis?
Brief · 2 marks · Nov 2022

An adequate sample size (1) gives sufficient power to detect a real difference, avoiding Type II error; (2) gives precise estimates with narrow confidence intervals (SE = SD/√n); (3) makes results valid and generalisable; (4) is ethical and economical: too small wastes subjects' participation, too large exposes extra subjects and resources. It depends on α, power, effect size, variability and expected dropouts.

Q. Write the relationship between sample size and power of the test.
Brief · 2 marks · Jul 2023

Power (1 − β) is the probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis. Sample size and power are directly related: as n increases, the standard error decreases, the H₀ and H₁ distributions overlap less, so power increases and Type II error (β) decreases, until the curve plateaus. Studies are usually planned for 80–90% power; a small sample is "underpowered". Power also depends on effect size, variability and α.

Q. What are the measures of central tendency?
Brief · 2 marks · Nov 2022

Measures of central tendency are single values that represent the centre of a data set around which observations cluster. They are: arithmetic mean (Σx/n), median (middle value of ordered data), mode (most frequent value), plus geometric mean and harmonic mean. In a normal distribution mean = median = mode.

Q. Define mode.
Brief · 2 marks · Dec 2023

Mode is the value that occurs most frequently in a set of observations. e.g., in 86, 90, 92, 95, 95, 95, 98, mode = 95. For grouped data: Mode = L + [(f₁ − f₀)/(2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)] × h. A distribution may be uni-, bi- or multimodal. Mode is useful for nominal data (commonest blood group) and is unaffected by extreme values.

Q. Define dropouts / What are dropouts in clinical research?
Brief · 2 marks · Dec 2023, Model paper

Dropouts are subjects who were enrolled/randomised in a clinical study but discontinued before completion, so their final outcome is not available as per protocol. Reasons: adverse effects, lack of efficacy, withdrawal of consent, loss to follow-up, relocation, death. They reduce power and may cause attrition bias; handled by inflating sample size [n/(1 − d)], intention-to-treat analysis and reporting in a CONSORT diagram.
